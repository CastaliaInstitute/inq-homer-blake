## Revised translation pass

When the Trojans and Hector had crossed the stakes and trench in flight,
and many Danaans had fallen beneath their hands, the survivors waited
beside their chariots, pale with fear. Then Zeus woke upon Ida,
beside golden-throned Hera. He sprang up and saw the Trojans,
the driven Argives, Poseidon among them, and Hector lying in the plain,
breathing with difficulty and vomiting blood after Ajax' blow.
The father of gods and men pitied him, but looked darkly upon Hera:

“You have contrived this, Hera; your helpless deceit has stopped
bright Hector from the battle and frightened his people. I do not know
whether you will be the first to profit from your painful trick,
and whether I must scourge you. Do you not remember when I hung you
from above, with two anvils at your feet and an unbreakable golden chain
around your hands? You hung in air and cloud while the gods on Olympus
could not come near to free you; whoever I seized I hurled from the threshold
until he reached the earth, scarcely breathing. Yet even then
the unceasing pain of Heracles did not leave my heart—Heracles whom,
after persuading the North Wind, you sent across the barren sea
with hostile thought, carrying him to well-built Cos far from his friends.
I rescued him from there and brought him to horse-pasturing Argos,
though he had endured much. I remind you of this so that you will cease
your deceit, and learn what love and bed can help you—love for which
you came apart from the gods and deceived me.”

Ox-eyed, august Hera trembled and answered: “Let Earth and wide Heaven above,
and the descending water of Styx—the greatest and most dreadful oath
among the blessed gods—witness; let your sacred head and our marriage bed
witness, the bed I would never swear falsely by. Poseidon is not harming
the Trojans and Hector for my will; his own heart must have urged him,
and, seeing the Achaeans suffering beside their ships, he pitied them.
I would even counsel him to go where you, dark-clouded one, command.”

The father of gods and men smiled: “If you, Hera, were always like-minded
with me among the immortals, Poseidon would soon turn his purpose
to match yours and mine, however much he now desires another course.
But if you speak truly, go among the gods and call Iris and Apollo
of the famous bow. Let Iris enter the bronze-clad Achaean host
and tell Poseidon to stop the war and return to the company of the gods
or to the bright sea. Let Phoebus rouse Hector to the battle again,
breathe strength into him, make him forget the pains that now weary him,
and turn the Achaeans back in helpless flight, so that they fall
among the many-benched ships of Achilles' son. Hector will raise
his companion Patroclus; bright Hector will kill him before Ilium,
after he has killed many strong young men, including my noble son Sarpedon.
In anger for him divine Achilles will kill Hector; from that hour
I will make the Achaeans drive the Trojans back continually,
until they take steep Ilium through Athena's counsels.

“Before that hour I will not cease my anger, nor permit any other immortal
to defend the Danaans here, until the desire of Peleus' son is completed,
as I promised him and sealed with my head when Thetis clasped my knees
and begged me to honor Achilles, sacker of cities.”

Hera did not disobey. She left Ida for broad Olympus, swift as the thought
of a man who, after traversing much land, turns over in his wise mind
where he has been and where he shall go. She came among the immortals
in Zeus' house. The gods sprang up and offered her cups;
she accepted one from fair-cheeked Themis, who had first run to meet her.

Themis asked: “Hera, why have you come? You look as if you were terrified.
Surely the son of Cronus, your husband, has frightened you.”

Hera replied: “Do not ask me this, Themis; you know how overbearing
and harsh his heart is. Begin the equal feast among the gods;
then all the immortals shall hear what evil deeds Zeus reveals.
I do not think every heart, mortal or divine, will rejoice in them,
even if someone still sits happily at the feast.”

She sat, and the gods were troubled throughout the house. She smiled
with her lips, but her dark brows did not brighten, and she rebuked them:
“Fools—why do we rage against Zeus in our blindness? Do we still desire
to approach him and stop him by word or force? He sits apart and does not care;
he says that among immortal gods he is best in strength and power.
Each of you must endure whatever evil he sends. I expect that Ares
has already met disaster: his dearest son, Ascalaphus,
has fallen in battle, the son whom fierce Ares calls his own.”

Ares struck his thighs with his hands and groaned: “Do not resent me,
Olympians, if I go to the Achaean ships and avenge my son's death,
even if it is my fate to lie among blood and dust, struck by Zeus' thunder.”
He ordered Deimos and Phobos to yoke the horses and put on his shining arms.

Then a greater and more grievous anger would have arisen beside Zeus,
if Athena, fearing for all the gods, had not leapt from the doorway,
left her seat, removed Ares' helmet and shield, and set down his spear.
She rebuked the furious god: “Madman, your mind is destroyed.
You have ears but do not hear, and have lost both understanding and shame.
Did you not hear what white-armed Hera says, returning from Zeus?
Do you wish, after making many evils, to return to Olympus
against your will and plant a great evil for all the others?
Zeus will leave Trojans and Achaeans to themselves and come among us,
seizing each one in turn, guilty or not. Give up the anger for your son;
already another man stronger than he has fallen or will fall.
It is hard to rescue every generation and every child of men.”
She seated furious Ares upon his throne.

Hera called Apollo and Iris, messenger of the immortals, outside the house:
“Zeus commands you both to come to Ida as quickly as possible.
When you reach him and look upon his face, do whatever he commands.”
She returned to her throne, and the two sprang away in flight.

They found wide-seeing Cronus' son seated on Gargaron,
crowned by a fragrant cloud. He did not grow angry when he saw them,
since they had obeyed his dear wife's swift words. He spoke first to Iris:
“Go, swift Iris, and tell Poseidon, lord of the sea, all I command.
Tell him to stop the battle and go among the gods or to the bright sea.
If he does not obey or disregards me, let him consider in heart and spirit
whether, though strong, he will endure my arrival; I say that I am much
stronger than he in strength and older by birth, yet his heart does not care
to call himself my equal, though the others fear it.”

Swift Iris obeyed, flying from Ida's mountains to holy Ilium
like snow or cold hail driven from clouds beneath the North Wind.
She stood near dark-haired Earth-Shaker and delivered Zeus' harsh command:
“Earth-holder, dark-haired one, I bring you a message from aegis-bearing Zeus.
He orders you to stop the battle and go among the gods or to the bright sea.
If you refuse, he threatens to come and fight you face to face,
and tells you to avoid his hands, since he is much stronger and older by birth,
though your heart does not care to call yourself his equal.”

The glorious Earth-Shaker groaned: “Though he is good, he has spoken
too arrogantly, if he will constrain me by force when we are equals.
We are three brothers born by Rhea to Cronus: Zeus, I, and Hades,
lord of the dead below. Everything was divided three ways,
and each received his portion of honor. I received the gray sea forever;
Hades received the misty darkness, and Zeus the broad heaven
among air and cloud; the earth and high Olympus remain common to all.
Therefore I will not live according to Zeus' mind; let him remain
quietly in his third share, though he is strong. Let him not frighten me
like a coward with his hands. Better that he threaten his own children
and daughters with terrible words; they will hear him when he commands.”

Iris answered: “Earth-holder, dark-haired one, shall I carry this harsh
and mighty word to Zeus? Will you change your mind? The minds of the brave
can turn; and you know that the Erinyes always follow the elder.”

Poseidon replied: “Iris, goddess, you have spoken this word rightly:
it is good when a messenger knows what is fitting. But bitter grief
reaches heart and spirit when one equal in honor and allotted fate
chooses to rebuke another with angry words. Yet now, though resentful,
I will yield. I tell you this also, as my heart commands:
if, without me, Athena of the spoil, Hera, Hermes, and lord Hephaestus,
Zeus spares steep Ilium and refuses to destroy it,
giving great victory to the Argives, know that our quarrel then
will be beyond healing.”

So he withdrew from the battle, and the sea received him.
