## Revised translation pass

So they fought, like the body of a flame consuming everything;
and Antilochus, swift-footed, came to Achilles as messenger.
He found him before the beaked ships, pondering in his spirit
the things that had already come to pass; and, troubled, he spoke
within his great heart:

“Ah me! Why are the long-haired Achaeans driven back again
in panic from the plain toward the ships?
May the gods not bring upon my heart the bitter sorrows
my mother once foretold, when she told me
that, while I still lived, the best of the Myrmidons
would leave the light of the sun beneath the hands of Troy.
Surely now the mighty son of Menoetius is dead,
the brave one. Yet I myself commanded him:
having thrust the enemy’s fire away, he was to return
to the ships, and not meet Hector in the strength of war.”

While he turned these things over in his mind and spirit,
the noble son of great-hearted Nestor came near,
shedding hot tears, and spoke the grievous message:

“Son of Peleus, warrior most terrible, you will hear
news bitter and grievous—would that it had never been.
Patroclus lies dead, and the armies fight around the naked body;
but Hector of the shining helmet holds the armor.”

So he spoke; and a black cloud of grief covered Achilles.
With both hands he seized the smoking dust and poured it
over his head, defiling his fair face;
black ash settled upon his nectar-bright tunic.
Then he stretched his great body full length in the dust,
and with his own hands tore and soiled his hair.

The serving-women whom Achilles and Patroclus had taken in war,
grieving deeply, cried aloud around the wise-hearted Achilles;
and each ran out from the tents, striking her breast with her hands,
while beneath them every limb was loosened.
On the other side Antilochus mourned, pouring tears,
and held the hands of Achilles, who groaned in his proud heart;
for he feared that Achilles might cut his own throat with iron.
Achilles uttered a dreadful cry. His honored mother heard him,
sitting in the depths of the sea beside her aged father;
and she cried aloud, while all the goddesses who were Nereids
in the sea’s depth gathered around her.

There were Glauce, Thalia, Cymodoce, Nesaia, Speio, and Thoe;
wide-eyed Halia, Cymothoe, Actaia, and Limnoreia;
Melite, Iaera, Amphithoe, Agave, Doto, Proto,
Pherusa, Dynamene, Dexamenê, Amphinome, and Callianeira;
Doris, Panope, glorious Galatea, Nemertes,
Apseudes, and Callianassa; Clymene was there,
and Ianira, Ianassa, Maira, Oreithyia,
fair-haired Amatheia, and all the other Nereids
who dwell within the sea’s depth.

Their bright cave was filled with them. All struck their breasts
with their hands, and Thetis began the lament:

“Hear me, my sisters, Nereids, that you may all know
what grief lies in my heart.
Ah me, unhappy! Ah me, mother of a son most sorrowful!
I bore a son blameless and strong, beyond other heroes;
he sprang up like a young tree. I raised him like a plant
in the garden, and sent him upon the curved ships
to Ilium, to fight the Trojans; but I shall not receive him again,
returning home into the house of Peleus.
And while he lives and looks upon the light of the sun,
he suffers, and I cannot help him, though I go to him.
Yet I will go, to see my dear child,
and hear what sorrow has reached him while he remains apart
from the battle.”

So speaking, she left the cave; and the tearful goddesses
went with her. Around them the sea’s wave broke apart.
When they came to the rich shore of Troy,
they went up upon the beach in a close line,
where the Myrmidons’ many swift ships stood gathered
around Achilles.

The honored mother stood beside her deeply groaning son.
She cried aloud, seized her child’s head, and spoke to him
in lamenting, winged words:

“Child, why do you weep? What grief has entered your spirit?
Speak it aloud; do not hide it. The things you once prayed for
with hands uplifted—your Achaeans penned against the sterns,
in need of you, suffering deeds they could not bear—
all these have now been accomplished for you by Zeus.”

Swift-footed Achilles answered her with a heavy groan:

“My mother, the Olympian has indeed brought those things to pass.
But what joy is there for me, now that my dear companion is dead,
Patroclus, whom I honored above all my companions,
as I honored my own head? I have lost him;
and Hector, having slain him, stripped away the armor,
great and wondrous to behold, the splendid gifts
which the gods gave Peleus on the day they cast you,
a goddess, into the bed of a mortal man.

Would that you had remained there among the immortal sea-gods,
and Peleus had taken a mortal wife!
But now an immeasurable grief must enter your heart as well,
for your son has perished, and you will not receive him again
returning home; nor does my spirit command me to live
or to remain among men, unless Hector first,
struck beneath my spear, loses his life,
and pays the price for Patroclus, son of Menoetius.

“Let me die at once, since I could not defend my companion
when he was killed. Far from his fatherland he perished,
and he needed me to be his defender against destruction.
Now I shall not return to my dear native land,
nor have I been a light to Patroclus or to my other companions,
whom Hector has laid low—yet I sit useless beside the ships,
a burden upon the earth, such as no other bronze-clad Achaean
is in war; though in the assembly there are others better than I.

“Would that strife might perish from gods and men,
and the anger that makes even the wise grow harsh,
anger sweeter than honey dripping from the mouth,
that swells in a man’s breast like smoke.
So has Agamemnon, lord of men, angered me.
But let the past be past, though we suffer;
let necessity master the heart within our breasts.
Now I will go, that I may find the slayer
of my dear companion, Hector. Then I shall receive my death,
whenever Zeus and the other immortal gods will bring it to pass.
For not even the strength of Heracles escaped death,
though he was dearest to lord Zeus, son of Cronus;
fate subdued him, and the grievous anger of Hera.

“So shall I lie when a like fate has been appointed for me.
But now let me win noble fame, and make many a deep-bosomed
Trojan and Dardanian woman wipe the tears
from her tender cheeks with both hands,
and breathe a long, hard sigh; let them know that I have rested
from war for a long time. Do not hold me back from battle,
though you love me; you will not persuade me.”

The silver-footed goddess Thetis answered him, shedding tears:

“Yes, child, this is true, and it is no wrong
to defend your companions as they meet their steep destruction.
But your beautiful bronze armor is among the Trojans;
Hector of the shining helmet wears it himself
and glories in it upon his shoulders. Yet I do not think
he will glory in it long, for death is near him.
Do not yet enter the labor of Ares,
until you have seen me return here before your eyes.
At dawn I shall come back with the rising sun,
bringing beautiful armor from lord Hephaestus.”

So she spoke, and turned again from her son.
She addressed her sisters of the sea:

“Do you now sink beneath the broad fold of the sea,
to see the old man of the sea and our father’s house,
and tell him everything. I will go to high Olympus,
to Hephaestus, famed for his craft, and ask whether he will give
my son glorious armor, shining in every part.”

She spoke; and they at once sank beneath the sea’s wave.
But silver-footed Thetis, the goddess, went toward Olympus,
to bring glorious armor to her dear son.
