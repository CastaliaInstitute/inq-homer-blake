## Revised translation pass

### Lines 618–654 — Machaon and the old warrior

Nestor and Machaon stepped from the chariot onto the earth and cooled the sweat
from their tunics beside the sea-breeze. In the hut, Hecamede—the daughter of
Arsinoüs, whom Achilles had taken from Tenedos and the Achaeans had awarded
Nestor for his excellence—set before them a polished dark-footed table, a bronze
cup with four ears, gold rivets, and golden doves beside each handle. She mixed
Pramnian wine with goat’s cheese scraped by bronze and white meal, and told them
to drink. Patroclus stood at the door. Nestor rose and welcomed him, but Patroclus
declined the seat: the courteous Eurypylus had sent him to learn whom Nestor had
brought wounded. He already saw Machaon, and would carry the news to Achilles.

### Lines 655–768 — Nestor’s appeal and Pylian memory

Nestor asked why Achilles lamented the Achaeans but did not understand the grief
that filled the army. Diomedes lay wounded, Odysseus wounded by a spear, Agamemnon
wounded, and Eurypylus struck in the thigh by an arrow. The best leaders lay at
the ships. Would Achilles wait until the black ships burned and the Achaeans died?
Nestor remembered the strength of his youth, when he fought the Epeans over stolen
cattle. He killed Itymoneus, took fifty chariots and two men from each, and would
have killed the Moliones had their father Poseidon not hidden them in mist.

He recalled the campaign from Pylos: the cattle, sheep, swine, and mares taken;
the debt owed by the Epeans; the night march; Athena’s summons to battle; his
father Neleus hiding his horses because he thought him untrained; the Minyeian
river; sacrifices to Zeus, Alpheus, Poseidon, and Athena; and the defeat of the
Epeans. Nestor killed Mulius, son-in-law of Augeias and husband of Agamede,
who knew every medicine earth nourishes, then seized fifty chariots. Only the
Moliones escaped when Poseidon covered them. Nestor said that, if his youth and
strength were restored, Achilles alone would gain from the valor of the past;
yet Patroclus should remind him of Menoetius’s charge.

Menoetius had told Patroclus that Achilles was greater by birth and strength,
but that Patroclus was older and must speak wise counsel and guide him. If
Achilles still withheld himself from prophecy or obeyed his mother’s warning,
Patroclus should go forth with the Myrmidons and wear Achilles’ armor; the Trojans
might mistake him for Achilles, and the Achaeans gain a brief breath from battle.

### Lines 769–803 — Patroclus turns to Achilles

Patroclus ran beside the ships toward Achilles. The appeal retains Nestor’s
central distinction: he does not ask Patroclus to become Achilles, but to make
the absent warrior’s likeness serve the exhausted army. The task is an emergency
measure, bound to the armor, the Myrmidons, and the possibility of restoring
breath to the Achaeans before Hector reaches the ships.

### Lines 804–848 — Eurypylus and the wound

When Patroclus reached the ships of Odysseus, where assembly and sacred custom
stood beside the altars, Eurypylus met him limping from battle. The son of Euaemon
had been struck in the thigh by an arrow; sweat ran down his shoulders and head,
and dark blood streamed from the terrible wound, though his mind remained firm.
Patroclus pitied him and asked whether the Achaeans could still hold Hector,
or whether they would already fall beneath his spear.

Eurypylus answered that the Achaeans had no longer any defense: their best men
lay wounded at the ships while Trojan strength continually rose. He begged
Patroclus to save him, draw the arrow from his thigh, wash the black blood with
warm water, and sprinkle gentle medicines—those said to have been taught to
Patroclus by Achilles, who learned them from the just Centaur Chiron. Podalirius
and Machaon were themselves wounded or needed; Eurypylus remained on the field
until Patroclus found him. Patroclus replied that he could not abandon a man
in such suffering, since Nestor had sent him to Achilles. He led Eurypylus to
the hut. A servant spread hides; Patroclus extended the thigh, cut out the sharp
arrow, washed away the dark blood with warm water, and rubbed on a bitter root
that quieted pain. The wound dried, and the blood stopped.
