## Revised translation pass

Achilles rose and went into his hut. From the chest of his beautiful,
carved vessel he took the cup that silver-footed Thetis had placed there,
when she brought it aboard, well filled with shirts, wind-sheltering cloaks,
and soft woven rugs. In that cup no other man drank burning wine,
nor did Achilles pour a libation to any god, except to Father Zeus.

He took it from the chest, cleansed it first with sulfur,
then washed it with pure streams of water; he washed his hands,
and drew the dark wine. Standing in the midst of the enclosure,
he poured it while looking upward toward heaven;
and Zeus, who rejoices in the thunder, did not fail to hear him:

“Lord Zeus of Dodona, Pelasgian, dwelling far away,
guardian of stormy Dodona, where the Selloi,
your interpreters, dwell about you, with unwashed feet,
sleeping upon the ground—once you heard my prayer,
honored me, and greatly humbled the Achaean people;
now grant me this desire also.

“I myself shall remain here beside the ships,
but I send my companion to fight among the many Myrmidons.
Send him glory with him, wide-seeing Zeus,
and make his heart courageous, that Hector may know
whether my companion alone understands how to fight,
or whether his hands rage invincibly only when I enter
the tumult of Ares.
But when he has driven the battle and the war-cry
from the ships, let him return unharmed to me,
with all his armor and his companions in close combat.”

So he prayed, and the counselor Zeus heard him.
The father granted one part and denied the other:
he granted that Patroclus should drive the battle from the ships,
but denied that he should return safely from the battle.

When Achilles had poured the offering and prayed to Father Zeus,
he went back into the hut and set the cup within the chest.
Then he came and stood before the hut, still longing in his heart
to see the terrible battle of Trojans and Achaeans.

The men, armed together with great-hearted Patroclus,
marched forth until, bold in their hearts, they sprang upon the Trojans.
At once they poured out like wasps beside the road,
whom children, foolishly delighting in their sport,
continually provoke while they possess their roadside homes;
they make a common evil for many. If some traveler,
walking nearby, disturbs them unwillingly, the wasps,
whose hearts are strong, all fly forward and defend their young.
So the Myrmidons, with that heart and spirit, streamed from the ships,
and an unquenchable cry rose up.

Patroclus called to his companions, lifting his voice far:

“Companions of Peleus' son Achilles, be men, dear friends,
and remember your furious strength, that we may honor Peleus' son,
who is far the best of the Argives beside the ships,
and whose close-fighting companions are the best.
Let wide-ruling Agamemnon, son of Atreus, know his blindness,
that he honored not the best of the Achaeans.”

So he stirred the courage and heart of every man;
they fell together upon the Trojans, and the ships around them
rang terribly beneath the shouting of the Achaeans.

When the Trojans saw the brave son of Menoetius,
himself and his companion shining in their arms,
the heart of every man was shaken and the ranks were moved.
They hoped that swift-footed Peleus' son had cast away his anger
beside the ships, and chosen friendship instead;
each man looked about him for a way to escape steep death.

Patroclus was first to cast his shining spear straight into the midst,
where the greatest crowd was thrown together, beside the stern
of great-hearted Protesilaus' ship. He struck Pyraechmes,
who had led the horse-armored Paeonians from Amydon,
beside the broad-flowing Axius, striking his right shoulder.
Pyraechmes fell backward in the dust with a groan;
his Paeonian companions scattered around him,
for Patroclus had cast fear among them by killing their leader,
who had been foremost in battle.
He drove them from the ships and quenched the burning fire.
The half-burned ship remained there; the Trojans fled
with a heavenly tumult, and the Danaans poured among the hollow ships.
An unrelenting uproar arose.

As when Zeus, who gathers the lightning, moves a thick cloud
from a mountain's high peak, and all the watchtowers,
the ridges, and the valleys appear, while the boundless sky
breaks open from heaven—so the Danaans, having driven
the hostile fire from the ships, breathed for a little;
yet there was no rest from war. The Trojans did not flee
headlong from the black ships before the Achaean fighters,
but still resisted, and yielded the vessels only under compulsion.

Then, when the battle-line broke, man took man.
First the brave son of Menoetius, as Areilycus turned,
struck his thigh with the sharp spear; the bronze passed through,
and the spear shattered the bone. Areilycus fell face downward.
Then warlike Menelaus struck Thoas in the bare chest beside his shield,
and loosed his limbs.

Meges, son of Phyleus, watched Amphiclus rushing upon him,
and struck first at the upper part of his leg,
where the muscle is thickest in a man's body.
The spear-point split the sinews, and darkness covered his eyes.

Of Nestor's sons, Antilochus struck Atymnius with his sharp spear;
the bronze passed through his flank, and he fell before them.
Maris, standing close beside his brother's body,
sprang at Antilochus in anger. But godlike Thrasymedes,
before he could strike, thrust and did not miss his shoulder;
the spear-point severed the upper arm from its muscles,
and struck through to the bone. Maris fell with a crash,
and darkness covered his eyes.
Thus the two excellent companions of Sarpedon,
the spear-fighting sons of Amisodarus, went down to Erebus—
Amisodarus, who had nourished the unconquerable Chimera,
a great evil for many men.

Ajax, son of Oileus, sprang upon Cleobulus alive,
though he was stunned in the confusion, and loosed his strength
by striking his neck with the hilted sword.
The whole sword grew warm with blood;
purple death and the strong portion of fate seized his eyes.

Peneleus and Lycon rushed together. Their spears missed one another,
and each cast his shaft in vain; then again they met with swords.
Lycon struck the boss of Peneleus' horse-haired helmet,
but his sword broke around the shaft. Peneleus cut his neck
beneath the ear; the blade entered all the way,
and only the skin held the head, while the limbs gave way.

Meriones, running on swift feet, caught Acamas
as he was mounting his horses, and struck his right shoulder.
He fell from the chariot, and mist covered his eyes.

Idomeneus struck Erymas in the mouth with merciless bronze.
The bronze spear passed straight beneath the brain,
and shattered the white bones; his teeth were shaken out,
both eyes filled with blood, and blood burst from mouth and nostrils.
Black darkness of death covered him.

These were the Danaan leaders; each took a man.
As wolves fall upon lambs or kids, seizing them from the flocks
that are scattered upon the mountains—
