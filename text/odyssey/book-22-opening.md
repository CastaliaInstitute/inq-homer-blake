## Translation

Odysseus cast away his rags and sprang upon the great threshold, bearing
the bow and quiver. He poured the arrows before his feet: “The great contest
is finished. Now I will try another mark, one no man has yet struck—if Apollo
grants me glory.” Antinous lifted a golden cup to drink. He did not imagine
that, among so many feasters, one man could make death and black fate for them.
Odysseus drew and struck him in the throat. The arrow passed through the neck;
the cup fell, blood rushed from his nostrils, and food and flesh spilled from
the overturned table.

The suitors cried out and searched the walls for shields and spears, but no
weapon stood ready. “You thought I would never return,” Odysseus said.
“You consumed my house, forced yourselves upon the women while I lived,
and feared neither gods nor the judgment of men. Now the boundary of death
is upon you all.”

Eurymachus answered that Antinous had caused everything: he had plotted
Telemachus’s ambush and desired to rule Ithaca, while the rest would repay
whatever they had eaten and drunk, adding twentyfold the value in bronze
and gold. Odysseus rejected the bargain: even if they returned all their
fathers’ wealth and brought more, he would not stop his hands before every
suitor had paid for his outrage. They must fight or flee; none would escape.

Eurymachus urged the suitors to draw their swords, use tables as shields,
and rush together upon the bowman. He seized a sharpened bronze spear,
shouting, but Odysseus’s arrow struck his chest beside the nipple and fixed
it in the liver. His sword fell; he collapsed upon the table, spilling food
and cups. Amphinomus rushed with drawn sword, but Telemachus struck him
from behind with the bronze spear between the shoulders. Telemachus left
the spear in the fallen man and ran for armor, fearing that another Achaean
might wrench it free.

He brought four shields, eight spears, and four helmets for Odysseus,
himself, Eumaeus, and Philoetius, and the two herdsmen armed themselves.
The suitors’ arrows were spent. Odysseus laid aside the bow and quiver,
put on a four-layered shield, a well-made horsehair helmet, and two spears.
At the far end of the hall was a narrow passage to the street, with fitted
boards. He ordered Philoetius to guard it; one man could hold it against all.

Agelaus called for the door to be opened and a cry raised through the town.
Odysseus answered that one good man could keep the passage, and told them
to bring armor from the storeroom, for he and Telemachus had not placed it
elsewhere. Melanthius climbed through a passage to Odysseus’s chamber and
brought twelve shields, twelve spears, and twelve helmets. Odysseus’s knees
gave way when he saw the suitors arm. Telemachus confessed that he had left
the chamber door ajar, and sent Eumaeus to discover whether a woman or
Melanthius had done it.

Eumaeus and Philoetius found Melanthius returning with more armor. They
seized and dragged him into the chamber, bound his hands and feet behind him,
and hung him from a high pillar beneath the beams, leaving him alive in
torment. Then they returned to Odysseus, while the armed men stood at the
threshold and within the house.

Athena came near in the form and voice of Mentor. Odysseus rejoiced and asked
why she stood apart when they were in need. Agelaus warned the suitors that
Mentor must have become the bowman’s ally; perhaps Odysseus had armed him.
He urged Athena, disguised as Mentor, to fight beside them. Athena remained
silent, testing Odysseus, Telemachus, and the loyal servants. Odysseus then
ordered his allies to cast their spears at the suitors. He struck Demoptolemus;
Telemachus struck Euryades; Eumaeus struck Elatus; and Philoetius struck
Peisander. The suitors fell, and the allies drew the weapons from the dead.

The suitors cast back. Athena made their spears fly wide: one struck a pillar,
another the door, another the wall. Amphimedon grazed Telemachus’s wrist;
Ctesippus struck Eumaeus above his shield, but the spear flew over him.
The four allies cast again—Odysseus killed Eurydamas, Telemachus Amphimedon,
Eumaeus Polybus, and Philoetius Ctesippus, saying, “Take this guest-gift in
return for the ox’s foot you once threw at our godlike master.” Telemachus
struck Leiocritus in the belly, and the man fell face-first.

Then Athena raised the deadly aegis from the roof. The suitors’ minds failed;
they fled through the hall like cattle before a gadfly, while the allies struck
them from every side. Agelaus urged escape through the passage and a call
to the town, but Odysseus shot him through the throat. The bodies lay in blood
and dust like fish drawn from the gray sea upon the shore. Odysseus searched
the house for any living enemy; all had fallen.

Telemachus asked what first should be done. Odysseus told him to fetch
Eurycleia while he and the herdsmen cleared the hall. The singer Phemius,
who had sung among the suitors under compulsion, stood by the doors weighing
whether to flee to the great altar or clasp Odysseus’s knees. He chose the king,
laid down his lyre between the mixing bowl and silver-studded chair, and begged
for life, saying that a god had planted every song in him and that the suitors
had forced him to serve. Telemachus testified that Phemius was not guilty.

Medon, the herald who had cared for Telemachus as a child, lay crouched beneath
a fresh ox-hide. Telemachus asked that he too be spared. Odysseus agreed,
and the herald and singer sat beside Zeus’s altar, watching for death.

Odysseus looked over his house: the suitors lay like fish scattered upon sand.
He told Telemachus, the cowherd, and the swineherd to clean the beautiful
chairs and tables with water and sponges, scrape the floor, and then bring
the disloyal women into the narrow space between the dome and the courtyard
fence. There, he said, they should strike them with long swords until they
forgot the secret beds they had shared with the suitors.

The women carried the dead outside, leaning upon one another and weeping;
Odysseus drove them on. They washed the chairs and tables. Telemachus,
Eumaeus, and Philoetius scraped the floor, and the women bore the refuse out.
When the house was ordered, the women were enclosed in the narrow place.
Telemachus said that they had poured shame upon him and his mother and slept
beside the suitors. He tied a rope from a great pillar around the dome,
raised it high so no foot could reach the ground, and the women hung in a row,
their necks in nooses, their feet twitching briefly.

Melanthius was dragged down. They cut away his nose and ears, severed his
hands and feet, and divided them for the dogs. Then Odysseus asked Eurycleia
for sulfur and fire, so that he might cleanse the hall, house, and courtyard,
and told her to summon Penelope and the women. The nurse said he should first
put on cloak and tunic, lest he stand in the hall covered only in rags.

He answered that fire must come first. Eurycleia brought it and the sulfur;
Odysseus purified the house and courtyard. Then she went through the rooms,
carrying the light and urging the women to come. They came out, embraced
Odysseus, kissed his head, shoulders, and hands, and awakened in him the sweet
desire for weeping and lamentation: he knew every one of them in his heart.

## Decision log

- The collation keeps Antinous’s cup and throat wound, Eurymachus’s rejected
  settlement, the named first-round deaths, and the exact armory sequence.
- Melanthius’s two trips, his binding and hanging, Athena’s Mentor disguise,
  the aegis, and the fish-and-gadfly battle similes remain distinct.
- Phemius and Medon are spared through Telemachus’s testimony; the punishment
  of the disloyal women and Melanthius is retained without moving Book 23’s
  Penelope scene into this book.
- The register is ceremonial and blank-verse-oriented, a Longfellow-inspired
  working draft rather than a claim of exact imitation.
- This draft requires independent Greek-fidelity review, literary review,
  meter review, notes review, and production sign-off before promotion beyond
  `draft`.
