## Revised translation pass

When the Achaeans' curved ships had come, Melanippus returned to Ilium;
he stood among the Trojans, and beside Priam made his dwelling,
who honored him as he would honor his own sons. Hector rebuked him,
and called him by his name:

“Will you then, Melanippus, let us rest? Have you no shame within you
for the blood of your cousin, who has fallen? Do you not see
how the Achaeans bear away the armor of Dolops? Come with me;
there is no longer room to fight from far off with the Argives:
we must either slay them, or take steep Ilium and its people.”

He spoke, and led the way; Melanippus, a man like the gods, followed.
Great Telamonian Ajax urged the Argives:

“Be men, dear friends, and set shame within your hearts;
respect one another in the hard encounter. When men are mindful,
more are saved than slain; but from the flight of cowards neither glory
arises, nor any strength.”

He spoke; and they themselves desired to ward the danger from them,
and in their hearts took his word, and fenced the ships with bronze.
Zeus roused the Trojans. Menelaus called to Antilochus:

“Antilochus, no Achaean is younger than you, nor swifter of foot,
nor more courageous when you fight. Leap out and strike some Trojan.”

So he spoke, and sprang again among the foremost; he looked about him,
and cast his shining spear. The Trojans shrank before the throw;
his spear was not sent vainly, but struck Melanippus, son of Hicetaon,
as he came back from battle, beneath the breast beside the nipple.
He fell with a crash, and darkness covered both his eyes.

Antilochus leaped upon him like a hound that springs upon a wounded fawn,
when the hunter has struck it as it leaves its lair and loosened its limbs;
so upon you, Melanippus, rejoicing in the fight, Antilochus sprang,
to strip your armor. Yet noble Hector did not fail to see him;
he came running through the battle to meet him.

Antilochus, swift though he was and practiced in war, did not wait;
he fled like the beast that has done a deadly thing,
and flees before the gathering crowd of men, though it has killed a dog
or a herdsman among the cattle. So fled the son of Nestor;
and behind him Trojans and Hector poured their groaning missiles,
until he turned and stood within the ranks of his companions.

Like flesh-eating lions the Trojans rushed upon the ships,
fulfilling the commands of Zeus, who always awakened their great courage,
bewitched the hearts of the Argives, and took their glory from them,
while he roused the Trojans. For his heart desired to grant the glory
to Hector, Priam's son, that he might cast the unwearying fire of heaven
upon the curved ships, and make complete the dreadful prayer of Thetis;
for Zeus, the counselor, longed to see with his own eyes
the brightness of a burning vessel. From that sight he would set the Trojans
back in flight from the ships, and grant the Danaans their glory again.

With this purpose he roused Hector beside the hollow ships,
though Hector himself was eager enough. He raged like Ares of the spear,
or like destructive fire that rages in the deep forest on the mountains;
foam gathered about his mouth, his eyes flashed beneath the terrible brows,
and his helmet shook dreadfully above the temples as he fought.
For Zeus himself was his defender from the upper air:
he honored and glorified Hector, alone among so many men,
though his day would be brief, and Pallas Athena already raised
the destined hour that should end him beneath the might of Peleus' son.

Hector tried to break the ranks where he saw the greatest multitude
and the finest armor; yet even in his fury he could not break them.
They stood joined like a tower, like a vast rock beside the gray sea,
which waits unmoved for the swift roads of shrieking winds
and for the great waves that roar against it. So the Danaans stood,
and held the Trojans fast, nor gave way.

But Hector, shining on every side with fire, leaped into the host,
and fell among them as a wave falls upon a swift ship,
a storm-wave raised beneath the clouds by the wind;
the whole ship is hidden under foam, the terrible wind roars in the sail,
and the sailors tremble in their hearts, for they are carried
but a little distance from death. So the hearts of the Achaeans
were broken within their breasts.

As a savage lion comes upon cattle grazing in a wide marsh,
thousands together, while the herdsman has not yet learned
how to fight the beast for the death of a horned ox—
he walks always beside the first and last, but springs upon the middle ox,
and all the herd recoils—so then the Achaeans fled in terror
before Hector and father Zeus. Hector alone killed Periphetes,
the Mycenaean, beloved son of Copreus, who had gone as herald
in the might of Heracles to Eurystheus, a king.

His son was far better than his father in every kind of excellence,
in his feet, in battle, and in judgment; among the Mycenaeans
he had been formed among the foremost. Yet then he gave Hector
the greater glory. Turning backward, he stumbled against the rim
of the shield he wore, a long defense against the missiles;
his feet were caught, and he fell upon his back, while his helmet
rang terribly around his temples. Hector saw him at once,
ran close, and fixed his spear within his breast,
killing him beside his loving companions. They could not help him,
though they grieved for their friend, so great was their fear of Hector.

The Argives were driven inward; the first ships held them round,
and the Trojans poured upon them. The Achaeans withdrew by necessity
from the ships that stood in the foremost line, but stayed together
beside their huts; they did not scatter through the camp.
Shame held them, and fear; they called continually to one another.
Nestor, most of all, the Gerenian guardian of the Achaeans,
begged each man upon his knees for the sake of his parents:

“Be men, dear friends, and set shame within your hearts.”
