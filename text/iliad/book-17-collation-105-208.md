## Revised translation pass

—if only we might draw the dead away; and the better course would be
to bring him naked to Achilles, son of Peleus.

While he pondered these things in his mind and heart,
the Trojan ranks came on, and Hector led them.
Golden-haired Menelaus drew backward, leaving the corpse,
turning his face again and again, like a long-maned lion
whom dogs and men drive from the fold with spears and shouting;
his brave heart grows cold within him, and unwillingly he leaves
the middle of the yard. So Menelaus went away from Patroclus,
then turned and stood when he reached his companions,
looking everywhere for great Telamonian Ajax.

At once he saw him on the left of all the battle,
giving courage to his companions and urging them to fight;
for Phoebus Apollo had cast a marvelous fear among them.
Menelaus ran to him, stood close, and spoke:

“Ajax, dear friend, let us hurry for dead Patroclus,
if somehow we can bring him naked to Achilles;
but Hector of the shining helmet has the armor.”

He spoke, and stirred the warlike heart of Ajax.
Ajax went through the foremost fighters, and golden-haired Menelaus
went with him.

But Hector, after stripping Patroclus of the famous armor,
dragged him, desiring to cut off his head with sharp bronze,
and give the corpse to the Trojan women and the dogs.
Then Ajax came near, carrying his shield like a tower;
Hector drew back into the crowd of his companions,
leaped into his chariot, and gave the beautiful armor
to the Trojans to carry toward the city, that it might be
a great glory for himself.

Ajax spread his broad shield around Menoetius' son
and stood over the hero like a lion over his cubs,
when men who hunt for their living meet it in the forest
while it leads its little ones; then the lion swells with strength,
and draws the whole brow low over its eyes.
So Ajax stood around the hero Patroclus.
On the other side war-loving Menelaus stood,
enlarging the great sorrow in his breast.

Glaucus, son of Hippolochus, leader of the Lycians,
looked Hector darkly in the face and rebuked him with hard words:

“Hector, your appearance is excellent, but in battle
you have been found greatly wanting. You have only a hollow name,
though you are a man who flees.
Consider now how you may save the city and its people,
you alone with those born in Ilium.
No Lycian will go forth to fight the Danaans around the city;
there is no gratitude in fighting the enemy without ceasing.
How could you save a lesser man among the multitude,
you pitiless one, when you have left Sarpedon,
your guest-friend and companion, to become spoil and a prize
for the Argives—Sarpedon, who was a great help to you
and to the city while he lived, but whom you did not dare
to keep the dogs from now?

“Therefore, if any Lycian will obey me,
let us go home; ruin without remedy has come upon Troy.
If the Trojans had that bold, fearless courage
that enters men who set labor and battle before their fatherland,
we might quickly drag Patroclus into Ilium.
If this dead man came to the great city of Priam,
and we dragged him out of the battle,
the Argives would soon strip the beautiful armor
from Sarpedon, and carry Sarpedon himself into Ilium;
for his companion has fallen—the man who was far the best
of the Argives beside the ships, and whose companions
were the best at close combat.
Yet you did not dare to stand before great Ajax,
look him in the eyes amid the enemy's cry,
or fight straight at him, since he is stronger than you.”

Hector of the shining helmet looked darkly at him and answered:

“Glaucus, why, though you are such a man,
have you spoken this over-bold word?
I thought you were greater in mind than all the men
who dwell in rich Lycia; but now I despise your judgment,
that you say I did not dare to stand against monstrous Ajax.
I do not fear battle, nor the thunder of horses;
but the will of aegis-bearing Zeus is always stronger,
and he easily puts to flight the brave man
and takes victory away, or at another time urges him to fight.

“Come, dear friend, stand beside me and see the deed:
shall I be weak all day, as you say,
or shall I hold back some Danaan, however eager,
from defending Patroclus, who has died?”

So he spoke, and called the Trojans in a loud voice:

“Trojans, Lycians, and close-fighting Dardanians,
be men, dear friends, and remember your furious strength,
until I put upon me the blameless armor of Achilles,
beautiful armor which, when I killed Patroclus,
I stripped from his shoulders.”

So he spoke, and radiant Hector went away from the deadly battle.
He ran swiftly and overtook his companions,
who had not yet gone far, carrying the famous armor of Peleus' son.
Standing apart from the tearful battle, he exchanged the armor:
he gave his own for the Trojans to carry toward sacred Ilium,
and clothed himself in the immortal armor of Peleus' son,
which the heavenly gods had given to his dear father,
and he, growing old, had handed on to his son;
but the son did not grow old in his father's armor.

When cloud-gathering Zeus saw him arming himself
in the armor of godlike Peleus' son, he shook his head
and spoke to his own great heart:

“Poor man, death is not at all in your thoughts,
though it stands near you. You put upon yourself
the immortal armor of the best man,
before whom other men tremble.
You have killed his gentle and strong companion,
and stripped his armor from head and shoulders against all order.
Now I shall grant you great strength;
but Andromache shall not receive the beautiful armor
of Peleus' son, when you return no more from the battle.”
