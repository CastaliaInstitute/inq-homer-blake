## Revised translation pass

### 13.1–65 — Poseidon leaves Samothrace

When Zeus had driven the Trojans and Hector toward the ships,
he left them there to labor and suffer without rest. Then he turned
his shining eyes away, looking toward the land of the Thracian horsemen,
the Mysians who fight at close quarters, the milk-drinking riders,
the Abians, most just among mankind. He did not wholly turn his gaze
toward Troy; he expected no god to come, in secret purpose,
to aid the Trojans or the Danaans.

But the mighty Earth-Shaker was not blind to the war.
He sat in wonder on the highest summit of wooded Samothrace,
and from there Ida, Priam's city, and the Achaean ships
all lay within his sight. Coming up from the sea, he pitied
the Argives crushed beneath the Trojans, and bitterly resented Zeus.

At once he descended the rugged mountain, moving swiftly;
the long ridges and the forest trembled beneath the immortal feet
of Poseidon. Three strides he took, and the fourth brought him
to Aegae, where beneath the deep waters his bright golden halls
were built, imperishable forever. There he yoked beneath his chariot
the bronze-hoofed, swift-flying horses with golden manes.
He clothed his body in gold, took up a golden whip, well wrought,
and mounted his car. He drove across the waves; the sea-creatures
played around him from every hidden place and knew their lord.
The sea opened gladly, and the bronze axle stayed dry beneath him;
his eager horses bore him swiftly to the Achaean ships.

There is a broad cave in the sea's deep hollow, midway between Tenedos
and rugged Imbros. Poseidon halted his horses there, loosed them
from the chariot, and set ambrosial food before them. Around their feet
he laid golden fetters, unbreakable and not to be loosed,
that they might wait there until their lord returned. Then he went
into the Achaean host.

The Trojans followed Hector in a mass, like flame or a storm,
making an unwearied uproar. They hoped to take the ships
and kill beside them all the noblest men. But Poseidon, lord of earth,
came from the deep sea and roused the Argives, taking upon himself
the form and tireless voice of Calchas. First he spoke to the two Ajaxes,
who were eager for the fight:

“Ajaxes, you must save the Achaean people by remembering your strength,
and not by yielding to the chill of fear. Elsewhere I do not fear
the invincible hands of the Trojans who have crossed the great wall;
the well-greaved Achaeans will hold them. But here I fear exceedingly,
where Hector, raging like flame, leads them—Hector who boasts himself
the son of mighty Zeus. May some god put this courage into both your hearts:
to stand firm yourselves and command the others. Then you might drive
even this rushing man away from the swift ships, though Olympus itself
had roused him.”

With his staff the Earth-Shaker struck them both and filled them
with powerful courage, making their limbs light above and below.
Then he sprang away like a hawk, which rises from a cliff's sheer edge
and darts across the plain to chase another bird.

Swift Ajax, son of Oileus, recognized the god's departing feet
and said to Telamonian Ajax: “Ajax, some god who holds Olympus,
wearing the likeness of a prophet, commands us to fight beside the ships.
He is not Calchas the seer: I know the tracks of his feet behind him,
and the gods are easily recognized. My own heart now presses me more
strongly toward battle; my feet beneath me and my hands above
are eager for the work.”

Telamonian Ajax answered: “So too my hands are eager around my spear;
my strength has risen, and my feet beneath me are already moving.
I alone desire to meet Hector, Priam's son, whose hunger for battle
will not cease.” So they spoke, rejoicing in the courage the god had breathed
into their hearts; meanwhile the Earth-Shaker roused the Achaeans behind them,
those who had been cooling their hearts beside the swift ships.

### 13.66–125 — the hidden exhortation

Their limbs were loosened by harsh labor, and grief entered their hearts
when they looked upon the Trojans who had crossed the great wall.
They poured tears from beneath their brows, believing they could not escape
the ruin; but the Earth-Shaker, easily taking another form,
roused the battle-lines. First he went to Teucer, Leitus,
Peneleos, Thoas, Deipyros, Meriones, and Antilochus,
masters of the war-cry, and addressed them with winged words:

“Shame on you, young men of Argos! In your struggle I trusted you
to save our ships. But if you abandon this grievous battle,
today is the day you will be overcome by the Trojans.
What a great wonder I behold—dreadful, a thing I never thought would come:
Trojans upon our ships. Before this they were like frightened deer,
who wander through the forest and become food for panthers, wolves,
and leopards, having no courage for the fight. So the Trojans once
would not stand against the might and hands of the Achaeans,
not even for a little while. Now, far from their city, beside our ships,
they fight because their leaders are bad and their people neglectful;
they would not stand together to defend the vessels,
but are dying there among them.

“Even if Agamemnon, broad-ruling son of Atreus, is wholly to blame,
because he dishonored swift Achilles, it is not permitted us
to abandon the battle. Let us heal it quickly; the minds of brave men
are physicians. And you, best among the army, must no longer relax
your warlike strength. I would not rebuke a man who held back,
weak with battle; but I am angry at you from the heart.
Dear friends, you will soon make a greater evil through this neglect.
Let shame and righteous anger enter each mind, for a mighty conflict
has risen. Hector, skilled in the war-cry, fights beside the ships;
he has broken the gates and the long bar.”

So the Earth-Shaker roused the Achaeans. Around the two Ajaxes
strong ranks stood—ranks that neither Ares nor Athena, driver of hosts,
would have scorned. These men, chosen as the best, waited for the bright Trojans
and Hector, making spear answer spear, shield press shield,
helmet touch helmet, man close against man. The horse-haired crests
brushed one another as the warriors bent together; the spears trembled
from their bold hands, and all looked straight ahead, eager for the fight.

The Trojans struck together, and Hector led them straight on,
like a stone that a winter torrent, swollen by a ruthless rain,
breaks from the mountain's rim and drives high, leaping through the forest,
until it reaches the level ground, where, though rushing, it no longer rolls.
So Hector promised that, until he reached the sea, he would pass
through huts and ships, killing; but when he encountered the close ranks,
he stopped, pressed hard against them. The Achaeans met him
with swords and spears, and drove him back; he staggered away.

Then he cried through the ranks: “Trojans, Lycians, and Dardanians,
fighters at close quarters, stand firm! The Achaeans will not hold me long,
though they have arranged themselves tower-like. I think they will give way
beneath my spear, if Hera's best and loud-thundering husband
has truly roused me.” Thus he stirred each man's strength and heart.

### 13.156–205 — Teucer, Hector, and the first bodies

Deiphobus, son of Priam, strode among them, full of confidence,
holding his rounded shield before him and stepping lightly beneath it.
Meriones aimed his shining spear and struck the bull-hide shield;
but the long shaft broke in the wood before it passed through.
Deiphobus held the shield away and feared Meriones' spear.
The hero withdrew among his companions, enraged at both his lost victory
and his broken weapon, and went beside the huts and ships
to fetch the long spear that lay there.

The others fought on, and the unquenchable cry rose. Teucer was first
to kill Imbrius, son of Mentor, rich in horses. Imbrius had lived at Pedasus
before the Achaeans came, and had taken Priam's bastard daughter,
Medesicasta, to wife. When the Danaans' ships arrived, he returned to Ilium
and stood out among the Trojans; Priam honored him as his own children.
Teucer struck him beneath the ear with his long spear and drew it out;
Imbrius fell like an ash tree seen from afar on a mountain height,
cut by bronze until its tender leaves reach the earth.

Teucer rushed to strip his armor. Hector cast his shining spear at him;
Teucer saw it coming and escaped the bronze point by a little.
But Hector struck Amphimachus, son of Cteatus, in the breast
as he came into battle; the man fell with a crash, and his armor rang.
Hector sprang to seize the helmet fixed upon Amphimachus' temples.
Ajax rushed at Hector with his shining spear, but it did not reach his flesh:
terrible bronze covered him everywhere. Ajax struck the boss of his shield
and drove him back with great force. Hector withdrew from both bodies,
and the Achaeans dragged them away.
