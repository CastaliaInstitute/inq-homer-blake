## Revised translation pass

—seizing them from the flocks that wander without a shepherd's wisdom;
and when the wolves have seen them, they quickly tear apart
the lambs whose hearts have no defense. So the Danaans fell upon the Trojans;
but the Trojans remembered the loud terror of flight
and forgot the furious strength that had been theirs.

Great Ajax still hurled himself against Hector in bronze;
but Hector, knowing the craft of war, covered his broad shoulders
with the hide of a bull, and watched the whistling arrows
and the heavy striking of the spears. He knew well
that victory had turned to the other side, yet he remained,
and saved his steadfast companions.

As when a cloud comes from Olympus into the bright air,
when Zeus stretches out a storm, so from the ships rose
the shouting and the terror; they did not pass back in order.
Swift-footed horses carried Hector away with his armor,
and he left the Trojan people behind, whom the dug trench held unwillingly.
Many swift horses, dragging chariots, broke their yokes in the trench;
they left their lords' chariots there.

Patroclus followed, shouting fiercely and commanding the Danaans,
with evil thoughts for the Trojans. The roads were filled with their cry
and fear when the lines were broken; high beneath the clouds
the whirlwind scattered, and the one-hoofed horses stretched themselves,
running back toward the city, away from the ships and huts.

Wherever he saw the greatest multitude in confusion,
Patroclus drove them with his shout. Beneath the axles men fell
face downward from the chariots, and the chariots overturned.
The deathless horses, beautiful gifts the gods had given Peleus,
leaped straight across the trench; and Hector's heart desired
to cast at Patroclus, but the swift horses carried him away.

As when in an autumn storm the whole black earth grows heavy,
when Zeus pours the most violent rain in anger against men
who in the assembly judge crooked judgments by force,
and drive justice out, caring nothing for the gods' regard—
then all the rivers rise as they run,
and many ravines cut away the hills,
roaring greatly as they flow from the mountains into the purple sea,
and the works of men grow small—so the Trojan horses,
racing onward, groaned beneath their harness.

When Patroclus had cut through the first ranks,
he drove them back toward the ships. He did not permit them
to reach the city, but between the ships, the river, and the high wall
he leaped upon them and killed, exacting the price of many.

There first he struck Pronous with his shining spear
in the bare chest beside the shield, and loosed his limbs;
he fell with a crash. Then he rushed upon Thestor,
son of Enops, who sat crouched in his polished chariot,
his wits stunned; the reins had flown from his hands.
Patroclus stood beside him, thrust his spear into the right jaw,
and drove it through the teeth. Taking hold above the rim,
he dragged him from the chariot, as a fisherman seated
upon a projecting rock draws a holy fish from the sea
with line and glittering bronze; so he drew Thestor,
mouth gaping, from the chariot with his shining spear,
and thrust him down upon his face; the life left him.

Then he met Erylaus rushing upon him and struck him
with a stone in the middle of the head; the whole skull split
inside the heavy helmet, and he fell face downward upon the earth;
death that destroys the spirit poured around him.

After him he sent Erymas, Amphoteron, Epaltus,
Tlepolemus son of Damastor, Echius, Pyris,
Ipheus, Euippus, and Polymelus son of Argeas—
all in succession he brought down upon the nourishing earth.

When Sarpedon saw his companions, their tunics without armor,
falling beneath the hands of Patroclus, son of Menoetius,
he called to the godlike Lycians and rebuked them:

“Shame, Lycians! Where do you flee? Be swift now!
I myself will meet this man, that I may learn
who he is who holds such power, and has done so much evil
to the Trojans, since he has loosed the knees of many brave men.”

He leaped from his chariot with his armor upon him,
and Patroclus, seeing him, sprang down from his own.
They rushed upon one another, crying aloud,
like two curved-clawed vultures fighting upon a high rock,
with a great and terrible cry.

The son of crooked-counseling Cronus saw them and pitied Sarpedon,
the dearest of men to him, and spoke to Hera, his sister and wife:

“Ah me! Fate has appointed that Sarpedon, dearest of men to me,
shall be overcome beneath Patroclus, son of Menoetius.
My heart is divided while I deliberate:
shall I seize him alive from the tearful battle,
and set him down in the rich land of Lycia,
or shall I let him be overcome beneath the hands of Menoetius' son?”

Ox-eyed, august Hera answered him:

“Most terrible son of Cronus, what word have you spoken?
Would you free from death a mortal man,
long ago appointed by his fate?
Do it; but know that all the other gods will not approve.
And consider this within your heart:
if you send your beloved son home alive from the hard battle,
then another god may wish to send his own dear son away.
Many sons of the immortals fight around great Priam's city;
you will kindle a dreadful resentment in all their hearts.
But if he is dear to you, and your heart laments him,
let him be overcome in the hard battle by Patroclus, son of Menoetius.
When breath and life have left him, send Death and Sleep
to carry him until they reach the people of broad Lycia.
There his brothers and companions will prepare him for burial,
with a mound and a pillar; that is the honor of the dead.”

So she spoke, and the father of gods and men did not refuse.
He poured bloody drops upon the earth, honoring his dear son,
whom Patroclus was destined to kill in fertile Troy,
far from his fatherland.

When the two were near one another, Patroclus struck
the renowned Thrasymelus, Sarpedon's excellent companion,
low in the belly; he loosed his limbs.
Sarpedon cast his shining spear a second time,
but missed, and struck the right shoulder of Pedasus,
the horse. The horse cried out as his life went from him,
and fell in the dust, while the spirit flew away.
The two horses sprang apart, the yoke cracked,
and the reins were tangled, when the side horse lay in the dust.

Automedon, famous for his spear, found the remedy.
He drew the long sword thick upon his thigh,
sprang forward, and cut away the side horse without missing.
The other two horses straightened, and the reins were drawn taut;
then again the two heroes came together over the battle that eats the heart.

Sarpedon again missed with his shining spear;
the point passed above Patroclus' left shoulder.
Then Patroclus rose behind his bronze,
and his cast did not fly vainly from his hand:
he struck Sarpedon where the midriff encloses the inward heart.
He fell as an oak falls, or a poplar,
or a tall pine which the carpenters cut upon the mountains
with sharpened axes, preparing it to be a ship's timber.
So he lay stretched before the horses and chariot,
roaring, and clutching the dust that ran red with blood.

As a lion kills a great-horned bull among the herd,
the bull that bellows beneath the lion's jaws,
so the lord of the shield-bearing Lycians, dying beneath Patroclus,
struggled, and called his dear companion by name:

“Glaucus, dear friend, now you must be a spearman
and a bold fighter among men, if you are swift.
Let the evil battle be your desire.
First go everywhere among the leaders of the Lycians,
and urge them to fight around Sarpedon;
then fight for me yourself with the bronze.

“I shall be a shame and a reproach to you every day,
if the Achaeans strip my armor when I fall
among the ships. Stand fast, and rouse all the people.”

When he had spoken, the end of death covered his eyes and nostrils.
Patroclus planted his foot upon his chest,
and drew the spear from the body; with it the point
came out, and the midriff and the spirit followed together.
Then he drew the spear from the flesh.
At once the Myrmidons held the horses there,
snorting and eager to flee, since they had left their masters'
chariot behind.
