## Revised translation pass

Nestor was not unaware of the cry, although he drank; he spoke to Machaon,
son of Asclepius: “Consider, noble Machaon, how this work will end.
A greater cry now rises beside the ships of vigorous young men.
Sit here and drink the shining wine until fair-haired Hecamede
warms the hot bath and washes the blood from you. I will go
to the watch-post and learn what is happening.”

He took the bright shield of his son, horse-taming Thrasymedes,
which lay in the hut, and wore his own father's shield;
then he took a strong spear sharpened with bronze and stood outside.
At once he saw the shameful work: some Trojans were being driven,
others were driving the Achaeans, and the Achaean wall lay fallen.

As the wide sea purples beneath a muffled wave, watching the swift roads
of shrill winds, yet does not roll forward or back until a chosen gust
descends from Zeus, so the old man debated in his divided heart:
whether to go among the swift Danaans, or to seek Agamemnon,
the shepherd of the people. He judged it better to go to Atreus' son.

The men were stripping one another in battle; the tireless bronze rang
upon their flesh beneath swords and double-edged spears. Then Nestor met
the kings fostered by Zeus, coming up from the ships—Tydeus' son,
Odysseus, and Agamemnon—all wounded by bronze. The ships had been drawn
far back from the gray sea, for the first line stood toward the plain,
and they had built the wall behind the sterns. Even the shore, broad though
it was, could not contain every ship, and the army was crowded;
therefore they dragged the vessels forward, filling the long mouth
of the beach as far as the headlands enclosed it. So the kings came,
leaning upon their spears, eager to know the battle, and their hearts grieved.

Agamemnon said: “Nestor, great glory of the Achaeans, why have you left
the man-destroying war and come here? I fear that mighty Hector will fulfill
the word he once spoke in the Trojan assembly: that he would not return
from the ships toward Ilium until he had burned them with fire
and killed us too. Now everything he promised is coming to pass.
Ah me—perhaps the other well-greaved Achaeans hold anger against me
in their hearts, like Achilles, and will not fight beside the sterns.”

Nestor, the Gerenian horseman, answered: “These things are already made,
and loud-thundering Zeus himself could not make them otherwise.
The wall we trusted to be an unbreakable defense for ships and men
has fallen. Beside the swift ships they fight without rest,
and even a watchman could not tell from which side the Achaeans
are driven, so mingled are the deaths and so high the cry.
Let us consider what the mind may do; I do not advise us to enter the fight,
for a wounded man cannot fight.”

Agamemnon answered: “Since they fight beside the sterns,
and neither wall nor trench has helped us—though the Danaans suffered much
to make them, hoping they would be an unbreakable defense—
then it must be Zeus' will that we perish here without a name,
far from Argos. I knew when he willingly defended the Danaans;
now I know that he honors the Trojans as the blessed gods,
and has bound our strength and hands. Come: let all obey my word.
Let us drag the ships nearest the sea into the divine water,
and moor them high upon the shore until the deathless night arrives,
if the Trojans then hold back from battle. In the night we could drag
all the ships away. There is no reproach in escaping evil,
even by night; better to flee and escape disaster than to be captured.”

Odysseus looked darkly at him: “What word has escaped the fence of your teeth,
Atreus' son? Would that you commanded some other, worthless army,
and not us, whom Zeus has granted from youth to old age
to endure grievous wars until each man perishes. Do you truly desire
to abandon the broad-streeted city of Troy, for which we suffer so much?
Be silent, lest another Achaean hear this speech—a speech no man
who knows how to speak wisely, and rules so many people, would utter.
I despise your mind for saying that, while battle and shouting stand firm,
you would drag well-benched ships to the sea, to make the Trojans'
victory more certain and our destruction more near. The Achaeans
will not hold the battle while the ships are drawn down;
they will look backward and withdraw from the fight. There your counsel
will destroy us, leader of peoples.”

Agamemnon answered: “Odysseus, your bitter rebuke has reached my heart.
Yet I did not command the sons of Achaea to drag the ships unwillingly.
If only someone, young or old, would speak a better plan;
I would welcome it.”

Then Diomedes, good at the war-cry, spoke: “The man is near;
we need not search long, if you are willing to obey and do not resent me
because I am the youngest among you. I too boast a good father,
Tydeus, whom the heaped earth covers at Thebes. Portheus had three
blameless sons, who lived in Pleuron and steep Calydon:
Agrius, Melas, and horseman Oeneus, my father's father,
who surpassed them all in courage. He remained there;
my father wandered to Argos, as Zeus and the other gods desired.
He married a daughter of Adrastus and lived in a wealthy house,
with broad wheat-bearing fields, many orchards, and many sheep;
he surpassed all the Achaeans with his spear—you would have heard it,
if the story were true. Therefore do not dishonor my speech,
thinking my birth poor or my courage weak.

“Come, let us go into battle, wounded though we are by necessity;
then let us keep ourselves away from the missiles, lest wound be added
to wound, but rouse the others, those who have held back
and no longer fight, though once they gave us aid from the heart.”

They listened and obeyed, and Agamemnon led them. The Earth-Shaker
was not blind to them; he came among them in the likeness of an old man,
took Agamemnon's right hand, and spoke: “Atreus' son, surely now
Achilles' destructive heart rejoices in his breast, watching the flight
and slaughter of the Achaeans; he has no understanding, not even a little.
Let him perish so; let the god cripple him. But the blessed gods
are not yet wholly angry with you. The Trojan leaders will soon
raise a great cloud of dust across the plain, and you will see them
fleeing toward the city from the ships and huts.”

Having spoken, he cried across the plain. As when nine or ten thousand
men shout together in battle, bringing the conflict of Ares,
so the Earth-Shaker sent his voice from his breast; he put great strength
into every Achaean heart, to fight without rest and endure the war.

Golden-throned Hera saw from Olympus, standing upon its ridge,
her brother and lord moving through the glorious battle, and rejoiced;
but when she saw Zeus seated on the highest peak of many-springed Ida,
her heart became bitter. Then ox-eyed, august Hera deliberated
how she might deceive the mind of aegis-bearing Zeus.

This seemed the best plan: to go to Ida after adorning herself,
that he might desire to lie with her, and pour upon his eyelids
and understanding a sleep gentle and unharming. She entered the chamber
her dear son Hephaestus had made, its close-fitting doors fastened
with a hidden key no other god could open. She shut the shining doors.

First she washed every stain from her lovely skin with ambrosia,
then anointed herself with ambrosial oil, sweet and fragrant;
when it moved through the bronze-floored house of Zeus,
its scent reached earth and heaven. She combed her hair and braided
the shining, beautiful ambrosial locks from her immortal head.
She put on the ambrosial robe Athena had worked and filled with devices,
fastening it with golden pins across her breast. Around her waist
she tied a belt with a hundred tassels, and in her pierced ears
placed triple-gemmed earrings, from which great grace shone.
Above, she covered herself with a beautiful new veil, white as the sun,
and bound splendid sandals beneath her shining feet.

When she had placed all her adornment upon her body, she left the chamber
and called Aphrodite apart from the other gods: “Dear child, will you obey
what I ask, or refuse in anger because I aid the Danaans while you aid Troy?”

Aphrodite, daughter of Zeus, replied: “Speak what you intend, revered goddess,
daughter of mighty Cronus; my heart urges me to do it, if I can do it,
and if it is a thing that can be done.”

Hera, thinking deceitfully, answered: “Give me now the love and longing
by which you subdue all immortals and mortal men. I am going to visit
the ends of the nourishing earth, Oceanus and mother Tethys,
who raised me well in their house after Rhea entrusted me to them,
when Zeus placed Cronus beneath earth and the barren sea.
I will visit them and settle their endless quarrel, for they have long
kept apart from bed and love since anger entered their hearts.
If I persuade their dear hearts with words and bring them together in love,
I shall always be called dear and honored by them.”

Smiling Aphrodite answered: “It is neither possible nor fitting to refuse
your word, since you sleep in the arms of Zeus, the greatest.”
She loosened from her breast the embroidered strap in which all
the spells were woven: love, desire, and intimate speech—
the beguiling words that steal the mind even from the most thoughtful.
She placed it in Hera's hands and said: “Hide this varied strap
within your bosom; all things are wrought inside it. I do not think
you will return unsuccessful in whatever your mind desires.”
Ox-eyed, august Hera smiled and hid it in her bosom.
