## Revised translation pass

So he spoke in anger, and cast the scepter upon the ground;
Tears rushed from his eyes, and pity seized the whole people.
All the others were silent; no one dared to answer
Telemachus with harsh words. Antinous alone replied:

“Telemachus, high speaker, with your unrestrained strength,
What word is this you have spoken, shaming us?
Would you fasten blame upon us? It is not the suitors’ fault,
But your own dear mother, who knows profit beyond all women.
Already this is the third year, and soon the fourth will come,
Since she has cheated the hearts of the Achaeans.
She gives every man hope, and sends promises to each;
Yet her mind intends another thing. This other device
She fashioned in her heart: she set up a great loom
Within the halls and wove a cloth, fine and broad, and spoke to us:

“‘Young men, my suitors, since noble Odysseus is dead,
Wait, though your desire urges you toward my marriage,
Until I finish this winding-sheet, lest all these threads
Be woven to no purpose—the burial cloth for the hero Laertes,
When the fatal portion of grievous death shall take him.
Let no Achaean woman throughout the land rebuke me,
If he should lie without a winding-sheet, though he has possessed
So many things.’

“So she spoke, and our proud hearts were persuaded.
From that day she wove the great cloth by day before us all,
But at night she would unravel it, after setting torches near her.
For three years she escaped our notice and persuaded the Achaeans;
But when the fourth year came, and the seasons passed,
One of the women who knew clearly told us;
We found her unraveling the shining cloth.
Then she finished it against her will, compelled by necessity.

“And now the suitors tell thee this, that thou mayest know it
Within thy own heart, and that all the Achaeans may know:
Send thy mother away, and command her to marry the man
Whom her father orders and whom she herself finds pleasing.
But if she continues to trouble the sons of the Achaeans
For a long time, thinking upon the gifts Athena gave her—
The skill to make beautiful works, the excellence of her mind,
And those cunning counsels such as we have never heard
Of any of the well-haired Achaean women of old,
Neither Tyro, nor Alcmene, nor fair-crowned Mycene—
Then, as long as she holds this purpose in her heart,
They shall consume thy livelihood and possessions.
She shall win great fame by it, but thou shalt lose
The great store of wealth that belongs to thee.
We shall go neither to our work nor anywhere else,
Until she marries whichever Achaean she chooses.”

## Decision log

- Preserve the scepter’s fall, Telemachus’ tears, the assembly’s silence, and
  Antinous’ isolated reply as a shift from civic pity to adversarial rhetoric.
- Keep Antinous’ charge that Penelope “cheats” the suitors while leaving the
  actual mechanics of her delay visible through the loom and nighttime undoing.
- Retain the shroud’s stated purpose for Laertes and Penelope’s fear of reproach
  from Achaean women; the craft is social obligation as well as deception.
- Preserve the exact duration—three years hidden, the fourth exposed—and the
  compulsion that finally forces Penelope to complete the cloth.
- Keep Antinous’ extraordinary catalogue of Penelope’s gifts and predecessors:
  Athena, Tyro, Alcmene, and Mycene, without treating the comparison as praise.
- Preserve the suitors’ possessive framing of Telemachus’ livelihood and their
  refusal to leave until Penelope chooses a husband.
