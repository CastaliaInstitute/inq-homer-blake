## Revised translation pass

Idomeneus struck Othryoneus as he sprang high; the bronze breastplate
could not stop him, and the spear was fixed in the middle of his belly.
As he fell he groaned. Idomeneus mocked him:
“Othryoneus, above all mortals I praise you—if you will truly fulfill
all that you promised Priam, who promised you his daughter.
We too would make you this offer: we would bring from Argos
the fairest daughter of Atreus, if you would help us take
well-inhabited Ilium. Come with us to the ships, and we will arrange
the marriage; we are not poor arrangers of bride-gifts.”

So he spoke, and dragged the hero by the foot through the battle.
Asius came to aid him, on foot before his horses; his breathless driver
held the reins tight over the horses' shoulders while Asius aimed at Idomeneus.
But Idomeneus struck first, beneath the throat and through the bronze.
Asius fell like an oak, an alder, or a tall pine that woodcutters
have felled with newly sharpened axes for a ship's timber;
he lay stretched before his horses and chariot, roaring in the dust
and clutching at the blood.

His driver was stunned in the mind and did not dare turn the horses
back through the enemy's hands. Antilochus, steadfast in battle,
struck him through the middle; the bronze breastplate could not hold,
and the spear entered his belly. He fell gasping from the polished chariot,
and Antilochus, son of great-hearted Nestor, drove the horses
away from the Trojans and toward the well-greaved Achaeans.

Deiphobus came near Idomeneus, grieving for Asius, and cast his shining spear.
Idomeneus saw it and avoided the bronze; his many-layered shield,
of ox-hide and gleaming bronze, hid him. The spear flew over it,
but the heavy hand was not spent: Deiphobus struck Hypsenor,
son of Hippasus, shepherd of the people, beneath the liver,
and at once loosened his knees.

Deiphobus shouted: “Asius does not lie dishonored here.
Even as he goes to the gates of strong Hades, he will rejoice,
for I have sent him a companion.” The Argives grieved at his boast,
and most of all it stirred warlike Antilochus. Yet, though grieving
for his companion, he did not forget him: he ran around the body
and covered it with his shield. Two faithful companions, Mecisteus,
son of Echius, and splendid Alastor, bent beneath the burden
and carried Hypsenor, groaning heavily, to the hollow ships.

Idomeneus did not cease his great courage. He sought either
to cover some Trojan with dark night, or himself to fall with a crash
while defending the Achaeans from destruction. There he killed Alcathous,
son of Aesyetes, son-in-law of Anchises. Alcathous had married
Hippodamia, the eldest daughter, whom her father and honored mother
loved in the house. She surpassed all her companions in beauty,
work, and understanding; therefore the best man in broad Troy
had married her. But Poseidon subdued him beneath Idomeneus:
he bewitched his shining eyes and fettered his bright limbs,
so that he could neither flee backward nor avoid the blow.

Idomeneus struck him in the middle of the chest, like a standing pillar
or a high-leaved tree. The spear broke the bronze tunic that had been
his defense; he cried out, bent around the weapon, and fell with a crash.
The spear stood fixed in his heart; his struggling heart shook the point,
until mighty Ares left him. Idomeneus shouted a terrible boast:

“Deiphobus, do you think that three men have fallen for one,
as you have boasted? Madman, stand before me yourself,
that you may see what sort of son of Zeus has come here—
the line that first gave Minos to Crete, Minos the blameless,
Minos who fathered Deucalion, and Deucalion who fathered me,
lord of many men in broad Crete. Now these ships have carried me here
to bring evil to you, your father, and the other Trojans.”

Deiphobus pondered in two ways: whether to call one of the great-hearted
Trojans back with him, or to test the fight alone. He thought it better
to go to Aeneas, whom he found standing last in the crowd;
for Aeneas was always angry with divine Priam, because the king
did not honor him, though he was a good man among the warriors.

Standing near him, Deiphobus spoke: “Aeneas, counselor of the Trojans,
you must now defend your brother-in-law, if any concern for him reaches you.
Come with us to aid Alcathous, who, though your brother-in-law,
raised you in his house when you were still a child. Idomeneus,
famous for the spear, has just stripped him.”

The words stirred Aeneas' heart, and he went eagerly toward Idomeneus,
intent upon the great work of war. Idomeneus was not seized by fear
like a child; he stood as a boar that trusts its strength in the mountains,
and waits for a great crowd of men rushing upon him in a lonely place.
His eyes flashed fire, he sharpened his teeth, eager to defend himself
against dogs and men. So Idomeneus, famous for the spear, stood waiting
for Aeneas' approach and called his companions—Ascalaphus, Aphareus,
Deipyros, Meriones, and Antilochus, masters of the war-cry:

“Come, dear friends, stand beside me, though I am alone.
I fear swift-footed Aeneas as he advances against me—
a man very strong in battle to kill his enemies,
and still in the flower of youth, when strength is greatest.
If we were equal in age and spirit, one of us would quickly carry away
the great victory.”

They all stood near him with one mind, leaning their shields upon their shoulders.
On the other side Aeneas called to Deiphobus, Paris, and godlike Agenor,
the Trojan leaders beside him; the people followed as sheep follow
their shepherd after pasture, and the shepherd's heart rejoices.
So Aeneas' heart rejoiced when he saw the host moving after him.

They rushed around Alcathous with long spears; the bronze clashed terribly
around their chests as they aimed at one another. Two men above all,
Aeneas and Idomeneus, equal to Ares, sought to cut each other's flesh
with pitiless bronze. Aeneas cast first, but Idomeneus saw the spear
and escaped; its point quivered and struck the ground, for it had leapt
from Aeneas' strong hand in vain. Idomeneus struck Oenomaus in the belly,
broke the hollow of his breastplate, and drove the bronze through his entrails.
Oenomaus fell in the dust and grasped the earth with his hand.

Idomeneus drew the long-shadowed spear from the corpse,
but could not strip the beautiful armor from his shoulders;
the missiles pressed him, and his legs were no longer steady for leaping.
He could still defend himself in close battle, but his feet would not
carry him swiftly from war, nor could he easily avoid a cast spear.

Deiphobus, who had held his anger always, cast his shining spear
at him as he went slowly away. But then he missed, and struck Ascalaphus,
son of Enyalius, through the shoulder; the heavy spear held him fast.
Ascalaphus fell in the dust and grasped the earth. Yet loud, mighty Ares
did not know that his own son had fallen in the hard battle;
he sat on highest Olympus beneath golden clouds, bound by Zeus' will,
as the other immortal gods were kept from the war.

Around Ascalaphus the fighters rushed together. Deiphobus tore the shining
helmet from him, while Meriones, equal to swift Ares, sprang and struck
Deiphobus' arm; the cheeked helmet fell from his hand with a hollow ring.
Meriones sprang again like a vulture, drew the heavy spear from the arm's base,
and withdrew among his companions. Polites, Deiphobus' own brother,
stretched out his hand and led him from the harsh battle to the swift horses;
they stood behind the fighting with chariot and driver ready.

The others fought on, and the unquenchable cry rose. Aeneas rushed at Aphareus,
son of Caletor, striking his turned neck with a sharp spear;
his head leaned aside, and shield and helmet fell as death poured over him.
Antilochus watched Thoon turn, sprang forward, and cut the vein
that runs up the back and reaches the neck. He fell upon his back,
stretching both hands toward his companions. Antilochus rushed upon him
and stripped the armor, looking around him; but Trojans surrounded him,
striking his broad, all-bright shield. They could not reach his tender flesh,
for Poseidon, Earth-Shaker, guarded Nestor's son amid the many missiles.

Antilochus was never far from his enemies; he wheeled among them,
and his spear never rested, but shook and spun continually
as he considered whom to strike or where to spring close.
He did not fail to see Adamas, son of Asius, aiming through the crowd;
Adamas struck the center of Antilochus' shield from close range.
But blue-haired Poseidon weakened the spear, hating Adamas' life:
one half remained in the shield like a stake burnt by fire,
the other half lay upon the ground. Adamas withdrew among his companions,
avoiding the fates; Meriones followed and struck him between the genitals
and navel, where Ares is most painful to grieving mortals.
The spear stood fixed; Adamas writhed like a bull that herdsmen bind
against its will and lead from the mountains. So he writhed a little while,
until Meriones came near and drew the spear from his flesh;
then darkness covered his eyes.
