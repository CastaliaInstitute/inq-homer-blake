## Revised translation pass

While Trojans and Achaeans fought outside the swift ships,
Patroclus sat in the hut of generous Eurypylus, pleasing him with words
and laying healing medicines upon his grievous wound to quiet its black pain.
But when he saw the Trojans rushing over the wall, and heard fear
and shouting among the Danaans, he groaned and struck his thigh:

“Eurypylus, I can no longer remain here, though I desire to help you;
a great battle has risen. Let your companion comfort you;
I will hurry to Achilles, that I may persuade him to enter the fight.
Who knows whether, with a god's help, I may stir his heart by persuasion?
Good is the counsel a companion gives.”

His feet carried him away. The Achaeans stood firm against the advancing
Trojans, though fewer, and could not drive them from the ships;
nor could the Trojans break the Achaean ranks and mingle with huts and vessels.
The battle held equal, like a carpenter's rule straightening ship-timber
in the hands of a skilled craftsman who knows every art by Athena's instruction.
Elsewhere men fought around other ships; Hector and glorious Ajax
were matched around one vessel, and neither could drive the other away:
the god had brought them together for that labor.

There Ajax, son of Clytius, struck Caletor in the breast with his spear
as he carried fire toward the ship; Caletor fell with a crash,
and the torch dropped from his hand. When Hector saw his cousin
falling in the dust before the dark ship, he called to Trojans and Lycians:
“Do not give way in this narrow place; save the son of Clytius,
lest the Achaeans strip his armor when he falls among the ships.”

He cast his shining spear at Ajax, but missed, and struck Lycopyhron,
son of Mastor, a Cytherean and companion of Ajax, who lived with him
after killing a man in sacred Cythera. Hector struck him in the head
above the ear; Lycopyhron fell backward from the stern into the dust,
and his limbs came loose. Ajax shuddered and called to his brother:

“Teucer, our faithful companion, Mastor's son, is killed—
the man we honored in our house like our own parents' child,
whom Hector has killed. Where are your swift-death arrows
and your bow, which Phoebus Apollo gave you?”

Teucer understood, came running beside him with his recurved bow
and arrow-bearing quiver, and quickly shot at the Trojans.
He struck Cleitus, splendid son of Peisenor, companion of Polydamas,
who held the reins; he had worked hard among the horses
where the densest ranks were driving for Hector and the Trojans.
The evil came upon him suddenly: the arrow entered the back of his neck.
He fell from the chariot, and the horses clattered empty harness.
Polydamas saw it first, ran before the horses, and gave them to Astynous,
son of Protiaon, urging him to hold them close; then returned to the foremost.

Teucer took another arrow against bronze-armed Hector,
and would have stopped his fighting beside the Achaean ships,
if he had struck him while he excelled and taken his life.
But Zeus' wise mind did not overlook it: he guarded Hector,
and robbed Teucer of glory. As Teucer drew the newly twisted bowstring,
it broke; the heavy bronze arrow veered elsewhere, and the bow fell from him.
Teucer trembled and said to Ajax:

“A god cuts the plans of our battle at the root. He has cast my bow
from my hands and broken the new-twisted string I tied this morning,
that it might endure the many arrows leaping from it.”

Great Telamonian Ajax answered: “Let the bow and the dense arrows lie;
the god has mixed them into grief for the Danaans. Take the long spear
and shield upon your shoulder, fight the Trojans, and rouse the other people.
Let them not take the well-benched ships without a struggle;
let us remember our courage.”

Teucer set the bow inside the hut, placed the four-layered shield
around his shoulders, set a well-made horse-haired helmet on his head,
its terrible crest nodding above, took a strong spear sharpened with bronze,
and went quickly to stand beside Ajax.

When Hector saw Teucer's arrows blunted, he called through the Trojans:
“Trojans, Lycians, and Dardanians, fighters at close quarters,
be men and remember your warlike strength beside the hollow ships.
I saw the arrows of a foremost man blunted by a god.
The strength Zeus gives to men is easily recognized:
he grants greater glory to some, and diminishes others,
as now he diminishes the Argives' strength and helps us.
Fight beside the ships in a mass. Let the man who is struck
and meets his death die; there is no shame in dying while defending
one's fatherland. His wife remains safe, his children, his house,
and his unviolated allotment, if the Achaeans go away with their ships
to their dear native land.”

Thus he stirred each man's heart. Ajax called from the other side:
“Shame, Argives! Now we must either perish or be saved,
and drive the evil from the ships. Do you hope that when Hector takes
the vessels each man will reach his own fatherland on foot?
Do you not hear him urging all his people to burn the ships?
He does not call them to the dance, but to fight.
We have no better counsel than to join hands and strength at once.
Better to perish in a single moment, or live, than to be worn out
for a long time beside the ships by men who are worse than we.”

He stirred the hearts of all. Hector killed Schedius, son of Perimedes,
leader of the Phocians; Ajax killed Laodamas, beautiful son of Antenor,
leader of the footmen. Polydamas killed Otus, companion of Phyleus,
leader of the great-hearted Epeians. Meges sprang at Polydamas,
but he dodged beneath the spear—Apollo would not let Panthous' son fall
among the foremost—and Meges struck Croesmus in the chest instead;
Croesmus fell with a crash, and Meges stripped the armor from his shoulders.

Dolops, skilled with the spear, sprang at Meges. He struck the middle
of his shield, but the close-fitted breastplate saved him:
the cuirass Phyleus had once brought from Ephyra, a guest-gift
from King Euphetes for protection in war, now guarded the son.
Meges struck the bronze horse-haired helmet at its crest,
and broke away the horsehair plume; the whole helmet fell in the dust
like a newly cut palm-tree, shining and young.
While Meges fought and hoped for victory, warlike Menelaus came to help;
he stood broad beside him, unseen, and struck Dolops from behind in the shoulder.
The spear passed through his chest, and he fell forward.

The two men began to strip his bronze armor, but Hector called all his brothers,
and first rebuked Melanippus, son of Hicetaon. While the man had been far
from the enemy grazing his cattle in Perkote, he had lived; but when the ships
arrived he returned to Troy and excelled among its fighters.
