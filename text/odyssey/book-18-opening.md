## Translation

Irus, the public beggar, came to drive Odysseus
From the threshold: “Move, old man, before they drag you out.
Do you not hear them urging me? I am ashamed.”
Odysseus looked darkly at him: “I do you no harm.
This doorway holds us both. Do not provoke me, lest I stain
Your lips and breast with blood. Tomorrow you will not return
To the house of Laertes' son.”

Antinous heard and laughed. He brought goat bellies filled
With fat and blood, promising the victor his choice,
And no other beggar entry to the feast. Odysseus asked
The suitors to swear that no one would strike him to help Irus.
Telemachus welcomed the contest. The king girded up his rags;
His broad thighs, shoulders, chest, and strong arms appeared.
Athena enlarged his limbs, and every suitor marveled.
Antinous threatened Irus with King Echetus if he lost.

The beggars raised their hands. Odysseus chose to strike lightly,
That the Achaeans would not recognize him. Irus struck his shoulder;
Odysseus struck beneath the ear and crushed the bone.
Dark blood poured from his mouth; he fell in dust, gnashing
His teeth and kicking the earth. The suitors laughed.
Odysseus dragged him beyond the porch, set him beside the fence,
And put the staff into his hand: “Sit here and keep
The dogs and pigs away. Do not lord it over strangers.”

Antinous gave the victor a great belly of goat; Amphinomus
Brought two loaves and a golden cup: “Hail, stranger;
May prosperity come, though now you suffer much.”
Odysseus answered him: “You seem wise, like your father Nisus.
Nothing is weaker than man among all that breathes.
While the gods give strength he expects no evil, but when they send
Grief he bears it against his will. I too once was prosperous,
But trusted strength and kin, and did much wrong.
Let every man accept in silence what the gods bestow.
I see these suitors cutting a man's possessions and dishonoring
The wife of one who will soon return. May a god lead you away
Before you meet him; he and the suitors will not part without blood.”

Athena then put it in Penelope's mind to appear before the suitors,
That desire might increase and she might be more honored
Before husband and son. She spoke with false laughter to Eurynome:
“I wish to appear, though I hate them; I would tell my son
Not to keep company with men who speak well but plan evil.”
Eurynome told her to wash and anoint her cheeks. Penelope refused:
“The gods destroyed my beauty when my husband sailed away.
Call Autonoe and Hippodameia; I will not enter the hall alone.”

Athena poured sweet sleep over her, cleansed her face with ambrosia,
Made her taller, fuller, and whiter than sawn ivory,
Then departed. Sleep released Penelope. She prayed for a gentle death,
And came down with two attendants, holding her shining veil before her cheeks.
The suitors' knees loosened, and desire enchanted every heart.

Penelope rebuked Telemachus: “Your judgment is no longer steady.
What a deed has happened here, and you allowed the stranger to suffer!
If a guest were beaten in our house, shame would fall on you.”
Telemachus answered that he knew the good and the evil,
But was still young and surrounded by men who had no allies.
He wished Zeus, Athena, and Apollo would make the suitors bend
Their heads in the hall, as Irus now sat beside the doors,
Drunk and unable to stand.

Eurymachus praised Penelope's beauty and stature. She answered:
“The immortals destroyed my beauty when the Argives sailed to Troy.
When Odysseus left, he took my right hand and said he doubted
All the Achaeans would return unharmed. He told me to guard
His parents and our son; when the boy grew bearded, I might marry.
Now that hateful marriage approaches. Yet men who court a wealthy woman
Bring cattle, sheep, and gifts; they do not consume another's wealth
Without payment.” Odysseus rejoiced inwardly, for she drew gifts from them.

Antinous told her to receive whatever gifts the Achaeans brought.
The suitors sent for them: a many-colored robe and twelve golden pins
From Antinous, a golden necklace set with amber from Eurymachus,
Gemmed earrings, a shining ornament, and other gifts.
Penelope went upstairs, while her women carried the treasures.

The suitors turned to dancing and song. At evening three lamps burned;
Dry wood and torches gave the hall light. Odysseus told the handmaids
To go to the queen, turn wool, or card it with their hands:
“I will provide light. Even if these men wait for Dawn,
They will not defeat me; I am enduring.”

Melantho, whom Penelope had raised but who loved Eurymachus,
Mocked him: “Stranger, why not sleep in the smithy or public shelter?
You speak boldly among men. Has wine made you foolish?
Because you defeated Irus, do you think no better man will rise
And beat your head, driving you out covered in blood?”
Odysseus threatened to tell Telemachus her words; the women fled in fear.

Eurymachus mocked him: “You have not come to this house without a god.
Would you work for me at the edge of my land, repairing fences and planting trees?
I would give you grain, clothing, and sandals. But you have learned evil work;
You prefer to beg and feed your belly.”
Odysseus answered with challenges: he would race Eurymachus at spring labor
With curved sickles, or plough a straight furrow behind equal oxen;
And if war rose that day, he would stand in the foremost rank with shield,
Two spears, and bronze helmet. “If Odysseus returned,” he said,
“These wide doors would grow narrow for you as you fled.”

Eurymachus seized a footstool, but Odysseus sat at Amphinomus's knees;
The stool struck the cupbearer's hand, and the pitcher rang as he fell.
Telemachus rebuked the suitors for being stirred by food and drink.
Amphinomus urged them not to strike the stranger or the servants,
But to pour a libation and leave the guest in Odysseus's house to Telemachus.
They agreed, drank as much as they wished, and went home to sleep.

## Decision log

- The production-facing opening keeps Irus's contest, Antinous's wager,
  Athena's bodily enlargement, Amphinomus's warning, Penelope's staged
  appearance, and the named bride-gifts in source order.
- The late hall scene retains the lamps, Melantho's abuse, Eurymachus's
  labor challenge, the cupbearer's fall, Telemachus's restraint, and
  Amphinomus's final appeal. The later ox-foot assault and bow contest are
  not imported.
