## Revised translation pass

So they fought around the well-benched ship. Patroclus came before Achilles,
the shepherd of the people, pouring warm tears like a dark-water spring
that spills its shadowed stream down the face of a steep rock.
When swift-footed, godlike Achilles saw him, he pitied him and spoke:

“Why do you weep, Patroclus, like a little girl
who runs beside her mother, asking to be lifted,
clutching her robe, and holds her back though she hastens,
weeping, and looks up until she is taken in her arms?
Like her, Patroclus, you let the tender tear fall.
Do you bring news for the Myrmidons—or for me?
Have you alone heard some message from Phthia?
They say Menoetius, son of Actor, still lives;
and Peleus, son of Aeacus, lives among the Myrmidons.
We should grieve bitterly if either had died.
Or do you mourn the Argives, how they perish beside
the hollow ships, because of their own transgression?
Speak out; do not hide it in your heart,
that we may know it together.”

With a heavy sigh Patroclus, horseman, answered:

“Achilles, son of Peleus, far greatest of the Achaeans,
do not be angry; such grief has overtaken the Achaeans.
All those who were foremost before now lie beside the ships,
struck down and wounded. Diomedes, son of Tydeus, is wounded;
Odysseus of the famous spear is wounded, and Agamemnon;
and Eurypylus has been pierced in the thigh by an arrow.
Physicians, rich in remedies, labor over them,
healing their wounds; but you, Achilles, cannot be moved.

“May no such anger take me—your anger,
which you keep as though it were a virtue.
Who will gain from you hereafter, even if you are born late,
if you will not turn that shameful ruin from the Argives?
Hard-hearted man—your father was not horseman Peleus,
nor Thetis your mother; the gray sea and the steep rocks
gave you birth, for your mind is pitiless.
But if some prophecy troubles your heart,
and your august mother has told you a word from Zeus,
send me forth, at least, and give me the people
of the Myrmidons, that I may become a light to the Danaans.
Put your armor upon my shoulders,
so that the Trojans, taking me for you, may withdraw from war,
and the war-worn sons of Ares, the Achaeans, may breathe again.
The respite from battle would be small;
and easily, while we are fresh and they are weary,
we could drive them back from the ships and huts toward the city.”

So he pleaded, greatly foolish; for he was about to ask
for evil death and the dark fate appointed to himself.
Swift-footed Achilles, deeply troubled, answered:

“Ah me, Patroclus, born of Zeus, what have you said?
I do not heed the prophecy I know,
nor has my august mother brought me any word from Zeus.
But this unbearable grief reaches my heart and spirit:
when a man, my equal, wishes to take the prize from me,
and rob me again of the honor that was mine,
though he has advanced beyond me in power.
That is the unbearable grief I carry,
for I have endured pain within my heart.
The girl whom the sons of the Achaeans chose as my prize,
whom I won with my spear when I sacked a strong-walled city,
that girl great Agamemnon, son of Atreus,
took back from my hands as though I were some dishonored stranger.

“Let what is past lie quiet; it could not forever
have been my mind's desire to rage.
I said I would not cease my anger
until the tumult and the battle reached my own ships.

“Put my famous armor upon your shoulders,
and lead the war-loving Myrmidons into battle,
if indeed the dark cloud of the Trojans has covered
the ships with force, while the Argives lie pressed
against the sea's edge and possess but a little land,
and all the city of Troy has grown bold against them;
for they do not see near them the shining front of my helmet.
Soon, fleeing, they would fill the channels with their dead,
if only great Agamemnon knew mercy.
But now the army fights all around him.
The spear of Tydeus' son Diomedes no longer rages
in the hands of the Danaans, to ward death from them;
nor have I heard the voice of the son of Atreus
calling from his hated head. But Hector, killer of men,
shouts commands to the Trojans, and they possess the field,
conquering the Achaeans with their cry.

“Even so, Patroclus, drive the ruin from the ships;
fall upon them with all your force, lest the blazing fire
burn the vessels and take away our homeward return.
But lay this command deep within your heart,
that you may win great honor and glory for me
before all the Danaans, and bring back the beautiful girl
and shining gifts they will return to me.

“When you have driven them from the ships, come back.
And if the thunder-loving husband of Hera grants you glory,
do not desire to fight the war-loving Trojans without me;
you would lessen my honor by doing so.
Do not, exulting in war and slaughtering the Trojans,
lead them toward Ilium, lest one of the everlasting gods
descend from Olympus; far above all others Apollo the Far-Shooter
loves that city. Turn back when you have placed the light
in the ships, and leave them still to contend across the plain.”

If only, Father Zeus, Athena, and Apollo, no Trojan
would escape death, not one of all who are here,
nor one of the Argives; and only we two might withdraw
from destruction, and unbind the sacred veils of Troy.

## Decision record

| Greek range | Decision | Reason / remaining check |
|---|---|---|
| 16.1–19 | Preserve the dark spring simile and Achilles' three-way question about the source of Patroclus' tears. | The opening keeps private pity, public crisis, and the possibility of news from Phthia in one encounter. |
| 16.20–45 | Retain the wounded leaders, Patroclus' accusation, the sea-and-rock birth taunt, and the proposed Myrmidon relief. | The appeal must remain both compassionate and rhetorically severe; “light” and brief respite answer the ship-fire crisis. |
| 16.46–63 | Keep the narrator's warning that Patroclus asks for his own death, and Achilles' refusal of prophetic explanation. | The fatal irony belongs to the poem's narrative voice, not to an explanatory gloss outside the scene. |
| 16.64–79 | Preserve Achilles' grievance over Briseis and the exact battlefield imbalance he names. | His permission grows out of injury and present evidence, not sudden generosity. |
| 16.80–96 | Keep the permission to clear the ships and the restrictions against pursuing Troy or fighting without Achilles. | The rescue order and the boundary that Patroclus will cross later must remain unmistakable. |
| 16.97–100 | Retain Achilles' compressed wish that only he and Patroclus might escape destruction and undo Troy. | The wish quietly binds their shared glory to the fatal separation that follows. |
