## Revised translation pass

Menelaus aimed at Helenus, who drew his bow; Helenus' arrow struck
the hollow of Menelaus' breastplate and sprang away, as dark beans or chickpeas
leap from a broad winnowing shovel beneath the breath and sweep of the fan.
Menelaus cast his spear and pierced the hand that held the polished bow;
the bronze went through it. He withdrew among his companions,
his hand hanging, while Agenor drew out the ash spear
and bound the wounded hand with a sling of soft sheep's wool.

Peisander came straight against renowned Menelaus; fate was leading him
to his death, and to Menelaus' victory in the grievous battle.
When they met, Menelaus' spear turned aside; Peisander struck
the broad shield, but the bronze could not pass through it:
the shield held, and the spear broke at the socket. Peisander rejoiced,
hoping for victory. But Menelaus drew his silver-studded sword
and sprang upon him. Peisander lifted from beneath his shield
a bronze battle-axe, long and finely polished, with an olive haft.
They struck together: Peisander hit the horse-haired crest
at the very top of the helmet; Menelaus struck his approaching forehead
above the brow. The bones cracked; his bloody eyes fell into the dust
before his feet. Peisander twisted down, and Menelaus planted his foot
upon his chest, stripped his armor, and shouted:

“You will leave the swift ships of the Danaans at last,
you overweening Trojans, insatiable in your dreadful cry,
never lacking insult or shame, you evil dogs who dishonored me
and did not fear the bitter anger of Zeus, the loud-thunderer,
the god of guests—he who will one day destroy your steep city.
You carried away my lawful wife and much property without consent,
though she had welcomed you; now beside the sea-going ships
you burn to throw destructive fire and kill the Achaean heroes.
But you will be checked, however eager for Ares.

“Father Zeus, they say that you are wiser than all men and gods,
and that everything comes from you. Yet see how you favor
these insolent Trojans, whose reckless strength is never satisfied
with the equal war. All things have their fill—sleep, love,
sweet song, and blameless dance; each of these a man may desire
more deeply than war. But the Trojans cannot have their fill of battle.”

He stripped the blood-stained armor and gave it to his companions,
then went again among the foremost. Harpalion, son of King Pylamenes,
sprang at him. He had followed his dear father to fight at Troy,
and would not return to his native land. He struck the middle
of Menelaus' shield from close range, but could not drive the bronze through;
he withdrew among his companions, looking all around him,
afraid that some man might reach his flesh.

Meriones sent a bronze-headed arrow as he fled and struck the right buttock;
the arrow passed beneath the bone and through the bladder.
Harpalion sat down in the arms of his friends, breathing out his life,
stretched upon the earth like a worm; dark blood ran from him
and soaked the ground. The great-hearted Paphlagonians labored around him,
lifted him into a chariot, and carried him sorrowing toward holy Ilium.
His father followed, pouring tears, but no ransom could restore his son.

Paris was deeply angered at the death, for Harpalion had been his guest
among the Paphlagonians. In grief he sent a bronze-headed arrow.
There was a rich and noble man named Euchenor, son of the seer Polyidus,
who lived in Corinth and knew his fatal fate. His good old father
had often told him he would die at home by a grievous sickness,
or be overcome by the Trojans among the Achaean ships.
Therefore he had boarded the vessel both to escape the hateful sickness
and not to suffer its pains in his heart. Paris struck him beneath
the jaw and ear; quickly the life left his limbs,
and hateful darkness covered him.

The armies fought like a burning fire. Hector, dear to Zeus,
did not know that on the left of the ships the Argives were destroying
his people. Soon the Achaeans might even have won the glory,
for the Earth-Shaker roused and defended them with his strength.
But Hector remained where first he had broken the dense ranks
of shield-bearing Danaans, beside the ships of Ajax and Protesilaus,
drawn up along the gray sea. Above them the wall had been built lowest,
where men and horses fought most violently.

There Boeotians, Ionians with trailing tunics, Locrians, Phthians,
and bright Epeians rushed to defend the ships. They could not drive
bright Hector, like flame, away. Mnestheus, son of Peteus,
led the Athenians, with Pheidas, Stichius, and good Bias beside him.
Phyleides Meges, Amphion, and Dracius led the Epeians;
Medon, the mighty-hearted, and Podarces led the Phthians.
Medon was the bastard son of Oileus and brother of Ajax;
he lived far from his native Phylace, having killed a kinsman
of his stepmother Eriopis, Oileus' wife. Podarces was son of Iphiclus,
son of Phylacus. The Phthians fought armored before their ships
among the Boeotians.

Swift Ajax, son of Oileus, no longer stood even a little apart
from Telamonian Ajax. They stood side by side like two wine-dark oxen
that pull an even plow across a field; much sweat rose around their horns
as they strained beneath the yoke, the polished plow alone dividing them
as they cut the furrow to its end. So the two stood close together.

Many good companions followed Telamonian Ajax and took his shield
whenever toil and sweat reached his knees. But no Locrians followed
the great-hearted son of Oileus, for they had no heart for close combat;
they had neither bronze horse-haired helmets, round shields,
nor ash spears. Trusting their bows and carefully twisted sheep's wool,
they had followed him to Ilium, and with dense arrows broke the Trojan ranks.
Thus some fought in front with elaborate weapons against the bronze-armed
Trojans and Hector, while the others behind shot unseen;
the arrows scattered the Trojans' courage.

The Trojans would then have withdrawn painfully from ships and huts
to windy Ilium, if bold Polydamas had not stood beside Hector and said:
“Hector, you are difficult to persuade by another's words.
Because the god has given you the work of war, you wish to know
more than others in counsel too. Yet you cannot take all gifts yourself:
one man receives war, another dancing, another the lyre and song,
and Zeus places a wide-seeing mind within another's breast,
a good mind from which many people profit, and which most benefits itself.

“But I will say what seems best. The battle's crown burns around you.
The great-hearted Trojans, after crossing the wall, stand divided:
some are apart with their arms, and some fight at the ships,
scattered, fewer against many. Withdraw and call all the best men here;
then we can make a complete plan: whether we shall fall among
the many-benched ships, if the god grants us power,
or come away from the ships unharmed. I fear the Achaeans may recover
their strength from yesterday, since beside the ships there remains
a man insatiable of war, whom I do not think will wholly refrain.”

Polydamas' speech pleased Hector. He leapt from his chariot in armor
and spoke to him: “Hold all the best men here; I will go there
and meet the battle, and quickly return when I have given my orders.”
He went like a snow-covered mountain, shouting, through Trojans and allies.
They all hurried toward Polydamas when they heard Hector's voice.
Hector went among the foremost, seeking Deiphobus, Helenus' strength,
Adamas son of Asius, and Asius son of Hyrtacus. He found none unharmed:
some lay beside the Achaean sterns, their lives lost beneath Argive hands;
others were within the wall, wounded by the spear.

Soon he found godlike Alexander, husband of fair-haired Helen,
on the left of the tearful battle, encouraging his companions
and urging them to fight. Standing near, Hector addressed him
with shameful words: “Paris, beautiful in form, woman-mad deceiver,
where are Deiphobus, Helenus, Adamas, and Asius?
Where is Othryoneus? Now steep Ilium is destroyed from its foundations;
now your own steep death is certain.”

Godlike Alexander answered: “Hector, since your heart is set
on blaming one who is not at fault, I will withdraw from battle
more often another time; my mother did not bear me wholly without courage.
Since you raised the battle beside the ships, we have fought here
without rest against the Danaans, and the companions you seek have died.
Deiphobus and Helenus are gone, both struck through the hand
by long spears; Cronus' son defended them from death.
Now lead wherever your heart and spirit command; we will follow eagerly,
and I do not say our strength will fail while strength remains.
But no man can fight beyond his power.”

He persuaded his brother's mind, and they went where battle and strife
were strongest, around Cebriones, blameless Polydamas, Phalces,
Orthaetus, godlike Polyphates, Palmys, Ascanias, and Morys,
sons and leaders who had come from fertile Ascania the day before,
and whom Zeus now roused to fight. They advanced like a storm of harsh winds,
sent down beneath their father Zeus' thunder, meeting the sea
with wondrous roar; waves upon waves rose and foamed in the loud sea.
So Trojans came in ranks, one behind another, bronze flashing,
with their leaders in the midst.

Hector led them, like murderous Ares, holding before him
his many-layered, rounded shield, thick with ox-hide and bronze;
the bright helmet trembled around his temples. Everywhere he tested
the ranks, stepping before them to see whether they would yield
when he advanced beneath his shield. But he did not shake the Achaeans'
hearts. Ajax first called him forward, striding wide:

“Madman, come close. Why do you frighten the Argives so?
We are not strangers to battle; it is beneath Zeus' evil scourge
that the Achaeans have been overcome. Surely your heart hopes
to destroy our ships, but we too have hands to defend them.
Much sooner your well-inhabited city would be taken and destroyed
by our hands. And for yourself, I say, the time is near when,
fleeing, you will pray to your father Zeus and the other immortals
that your horses be swifter than hawks with fair manes,
the horses that will carry you, raising dust, back toward the city.”

As he spoke, an eagle flew high upon the right, and the Achaean army
shouted, encouraged by the sign. Bright Hector answered:
“Ajax, speaker of empty words, ox-like fool, what have you said?
Would that I were always the son of Zeus who bears the aegis,
and that honored Hera had borne me, honored as Athena and Apollo are—
as surely as this day brings evil to all the Argives,
and you among them, if you dare remain and meet my long spear,
which will stain your tender flesh. You will glut the dogs and birds
with your fat and flesh, falling beside the Achaean ships.”

So he led them on, and they followed with an unearthly clamor;
the army behind him shouted. The Argives answered from the other side,
and did not forget their courage, but waited for the Trojan leaders.
The cry of both armies reached the upper air and the rays of Zeus.
