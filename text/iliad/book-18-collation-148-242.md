## Revised translation pass

The feet of Thetis bore her toward Olympus; but the well-greaved Achaeans,
fleeing in wondrous tumult before man-slaying Hector,
reached their ships and the Hellespont.
Nor could they yet drag Patroclus, the dead companion of Achilles,
from the weapons. For the host and the horses came upon him again,
and Hector, son of Priam, whose strength was like a flame.
Three times glorious Hector seized him by the feet behind,
eager to drag him away, and called aloud to the Trojans;
three times the two Ajaxes, clothed in furious strength,
shook him from the corpse. Yet Hector, trusting in his steadfast might,
now sprang into the tumult, now stood and shouted terribly,
and would not withdraw at all.

As shepherds in the fields cannot drive a bright-maned lion
from the body of a great, famished beast,
so the two armored Ajaxes could not make Hector,
son of Priam, afraid and draw him from the dead.
He would have dragged the body away and won boundless glory,
had not swift-footed Iris, the wind-footed messenger,
come running from Olympus to the son of Peleus,
to summon him secretly to arm himself;
for Hera had sent her before Zeus and all the other gods
who dwell around high-snowed Olympus knew of it.

She stood near him and spoke in winged words:

“Rise, son of Peleus, most terrible of all men.
Defend Patroclus, for whose sake the bitter battle stands
before the ships, and men destroy one another—
some defending the dead, while the Trojans rush to drag him
toward windy Ilium. Hector above all is eager
to haul him away; his heart commands him to fix his head
upon the stakes, when he has severed it from the tender neck.
Rise; lie there no longer. Let reverence for Patroclus enter you,
lest he become a plaything for Trojan dogs;
and shame would be yours, if the corpse should suffer defilement.”

The godlike, swift-footed Achilles answered her:

“Iris, goddess, which of the gods has sent you as my messenger?”

Wind-footed Iris answered him again:

“Hera has sent me, the glorious wife of Zeus.
Nor does the high-throned son of Cronus know it,
nor any other of the immortals who inhabit snowy Olympus.”

Swift-footed Achilles replied:

“How shall I go into the battle? They hold my armor.
My dear mother would not let me arm myself
until I saw her come before my eyes;
she promised to bring beautiful weapons from Hephaestus.
I know no other man whose glorious shield I could put on,
unless it were the shield of Ajax, son of Telamon—
but even he, I think, is among the foremost ranks,
striking the enemy with his spear around dead Patroclus.”

Wind-footed Iris answered him:

“We know well that your famous armor is in their hands.
But go as you are to the trench and show yourself to the Trojans;
perhaps they will fear you and hold back from battle,
and the warlike sons of the Achaeans, weary as they are,
may draw one breath from their toil.”

So she spoke, and swift-footed Iris departed.
But Achilles, dear to Zeus, rose; and Athena cast around his shoulders
the tasselled aegis. Around his head the divine goddess set
a golden cloud, and from it kindled a flame that shone afar.

As when smoke rises from a city and reaches the sky,
from an island far away, when hostile armies fight around it,
and all day are judged by hateful Ares outside their own city;
but when the sun goes down, the watch-fires blaze in triple ranks,
and their light leaps high, that the neighboring people may see,
if perhaps defenders may come with their ships—
so from Achilles’s head the gleam reached the upper air.
He went from the wall and stood upon the trench;
he did not mingle with the Achaeans,
for he watched his mother’s solemn command.

There he stood and cried aloud. Far away Pallas Athena
lifted her voice; and among the Trojans she raised immeasurable panic.
As when a trumpet’s voice, clear and piercing,
cries around a city when enemies who destroy life surround it,
so clear and piercing then was the voice of the son of Aeacus.
When they heard the bronze voice of Aeacus’s son,
the hearts of all were shaken; and the fair-maned horses
turned their chariots backward, for they foresaw sorrow in their hearts.
The charioteers were struck with terror when they saw
the tireless, terrible fire burning above the head
of great-hearted Peleus’s son; bright-eyed Athena kindled it.

Three times divine Achilles cried aloud above the trench,
and three times the famous Trojans and their allies were thrown into confusion.
There twelve of the bravest men perished beside their chariots
and their spears. But the Achaeans gladly dragged Patroclus
from beneath the weapons and laid him upon a bier;
his dear companions stood around him mourning.
Swift-footed Achilles followed, shedding hot tears,
when he saw his faithful companion lying upon the bier,
torn by the sharp bronze—him whom he had sent to war
with horses and chariot, but whom he did not receive again
returning home.

Then ox-eyed, honored Hera sent the tireless sun
against his will to return into the streams of Oceanus;
the sun went down, and the divine Achaeans ceased
from the force of war and the equal battle.
