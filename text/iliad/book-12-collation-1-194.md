## Revised translation pass

### 12.1–39 — the wall's future

So, while the valiant son of Menoetius, within the shelter
of his hut, was tending Eurypylus, the wounded man,
the Argives and the Trojans fought in close confusion;
and yet the trench and wall above it could no longer
hold back the Danaans. It was a broad wall, built to guard
the ships, with a trench driven round it. They had made it
without the bright hecatombs that please the deathless gods,
that it might keep their swift ships and their abundant spoil
within its compass. Therefore it was not built to stand long.

So long as Hector lived, while still Achilles nursed his anger,
and Priam's city stood unbroken, so long the great Achaean wall
also endured. But when the noblest Trojans had fallen,
and many Argives too had perished, while others remained,
and Priam's city was taken in the tenth returning year,
and the Argives sailed homeward to their dear fatherland,
then Poseidon and Apollo would devise its ruin.

They would turn the strength of all the rivers upon it—
Rhesus, Heptaporus, Caresus, and Rhodius,
Grenicus, Aesepus, sacred Scamander,
and Simoeis, where so many shields and helmets fell
into the dust, and the race of half-divine men.
Toward the wall Phoebus Apollo would turn their mouths;
for nine days he would drive their waters against it,
while Zeus rained without ceasing, that the sea-born wall
might sooner be made smooth. Poseidon himself, the Earth-Shaker,
would lead, holding his trident in his hands, and send
all the foundations out upon the waves—the stones and timbers
which the Achaeans had laid there laboring. He would level it
beside the loud-flowing Hellespont, then cover the wide shore
again with sand, and turn the rivers back along their former beds.

### 12.34–59 — Hector and the trench

Such things would Poseidon and Apollo accomplish afterward;
but now the battle burned around the well-built work,
and the towers rang beneath the spears that struck them.
The Argives, broken under the scourge of Zeus, were penned
beside the hollow ships, and feared Hector, the strong contriver
of terror. He still fought as a whirlwind before them—
as when a boar or lion, wheeling, flashes its strength
against the hounds and men who stand in ranks before it,
their spears flying thickly from their hands. The beast's
great heart does not tremble, nor does it flee; its courage
will be its death. It turns and tests the close-drawn lines,
and wherever it rushes, there the ranks give way.

So Hector moved among the crowd, urging his companions
to cross the trench. But his swift horses would not attempt it;
they snorted, standing at its very edge. The trench was broad,
and neither leaping over nor passing through it could be easy:
steep banks rose on either side, and above them stood
the sharp stakes the Achaeans had planted, close and great,
to keep hostile men away. No horse, drawing a well-wheeled car,
could enter there; but the footmen longed to make the passage.

### 12.60–107 — Polydamas' counsel and the five divisions

Then bold Polydamas stood beside Hector and spoke:
“Hector, and you other leaders of the Trojans and their allies,
we drive our eager horses blindly through the trench,
though crossing it is hard, for sharp stakes stand within it,
and beside them is the Achaean wall. We cannot descend there
and fight with horsemen: the way is narrow, and I believe
we shall be wounded. If Zeus, the loud-thundering one,
who now thinks evil thoughts for us, intends to help the Trojans,
then I would gladly see these Argives perish here,
namelessly, far from Argos. But if they turn and drive us back,
and we are thrown into the excavated trench from the ships,
I do not think a messenger will return to the city,
after the Achaeans have wound themselves around us.

“Come, then: let us all obey the counsel I shall give.
Let the attendants hold our horses here beside the trench;
we ourselves, on foot and clothed in armor, all together,
will follow Hector. The Achaeans will not stand against us
if the edge of destruction has indeed been fastened to them.”

Thus Polydamas spoke, and Hector welcomed the harmless counsel.
At once he leapt from his chariot in his armor; nor did the others
remain upon their horses. When they saw the bright Hector descend,
they all sprang down. Each man charged his charioteer to keep
the horses in good order there beside the trench. Then they stood
apart, arranged in five companies, each behind its leader.

With Hector and blameless Polydamas went the largest and best,
most eager to break the wall and fight beside the hollow ships;
with them went Cebriones. Paris led the second company,
with Alcathous and Agenor; Helenus and godlike Deiphobus,
Priam's sons, led the third, with the hero Asius, son of Hyrtacus.
Aeneas, splendid son of Anchises, led the fourth,
with Archelochus and Acamas, sons of Antenor,
skilled in every kind of war. Sarpedon led the famous allies,
and chose beside him Glaucus and warlike Asteropaeon;
for, after himself, he judged them best among the host.

### 12.108–153 — Asius and the Lapith gate

All the other renowned Trojans and their allies obeyed
the blameless counsel of Polydamas. Yet Asius, lord of men,
son of Hyrtacus, would not leave his horses and attendant behind.
Fool that he was, he drove with them toward the swift ships,
though he was never to escape the evil death that waited there,
nor return in pride with horses and chariot to windy Ilium.
Idomeneus, son of Deucalion, was to meet him with the spear.

Asius came upon the left of the ships, where the Achaeans
were returning from the plain with horses and chariots.
He found the gates thrown wide—the doors and the long bar
were not set against the fugitives, but men stood ready
to save any companion who fled from battle. He held his horses
straight ahead, while those behind him shouted sharply;
they thought the Achaeans would no longer stand. At the gates
they found two men, the best of the Lapith spearmen:
strong Polypoetes, son of Pirithous, and Leonteus,
like murderous Ares among mortal men.

The two stood before the lofty gates like oak trees on the mountains,
whose great roots hold them fast while they endure the wind and rain.
Trusting their hands and strength, they waited without fear
for mighty Asius. His men came straight against the well-built wall,
lifting their dry ox-hide shields and raising a tremendous cry;
they surrounded Asius, Iamenus, Orestes, Asiades Adamas,
Thoon, and Oenomaus. Within, the Achaeans urged one another
to defend the ships. But when they saw the Trojans rushing upon the wall,
and fear and shouting rose among the Danaans, the two Lapiths
leapt outside the gates and fought like wild boars in the mountains,
who await the rushing hunters and dogs, then spring sideways,
breaking the thicket behind them, until a man strikes one down.

### 12.154–194 — the first assault and Lapith counterstroke

Thus the bright bronze rang upon the chests of men who met,
for they fought with all their strength, trusting their people
and the courage in their hands. From the towers the defenders threw
rough stones, guarding themselves, their huts, and the swift ships;
they fell like snowflakes when a violent wind shakes dark clouds
and pours them thickly over the earth that feeds all things.
So weapons streamed from the hands of Achaeans and Trojans alike;
helmets and hollow shields cried out beneath the blows.

Then Asius, son of Hyrtacus, groaned and struck his thigh,
and, raging, lifted his voice: “Father Zeus, you too are wholly
made deceitful! I thought the Achaean heroes could not withstand
our strength or our hands, which no one might restrain.”

But those two stood firm, like wasps or bees that build their house
beside a rugged road and will not abandon the hollow dwelling;
they guard their young and drive the hunters from the place.
So the two would not yield at the gates until they had killed
or been themselves overcome. Nor did Zeus bend his mind
to all Asius said; his heart desired to give Hector glory.

Elsewhere the battle raged around every gate, and it is hard for me,
like a god, to tell of all the things that happened there;
for a stone fire rose everywhere around the wall,
and the Argives, grieving, defended the ships beneath necessity.
All the gods who stood beside the Danaans in the war
were troubled in their hearts, and the Lapiths joined the battle.

There strong Polypoetes, son of Pirithous, struck Damasus
through the bronze-cheeked helmet; the spear broke through bone
and subdued the man in all his eagerness. Then he stripped
Pylon and Ormenus. Leonteus, branch of Ares, struck Hippomachus,
son of Antimachus, beneath the belt. Then he drew his sharp sword
and, springing through the crowd, struck Antiphates at close quarters;
the man fell backward upon the ground. After him he sent Menon,
Iamenus, and Orestes, one after another, down upon the earth.
