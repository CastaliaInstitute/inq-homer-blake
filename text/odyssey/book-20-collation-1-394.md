## Revised translation pass

### Lines 1–56 — The forecourt and Athena

Odysseus lay in the forecourt on an ox-hide,
Unworked and brown, with many fleeces heaped above him;
And over him Eurynome spread a cloak.
The women who had joined the suitors came forth,
Laughing together, careless in the night.
His heart was stirred: should he strike every one,
Or leave them yet to mingle with the arrogant men?
Within his breast it growled, as a bitch will growl
Beside her young when some strange man draws near.

He struck his breast and spoke to his own spirit:
“Endure, my heart. You endured a worse thing once,
When the Cyclops ate your brave companions there,
And craft brought you from the dark cave, though you
Believed that you would die.” His heart obeyed.

He turned from side to side, as one beside a fire
Turns the great stomach of a sheep, filled with blood
And fat, that it may roast on every side;
So he revolved the thought of laying hands
Upon the suitors, standing one man against so many.

Then Athena came from heaven in a woman’s likeness
And stood above his head. “This house is yours,”
She said; “your wife is here, and your son is such
As any man would pray to see beside him.”

“All this is true,” Odysseus answered, “goddess;
Yet how shall I confront these shameless men,
Who gather here continually? And when I have slain them,
If Zeus and you have granted me the victory,
Where shall I flee? Consider this for me.”

“You are a reckless man,” the goddess said;
“A mortal trusts a comrade even weaker than himself,
But I am a god, and I will guard you through your labor.
If fifty companies of men should ring us round,
All hungry for the battle, you would drive them off,
And carry home their cattle and their sheep.
Let sleep receive you; to keep watch through the night
Is weariness itself.” She went again to Olympus,
And sleep, the loosener of the limbs, descended on him.

### Lines 57–101 — Penelope’s prayer

Penelope awoke, sat upon her soft bed, and wept. When she had exhausted her lament, she prayed first to Artemis: “Strike me with an arrow and take my life now; or let a storm carry me away through the misty paths and cast me at Ocean’s backward-flowing mouth. Let the gods destroy me, or let Artemis shoot me, before I gladden the mind of a worse man than Odysseus.”

The poem recalls the daughters of Pandareus, whom the Harpies carried away to serve the hateful Erinyes after their parents died, though Aphrodite had nourished them and Hera, Artemis, and Athena had given them beauty, wisdom, and skill. Penelope wished such a death for herself, that she might see Odysseus and not be made the joy of another. Yet even death has some consolation: after weeping by day, sleep can make a sufferer forget both good and evil. But the god had sent Penelope a cruel dream, in which Odysseus appeared beside her just as he had been with the army; her heart rejoiced as though the dream were waking truth.

### Lines 102–143 — Thunder and the mill-woman

Odysseus heard her weeping and wondered whether she had recognized him. He gathered the cloak and fleeces, placed them on a chair within the house, carried the ox-hide outside, and raised his hands to Zeus: “Father Zeus, if you have brought me home by your will, send me a sign within the house; let another sign appear from without.”

At once Zeus thundered from bright Olympus, though there was no cloud. A mill-woman heard it nearby. Of twelve women who ground barley and wheat for the household, she alone had not yet stopped, being the weakest. She spoke a sign for her master: “You thunder from the starry heaven, though no cloud is here. Fulfill this word for me: let this be the last meal the suitors eat in Odysseus’s house, for their toil has broken my knees.” Odysseus rejoiced. Telemachus rose from bed like an immortal, armed with sword, spear, and bright sandals, and stood upon the threshold.

### Lines 144–240 — Telemachus, Eumaeus, and Melanthius

Telemachus asked Eurycleia whether the stranger still lay without, neglected in bed and food. She answered that he had drunk as long as he wished but said he was not hungry; when he remembered sleep she had offered a bed, but he chose the untanned hide and sheep fleeces in the forecourt, and the women had covered him with a cloak. Telemachus went to the assembly with two white dogs.

Penelope ordered Eurycleia and the women to spread rugs over the well-made chairs, wipe all the tables, clean the mixing bowls and cups, and fetch water from the dark spring. The suitors would return early, since it was a feast day. The women went to the spring; others worked within. Eumaeus brought three fat pigs, and Odysseus asked whether the suitors still dishonored him. “May the gods avenge their violence,” Eumaeus answered. “I weep at the thought of Odysseus. Yet I believe he may return and scatter them. If he were dead, I should long ago have gone to another king; but I wait for him.”

“You are neither foolish nor weak,” said Odysseus. “I swear by Zeus, the guest-table, and the hearth of Odysseus that, while you are here, Odysseus will come home; with your own eyes you shall see the suitors killed.” Eumaeus prayed that the word might be fulfilled.

Melanthius, the goatherd, came bringing goats distinguished among the herds for the suitors’ dinner. He tethered them and insulted Odysseus: “Why do you not go out? We shall not part before our hands have tested you; you beg without order, and there are other feasts among the Achaeans.” Odysseus silently shook his head, plotting evil.

### Lines 241–320 — The last feast and Ctesippus

The suitors were preparing death for Telemachus when an eagle flew upon their left, carrying a trembling dove. Amphinomus told them to remember the feast, and they entered Odysseus’s house. They sacrificed sheep, fat goats, pigs, and a cow; roasted the inward parts, mixed the wine, and shared the meal. Telemachus had ordered that Odysseus receive an equal portion, so those serving him set an equal share beside him at the stone threshold.

Ctesippus of Same, trusting in immense wealth and desiring the wife of long-absent Odysseus, said: “It is not right to deprive a guest of his portion.” He took an ox’s foot from a basket and threw it. Odysseus bent his head aside and smiled a dark, terrible smile; the foot struck the wall. Telemachus rebuked Ctesippus: “You did not strike the stranger. Had you struck him, my spear would have passed through you, and your father would have prepared your tomb instead of your wedding. We have endured the slaughter of sheep, the drinking of wine, and the dragging of our maidservants through this house. Do not continue. If you desire my death, say so; I would rather die than watch such deeds.”

### Lines 321–394 — The blood-wet vision

Agelaus urged the suitors to stop striking the stranger or any servant. He advised Telemachus and his mother to let Penelope marry the best man, the one who brought the greatest gifts, so that Telemachus might enjoy and divide his father’s possessions. Telemachus answered that he did not delay his mother’s marriage: she might marry whom she wished, and he would give her countless gifts. But he would not drive her unwillingly from the house. May no god bring that about.

The suitors broke into an unnatural laughter. Their minds were shaken; they ate bloody flesh, and tears filled their eyes while their hearts imagined lamentation. Theoclymenus, godlike in appearance, spoke: “What evil is this? Your heads, faces, and knees are wrapped in darkness. Cries burn around you; your cheeks are wet with tears; the walls and beautiful crossbeams drip blood. The porch and courtyard swarm with shades rushing toward Erebus. The sun has vanished, and evil mist covers the world.”

Eurymachus called for the young men to lead him outside, because he spoke like night. Theoclymenus answered: “I need no guide. I have eyes, ears, feet, and a mind that is not contemptible. I go outside, for I see the evil coming upon you, and none of the suitors who devise outrage in Odysseus’s house will escape it.” He went to Peiraeus, who welcomed him.

The suitors looked at one another and mocked Telemachus, laughing at his guest. One proposed putting the strangers on a ship and sending them to Sicily. Telemachus remained silent, watching his father and waiting for the hour when he would lift his hands against the shameless men. Penelope, seated among the women, heard every word. Their feast had been sweet and abundant, but never would another be so hateful as the meal that the goddess and the strong man were about to prepare for them, because they had first devised such outrage.

## Decision log

- The collation retains the full 394-line sequence, including the forecourt’s animal similes, Penelope’s Artemis prayer, the Pandareus remembrance, and the mill-woman’s clear-sky thunder prayer.
- Eumaeus’s loyalty and oath, Melanthius’s insult, the eagle and dove, the equal guest portion, Ctesippus’s ox-foot attack, and Telemachus’s threat remain distinct episodes.
- Theoclymenus’s vision preserves the wet blood, shades, vanished sun, and mist as signs the suitors cannot interpret.
- The register is ceremonial and blank-verse-oriented, a Longfellow-inspired working draft rather than a claim of exact imitation.
