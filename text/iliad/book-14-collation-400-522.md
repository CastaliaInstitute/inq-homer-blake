## Revised translation pass

The cry of Trojans and Achaeans was terrible as they rushed upon one another.
Hector cast first at bright Ajax, who was turned directly toward him;
but the two sword-belts stretched across Ajax' breast—one for his shield,
one for his silver-studded sword—and saved his tender flesh.
Hector was angered that the swift weapon had flown from his hand in vain,
and withdrew among his companions to escape the fates.

But great Telamonian Ajax, as Hector went, lifted a stone among the many
fragments of ships that rolled beneath the fighters' feet. He struck Hector
above the rim of the shield, near the neck. As when a tree falls uprooted
beneath the thunderbolt of Father Zeus, and the dreadful scent of brimstone
rises from it, so Hector's strength quickly fell into the dust.
He let the spear fall from his hand; shield and helmet fell with him,
and his ornamented armor rang around his body.

The Achaeans rushed upon him with a great shout, hoping to drag him away,
and threw their close-set spears. Yet none could wound or strike
the shepherd of the people, for the best surrounded him:
Polydamas, Aeneas, Agenor, Sarpedon, leader of Lycia,
and blameless Glaucus. None of the others neglected him;
they held their rounded shields before him, while his companions lifted
him in their arms and carried him through the battle to the swift horses,
which waited behind the war with chariot and driver. They bore him
groaning heavily toward the city.

When they reached the ford of the broad-flowing Xanthus,
the river born to immortal Zeus, they lifted him from the horses,
laid him upon the ground, and poured water over him. He breathed again,
opened his eyes, sat upon his knees, and vomited dark blood.
Then he fell backward upon the earth; black night covered his eyes,
and the missile still mastered his heart.

When the Argives saw Hector carried away, they sprang more fiercely
upon the Trojans and remembered their courage. Swift Ajax, son of Oileus,
was first: he leapt upon Satnius, son of Enops, whom a noble water-nymph
had borne beside the banks of the Satnioeis to the herdsman Enops.
Ajax struck him in the flank; he turned and fell, and Trojans and Danaans
closed in a hard battle around his body.

Polydamas, son of Panthous, came to defend him and struck Prothoenor,
son of Areilycus, in the right shoulder; the heavy spear held him fast,
and he fell into the dust and grasped the earth. Polydamas shouted:
“Surely I do not think the spear leapt in vain from the strong hand
of Panthous' son; some Argive received it in his flesh,
and, leaning upon it, I think he will descend into the house of Hades.”

The Argives grieved at his boast, and most of all it stirred the warlike heart
of Telamonian Ajax, for Prothoenor had fallen near him. Ajax cast his shining
spear swiftly; Polydamas avoided the dark fate with a sideways leap,
but Archelochus, son of Antenor, received it. The spear struck where head
and neck join, at the last vertebra, and severed both tendons.
His head, mouth, and nostrils struck the ground far before his knees.

Ajax cried to blameless Polydamas: “Consider this, Polydamas,
and tell me truly: was Archelochus not worth the life of Prothoenor?
He does not seem ignoble or born of ignoble people;
he was brother, or son, of horse-taming Antenor,
for his lineage was nearest to his. You knew him well.”
The Trojans were seized by grief when he spoke.

Then Acamas struck Boeotian Promachus, standing over his brother,
and dragged him by the feet. Acamas shouted a terrible boast:
“Argives, insatiable in arrows and threats, this labor and misery
will not be ours alone; you too shall one day be killed here.
Remember how Promachus sleeps, subdued by my spear,
that the vengeance for his brother may not remain long unpaid.
Then some man in his house may pray to have left behind
a kinsman who can defend him from harm.”

The Argives grieved at his boast, and it stirred the warlike heart
of Peneleos. He sprang at Acamas, but Acamas did not await his attack.
Peneleos struck Ilioneus, son of Phorbas, rich in sheep,
whom Hermes loved among the Trojans and had endowed with wealth.
His mother had borne Ilioneus as her only child.
Peneleos struck him beneath the brow, at the root of the eye;
he drove the eye from its socket, and the spear passed through eye and nape.
Ilioneus sat with both hands spread. Peneleos drew his sharp sword,
cut through the middle of his neck, and severed the head and helmet together;
the strong spear still stood in the eye. Peneleos lifted the head
like a poppy and showed it to the Trojans, saying:

“Tell the dear father and mother of Ilioneus to mourn him in their halls.
Nor shall the wife of Promachus, daughter of Alegenor,
rejoice when her beloved husband returns with the sons of Achaea
from Troy in their ships.”

At his words trembling seized every Trojan limb,
and each man looked about him for the way to escape steep death.

“Tell me now, Muses who possess the Olympian halls,
who first lifted the bloody spoils of the Achaeans,
when famous Earth-Shaker turned the battle.”

First Telamonian Ajax struck Hyrtius, son of Gyrtius,
leader of the strong-hearted Mysians. Antilochus stripped Phalces
and Mermes; Meriones killed Morys and Hippotion;
Teucer took Prothoön and Periphetes; then Atreus' son
struck Hyperenor, shepherd of the people, in the flank.
The bronze poured out his entrails, and his soul rushed through the wound;
darkness covered his eyes. Swift Ajax, son of Oileus,
took the greatest number, for no man among the fleeing could keep pace
with him when Zeus raises terror in the limbs.
