# Iliad Book 16 — read-aloud and fidelity revision log

**Scope:** Iliad 16.1–867, current working translation  
**Date:** 2026-09-02  
**Status:** editorial working evidence; not a gate pass  
**Reviewer:** AI-assisted draft review; human sign-off required

This pass tests Patroclus’s plea, Achilles’ conditional permission, the armor
handoff, the Myrmidon advance, Sarpedon’s death, the struggle for his body, and
Patroclus’s fall. It does not certify meter or replace an independent
omissions/additions review.

## Findings and actions

| Passage | Finding | Action or decision |
|---|---|---|
| 16.1–100 | Patroclus’s tears turn distant battlefield disaster into intimate action. | Preserve the wounded leaders, Eurypylus, Briseis’s tears, and the appeal for the Myrmidons without making Patroclus merely a messenger. |
| 16.101–220 | Achilles’ permission is generous but sharply bounded by a warning against pursuing Troy. | Retain the first ship-fire, the complete arming order, the exclusion of Achilles’ Pelian spear, Automedon’s horse genealogy, and the five Myrmidon commanders. |
| 16.221–353 | The Myrmidon charge turns borrowed armor into a temporary social force. | Preserve the wolf and flood similes, the first battle sequence, Pyraechmes, and the reversal of the Trojan advance. |
| 16.354–507 | Sarpedon’s challenge establishes the moral burden of kingship before his death. | Keep Glaucus’s answer, the Lycian obligation, Zeus and Hera’s deliberation, Thrasymelus and Pedasus, Automedon’s repair, and Sarpedon’s final appeal. |
| 16.508–704 | The fight over Sarpedon’s body is both physical contest and divine custody. | Retain Epigeus, Bathycles, Aeneas, Meriones, Apollo’s washing and transfer, the cloud, and Patroclus’s pursuit catalogue. |
| 16.705–867 | Apollo’s repeated repulse, Euphorbus’s wound, and Hector’s spear divide responsibility for Patroclus’s death. | Preserve the Asius disguise, Cebriones and the diver image, the lion and forest similes, the disarming blow, Euphorbus’s first strike, Hector’s killing spear, and Patroclus’s prophecy. |

## Critical notes

- Beauty depends on the book’s central transformation: Achilles’s armor changes
  the army’s sight, but not the wearer’s fate. Let the music grow more radiant
  as the danger becomes more exact.
- The Longfellow-inspired register should give Sarpedon’s speech and Patroclus’s
  final words a grave, lucid amplitude, while Apollo’s interventions and the
  disarming blow need abrupt physical clarity.
- The meter report is only a syllable screen. A named human reader must still
  review stress, proper names, armor terminology, battle geography, and the
  breath of the long catalogues.
