## Revised translation pass

While Achilles and Patroclus spoke together, Ajax could no longer hold;
the missiles pressed him hard. The will of Zeus and the splendid Trojans
were wearing him down. His bright, terrible helmet rang around his temples
under the blows; the shafts struck continually upon its well-made bosses.
His left shoulder ached, yet he held his shining shield before him,
and they could not stagger him by leaning their missiles upon him.
Always he breathed with painful force; sweat poured from every limb,
and nowhere could he draw breath; one evil rested upon another.

“Tell me now, Muses who possess the Olympian halls,
how first the fire fell upon the Achaeans' ships.”

Hector stood near Ajax and struck his ash spear behind the blade,
where the bronze met the shaft, and severed it across.
Telamonian Ajax held only the useless stump in his hand;
far from him the bronze point fell, and rang upon the ground.
Ajax knew it in his blameless heart, and shuddered at the work of the gods:
high-thundering Zeus was cutting all the counsel of the battle,
for he desired victory for the Trojans.
Ajax withdrew from the missiles. The Trojans cast unwearying fire
upon the swift ship, and at once the flame spread over its stern.

Achilles struck his thighs and spoke to Patroclus:

“Rise, Patroclus, born of Zeus, driver of horses!
I see beside the ships the cry and breath of hostile fire.
Let them not take the vessels, so that no refuge remains for us.
Put on the armor quickly; I will gather the people.”

So he spoke. Patroclus clothed himself in the flashing bronze.
First he set the beautiful greaves around his legs,
fastened at the ankles with silver clasps.
Next he drew around his breast the starry, many-colored corselet
of swift-footed Aeacides. Across his shoulders he cast
the bronze sword, its hilt bright with silver, and then the great,
strong shield. Upon his powerful head he placed the well-made helmet,
horse-haired; above it the dreadful crest nodded.
He took two stout spears that fitted his hands.

But he did not take the spear of blameless Aeacides,
the heavy, great, and massive spear no other Achaean could brandish;
only Achilles knew how to wield the Pelian ash,
which Chiron had given to his dear father from the peak of Pelion,
to be the death of heroes.

Achilles ordered Automedon to yoke the horses,
the man he honored most after himself, the most faithful companion
to stand beside him in the shout of battle.
Automedon brought beneath the yoke the swift horses Xanthus and Balius,
who flew together upon the breath of the wind;
the Harpy Podarge had borne them to Zephyrus the West Wind,
while she grazed beside the stream of Ocean.
In the side trace he placed the blameless Pedasus,
whom Achilles had brought away when he sacked the city of Eetion;
though mortal, that horse kept pace with immortal horses.

Achilles went among the huts and armed the Myrmidons,
all of them with their weapons. They rushed like flesh-eating wolves,
whose hearts contain measureless strength after they have torn apart
a great antlered stag upon the mountains; their cheeks are red with blood,
and in a pack they go down to a black-water spring,
lapping the dark water with thin tongues, then belching out
the clot of blood. Their courage within their breasts is without fear,
and their bellies swell with the strength of the kill.
So the captains and leaders of the Myrmidons moved around
the brave companion of swift-footed Aeacides; and among them
stood warlike Achilles, urging the horses and the shield-bearing men.

Fifty swift ships belonged to Achilles, whom Zeus loved,
and carried him toward Troy. In every ship fifty companions
sat at the oars. He had appointed five leaders whom he trusted
to give the signs; he himself held the great command over all.

The first line was led by Menesthius, flashing in bronze,
son of the heavenly river Spercheius. Fair Polydora,
daughter of Peleus, had borne him to the tireless river
after lying with the god; yet Borus, son of Perieres,
was called his father, for he openly married her
and gave the immeasurable bride-price.

The second was led by Eudorus, warlike and unmarried,
whom beautiful Polymele, daughter of Phylas, bore after Hermes
saw her among the dancers, and loved her eyes while she danced
in the bright choir of Artemis, whose bow is golden.
Hermes, the gentle herald, climbed secretly to the upper chamber,
lay with her, and gave her a glorious son, Eudorus,
swift of foot and excellent in battle. When the child-bringing goddess
Eileithyia brought him into the light and he saw the sun,
Echecles, mighty son of Actor, led the mother to his house,
giving countless bride-gifts; but old Phylas reared the child,
nurturing him and loving him as his own son.

The third was led by warlike Peisander, son of Maemalus,
who surpassed all the Myrmidons in fighting with the spear
beside the companion of Peleus' son.
The fourth was led by old Phoenix, driver of horses;
the fifth by blameless Alcimedon, son of Laerces.

When Achilles had arranged them all with their leaders,
he gave them this strong command:

“Myrmidons, let none forget the threats you uttered
against the Trojans beside the swift ships,
while anger held me, and each of you accused me:
‘Cruel son of Peleus, your mother nourished you in gall;
pitiless, you keep unwilling companions beside the ships.
Let us return home upon the sea-bearing vessels,
since such a bitter anger has entered your heart.’
You spoke these things often when you gathered around me.
Now the great work of battle you once desired is manifest.
Let every man who carries a valiant heart fight the Trojans.”

So he stirred the courage and spirit of each man,
and the ranks drew closer when they heard their king.
As a man builds a wall with close-fitted stones
to keep the violence of the winds from a high house,
so helmets and bossed shields were joined together:
shield leaned against shield, helmet upon helmet,
and man upon man; the horse-haired crests touched
with their bright ridges as the warriors stood close.

Patroclus and Automedon, of one mind,
