## Revised translation pass

Poseidon was angered to the heart when his grandson's companion fell.
He went beside the huts and ships, rousing the Danaans and making grief
for the Trojans. Idomeneus, famous for the spear, met him as he came
from a companion who had just returned from battle, wounded beneath
the knee by sharp bronze. His friends had carried the man in;
Idomeneus had given him to the healers, yet still desired to meet the war.

The Earth-Shaker took the voice of Thoas, son of Andraemon,
who ruled all Pleuron and steep Calydon among the Aetolians,
and whom the people honored like a god. He called to Idomeneus:
“Idomeneus, counselor of the Cretans, where are now the threats
with which the sons of Achaea threatened the Trojans?”

The Cretan leader answered: “Thoas, as far as I know,
no man is to blame. We all know how to wage war.
No one is held back by helpless fear, nor does cowardice persuade
him to enter this evil battle. Perhaps it is the will of mighty Cronus'
son that the Achaeans perish here, nameless, far from Argos.
But you, Thoas—who were valiant before and rouse another
whenever you see him yielding—do not cease to urge each man.”

Poseidon answered in the borrowed voice: “Idomeneus, may that man
never return from Troy, but become food and music for the dogs,
who willingly abandons the fight today. Take your armor and come;
we must hurry, that we two may be of some help. Joined strength
is a virtue even among the wretched; and you and I know
how to fight among the brave.”

So the god returned into the labor of men. Idomeneus reached his hut,
put on his beautiful armor, and took his spears. He went out,
like a lightning-flash that the son of Cronus seizes and shakes
from shining Olympus as a sign to mortals, whose rays are unmistakable;
so the bronze flashed around Idomeneus as he ran.

Meriones, his excellent, swift-footed companion, met him near the hut;
he was going to bring a bronze spear. Idomeneus, strong in battle, asked:
“Meriones, son of Molus, dearest of my companions, why have you left
the war and come here? Are you wounded, and does the point of a missile
torment you, or have you brought me some message? I have no desire
to sit within the hut; I long to fight.”

The thoughtful Meriones answered: “Idomeneus, counselor of the bronze-clad
Cretans, I have come to see whether a spear remains in your hut
to carry with me. I broke the one I had when I struck the shield
of overbearing Deiphobus.”

Idomeneus replied: “If you wish, you will find one spear—or twenty-one—
standing bright against the wall of my hut, Trojan weapons stripped
from men I have killed. I do not believe in fighting my enemies
from a distance. Therefore I have spears, bossed shields, helmets,
and breastplates shining with fresh bronze.”

Meriones said: “I too have many Trojan spoils beside my hut and black ship,
but they are not near enough to take. I have not forgotten my courage;
I stand among the foremost whenever battle rises. Perhaps another
of the bronze-clad Achaeans does not know me fighting, but you know me.”

Idomeneus answered: “I know what courage is in you. Why tell me this?
If now all the best men were chosen for an ambush beside the ships,
there courage is most clearly seen, and the coward and the brave
are revealed. The coward's color turns from place to place;
his heart will not let him sit still, but he shifts from foot to foot,
his heart beats loudly in his breast as he imagines the fates,
and his teeth chatter. But the brave man's color does not change;
he is not greatly afraid when first he sits among the ambush.
He prays to mingle quickly in the grievous fight.

“There your courage and hands could not be overlooked.
Even if a missile struck or a spear reached you, it would not fall
behind, in neck or back, but meet you in breast or belly
as you went forward among the foremost. Come, let us no longer
stand speaking like children, lest someone rebuke us in our pride.
Go to the hut and take the mighty spear.”

Meriones, equal to swift Ares, quickly took the bronze spear
and went beside Idomeneus, intent upon the great work of war.
As murderous Ares goes into battle, and his dear son Phobos,
strong and fearless, follows him—he who terrifies the boldest warrior—
so the two went armored in burning bronze, like those two.

Meriones asked: “Son of Deucalion, where do you intend to plunge
into the crowd—on the right wing, in the middle, or on the left?
I do not expect the long-haired Achaeans to be so short of war.”

Idomeneus replied: “Others defend the middle ships:
the two Ajaxes, and Teucer, best of all Achaeans with the bow,
and good also in close battle. They will easily hold off Hector,
however eager and strong, and it will be a hard thing for him
to conquer their courage and invincible hands and set fire
to the swift ships, unless Cronus' son himself casts a burning torch
among the vessels. Great Telamonian Ajax would yield to no man,
if he were mortal and ate Demeter's grain, though bronze or huge stones
could break him. Even Achilles, breaker of ranks, could not surpass him
in close combat, though no one may match Achilles in running.
We two should hold the left of the army, so that quickly we may learn
whether we shall give another glory, or another give it to us.”

Meriones led the way until they reached the place appointed.
When the men saw Idomeneus, with his companion and elaborate armor,
they called through the crowd and all moved toward him.
The battle rose around the sterns of the ships like storms
that rush under shrill winds on a day when dust is greatest on the roads;
the gusts lift one cloud of dust from the earth.
So the fighting met, and their hearts were bent on killing one another
through the crowd with sharp bronze. The deathly war bristled
with long spears that cut the flesh; the bronze flashed from helmets,
new-polished breastplates, and bright shields, and no one could have
looked upon the labor without grief, unless his heart were made of stone.

The two sons of Cronus, thinking separately, prepared bitter pain
for the heroic men. Zeus desired victory for the Trojans and Hector,
honoring swift-footed Achilles; yet he did not wholly wish
the Achaean people to perish before Ilium—he wished to glorify
Thetis and her strong-hearted son. Poseidon, slipping secretly
from the gray sea, roused the Argives; he was angry to see them mastered
by the Trojans and deeply resented Zeus. They were of one blood
and shared one homeland, but Zeus had been born first and knew more.
Therefore Poseidon avoided helping them openly, yet always stirred
the army in secret, moving in the likeness of a man.

They stretched between the two armies the limit of fierce strife,
an unbreakable, unloosable cord that loosened the knees of many men.
There Idomeneus, though already grizzled, called to the Danaans,
and, springing among the Trojans, drove fear into them.

He killed Othryoneus, who had come newly to the war's fame from Cabesus.
He had asked Priam for Cassandra, the fairest of his daughters,
without bride-gifts, and promised a great deed: to drive the sons
of Achaea unwillingly from Troy. The old king had accepted and nodded,
and Othryoneus fought trusting in the promise.

Idomeneus aimed his shining spear at him and struck him in the chest.
As he fell, Idomeneus mocked him: “Othryoneus, will you now marry
the daughter of Priam? We will give her to you without bride-gifts,
if you will follow me home from the city. I too would promise you this,
if you had come to my ships and asked for the daughter of Agamemnon;
but you may now take her—here, beside the ships, the wedding is made.”

So he spoke, and sprang to strip the armor from his fallen enemy.
