## Revised translation pass

So speaking, he left her there and went to the bellows.
He turned them toward the fire and commanded them to labor.
Twenty bellows all together blew through their nozzles,
breathing forth every kind of breath for kindling;
now they rushed to aid him in his haste, now again fell quiet,
as Hephaestus willed and as his work required.

He cast unwearied bronze upon the fire,
and tin, and precious gold, and silver.
Then he set the great anvil firmly in its stand,
grasped the mighty hammer in one hand,
and in the other took the pincers.

First he made a shield, great and strong,
working its surface everywhere with cunning art.
Around it he laid a shining rim, triple and bright,
and fastened to it a silver strap.
The shield had five folds; and within its breadth
he wrought many things, with all the wisdom of his heart.

There he made the earth, and there the heavens,
and there the sea; the sun that never tires,
and the full moon; and there all the signs
with which the heavens are crowned:
the Pleiades, the Hyades, and the strength of Orion,
and the Bear, whom men also call the Wain,
who turns there in his place and watches Orion,
and alone has no share in the baths of Ocean.

Upon it he made two beautiful cities of mortal men.
In one were weddings and splendid feasts;
brides, beneath the blaze of torches, were led from their chambers
through the city, and the marriage-song rose loud.
Young dancers whirled; among them flutes and lyres
sent forth their music, and the women stood at their doors,
each one in wonder as she watched.

The people were gathered in the market-place.
There a quarrel had arisen: two men contended
over the blood-price for a man who had died.
One promised to pay the whole, declaring it before the people;
the other refused to accept anything.
Both desired to bring the matter to a judge for its ending.
The people shouted for each man in turn,
each side supporting its own; but heralds held them back.
The elders sat upon polished stones in the sacred circle,
holding in their hands the staffs of the clear-voiced heralds.
With these they rose and judged the case in turn.
In the midst lay two talents of gold,
to be given to the man among them
who should speak the straightest judgment.

Around the other city two armies of men sat,
shining in their armor. Their counsel was divided:
whether to sack the lovely city,
or to divide among themselves all the possessions
that the beloved city held within its walls.
But its defenders did not yet yield;
they went forth secretly to lie in ambush.
Their wives and dear children stood upon the wall to guard it,
and with them the men whom old age held back.
The hidden army went on; Ares and Pallas Athena led them,
both fashioned of gold and clad in golden raiment,
beautiful and great, with their armor upon them,
conspicuous above the smaller host of men.

When they came to the place beside the river
where all the herds had their watering,
they sat down there, wrapped in flashing bronze.
Two scouts were stationed apart from the army,
waiting for the moment when they should see
the sheep and the horned cattle.

Soon the herds appeared, and two herdsmen followed,
delighting in their pipes; they did not foresee the treachery.
The men who saw them first rushed upon them,
and quickly cut down the cattle and the fair flocks
of white-fleeced sheep, and killed the shepherds.

But the besiegers heard the great uproar among the cattle,
as the scouts sat before the camp.
At once they mounted their high-stepping horses
and rode out in pursuit; quickly they came upon the raiders.
They halted, and fought beside the river’s bank;
they cast at one another bronze-tipped spears.
There Strife was present, and Tumult, and deadly Death.
Death held one man newly wounded and alive,
another unwounded, and another already dead;
and she dragged another corpse by the feet through the tumult.
Her garment upon her shoulders was red
with the blood of men whom she had slain.
They mingled like living mortals and fought,
and drew away the bodies of one another’s dead.
