## Revised translation pass

Telemachus, wise in counsel, answered him:
“Antinous, no longer can I sit among you,
The overweening, and feast in silence, glad at heart.
Was it not enough that, as a child, I watched you shear
My many good possessions, suitors, while I knew no more?
But now that I am grown, and hear the speech of other men,
And my own spirit gathers strength within my breast,
I shall attempt to loose upon you the evil fates—
Whether I reach Pylos, or remain within this land.
I go; nor shall the journey of which I speak be vain,
Though I go as one who travels, not as master of a ship;
For I have no skill in ships or in the ordering of rowers—
So, surely, it seemed better to you than it seemed to me.”

He tore his hand with ease from the hand of Antinous.
The suitors, within the house, prepared their feast;
And some among the young men mocked him with their words.
One of the proud youths spoke thus:
“Indeed Telemachus is meditating slaughter for us.
Perhaps he will bring avengers from sandy Pylos,
Or from Lacedaemon, since his heart is set so fiercely;
Or perhaps he means to go to Ephyra, rich in soil,
To bring from there the death-destroying poisons,
And cast them in the mixing-bowl, and destroy us all.”

Another of the proud youths replied:
“Who knows whether, going himself in a hollow ship,
He may perish far from those he loves, wandering like Odysseus?
Then he would increase our labor still more;
For we should divide among ourselves all his possessions,
And give his house to his mother and to the man who weds her.”

So they spoke. But Telemachus went down to the lofty chamber
Of his father, broad and high-roofed, where worked gold and bronze
Lay stored, and garments folded in the chests, and fragrant oil;
And where, set in a row against the wall, stood jars of old wine,
Sweet to the taste, holding within them the unmixed, godlike drink,
Reserved for the day when Odysseus should return to his home,
Having endured much sorrow and escaped his many dooms.
The doors were shut with close-fitting boards, double-folded;
And within, a woman steward watched by night and day,
Guarding all things with a mind rich in knowledge:
Eurycleia, daughter of Ops, son of Peisenor.

Telemachus called her to the chamber and addressed her:
“Nurse, come, draw for me sweet wine into the two-handled jars—
The sweetest after that which thou guardest here,
Keeping it for the hapless man, if ever, somewhere,
Godlike Odysseus should come home, escaping death and fate.
Fill twelve jars, and set the lids securely upon them all;
And pour barley meal into well-sewn leather bags—
Let there be twenty measures of meal, ground from the grain.
Thou alone must know; let all these things be gathered together.
In the evening I shall take them, when my mother has gone
Up to the upper chamber and has turned her thoughts toward sleep.
For I go to Sparta and to sandy Pylos,
To seek some tidings of my dear father, if I may hear them.”

So he spoke; and his beloved nurse Eurycleia wailed aloud,
And, lamenting, uttered winged words:
“Why, dear child, has such a thought arisen in thy heart?
And where dost thou desire to go, alone, across so much land,
Being thy mother’s only beloved? Godlike Odysseus has perished,
Far from his homeland, among a people of another speech.
And these men will plot evil behind thee as thou goest,
That by deceit they may destroy thee and divide these things
Among themselves. Stay here, seated beside thy possessions;
It is not fitting for thee to suffer evils upon the unharvested sea,
Nor to wander.”

Telemachus, wise in counsel, answered her:
“Take courage, nurse, for this design is not without a god.
But swear to me that thou wilt not speak these things
To my dear mother, before the eleventh and twelfth day come—
