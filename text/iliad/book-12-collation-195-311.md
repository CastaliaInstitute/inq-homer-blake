## Revised translation pass

While these men stripped the flashing armor from the fallen,
the sons who followed Polydamas and Hector—the greatest and best,
most eager to break the wall and set fire to the ships—stood still,
uncertain beside the trench. Then a bird appeared to them:
an eagle flying high upon the left, keeping the people back,
and carrying in its talons a huge, red, writhing serpent still alive.
It had not forgotten the battle; twisting backward, it struck
the bird beneath the breast beside the throat. The eagle writhed,
hurting beneath its pain, and let the serpent fall among the ranks;
then, crying, it flew away upon the breath of the wind.

The Trojans shuddered when they saw the gleaming snake
lying amid them, a portent of Zeus who bears the aegis.
Then bold Polydamas stood beside Hector and spoke:
“Hector, you rebuke me in the assembly whenever I advise
what is best; you say that a common man must not speak beyond
the council or the battlefield, and that your own power
must always be increased. Now I will tell you what seems best:
let us not go to fight beside the ships. For so, I think,
the sign has come to the Trojans who desired to cross:
the eagle flew high on the left, restraining the people,
and carried the huge, red serpent alive; then dropped it
before it could reach its home and give it to its children.

“So we, though we break through the gates and wall of the Achaeans
by force, shall not return by the same road in order to bring
our companions back; many will be left behind beneath the ships.
If some prophet, standing among us, should say this sign aloud,
we should not disobey him; the army would believe the word.
But the hawk's meaning is plain to me: we shall force the wall,
yet shall not all come home from beside the ships.”

The helmet-shining Hector looked darkly at him and answered:
“Polydamas, your words are no longer pleasing to me.
You know how to devise another speech better than this;
but if you speak these things in earnest, then the gods themselves
have taken away your mind. You would have me forget the will
of Zeus, the loud-thundering one, the will he promised me
and sealed with a nod. You tell me to obey long-winged birds;
I do not turn nor care whether they fly toward the dawn
and the sun, or toward the misty darkness on the left.
Let us obey the purpose of great Zeus, who rules
over mortal men and deathless gods alike.

“There is one omen best of all: to fight for one's fatherland.
Why do you fear battle and the work of death? Even if all the rest
of us should fall beside the Argive ships, you have no fear
of being killed; your heart is neither warlike nor steadfast.
But if you hold back from the fighting, or by your words
turn another man away from battle, then at once, struck beneath
my spear, you shall lose the life within you.”

So he spoke, and led them on; the others followed
with a wondrous clamor. Zeus who delights in thunder
raised from the mountains of Ida a storm of wind,
which drove the dust straight toward the ships,
bewildered the minds of the Achaeans, and gave glory
to the Trojans and to Hector. Trusting in this sign
and in their strength, they tried to break the great wall.

They tore away the battlements of the towers and threw down
the parapets; they pried up the projecting pillars
which the Achaeans had first planted in the earth
as the towers' defenses. The Trojans dragged these out,
hoping to break the Achaean wall. Yet the Danaans did not yield;
they fenced the parapets with ox-hide shields and hurled
from them at the enemy who came beneath the wall.

Both Ajaxes moved everywhere upon the towers, calling orders
and rousing the Achaean strength. One they addressed with gentle
words, another with hard rebuke, whenever they saw a man
wholly abandoning the battle: “Friends of Argos—whether you are
the foremost, the middle, or the weaker—this work is for all.
You know yourselves that men are not equal in war.
Let no one turn back toward the ships when he hears our cry;
press forward, call courage into one another, and perhaps Zeus,
the thunder-bearing Olympian, will grant us to drive the enemy
away from our walls and pursue them to the city.”

Thus the two called out and urged the battle. Stones flew
from both sides like thick snowflakes falling on a winter day,
when Zeus has risen to bring snow to mortals, stilling the winds
and pouring it down until it covers the mountain peaks,
the headlands, the lotus-bearing plains, and the rich works of men;
it lies upon the gray sea, the harbors, and the beaches,
and the wave that strikes the shore holds it there—
so thickly did the stones fly on both sides, and the whole wall
roared beneath their fall.

Even then the Trojans and bright Hector could not have broken
the gates and long bar of the wall, if Zeus the counselor
had not driven his own son Sarpedon against the Argives,
like a lion turned upon the cattle. At once Sarpedon held
before him his rounded shield, beautiful and beaten from bronze;
a smith had hammered it, then sewn within it many layers
of ox-hide, fastened in a continuous circle with golden rods.
Holding it before him, and brandishing two spears,
he advanced like a mountain lion, when hunger for flesh
has long consumed him and his fearless heart commands him
to test the sheep and enter the close-built fold.

For even if he finds shepherds there with dogs and spears
guarding the sheep, the lion is not unpracticed in the assault:
he either springs and carries off his prey, or is himself struck
among the foremost by a spear cast from a swift hand.
So the heart of godlike Sarpedon urged him to rush upon the wall
and tear away its battlements. At once he called to Glaucus,
the son of Hippolochus:

“Glaucus, why are you and I honored above all others in Lycia,
by seat, by portions of meat, and by the fuller cups of wine?
All men look upon us there as if we were gods, and we possess
a great estate beside the banks of Xanthus—beautiful orchards
and wheat-bearing fields. Therefore we must stand among the first
and meet the burning battle, so that a Lycian in his close armor
may say: ‘Our kings do not rule Lycia without glory;
they eat the fat sheep and drink the honeyed, excellent wine.
But their strength is also noble, since they fight among the first.’

“Dear friend, if, by fleeing this war, we could forever be
without age and death, I would neither fight in the foremost
nor send you into the battle where men win glory. But now
the countless fates of death stand near; no mortal can escape
or evade them. Come: either we shall give another his glory,
or another shall give it to us.”

So he spoke, and Glaucus neither turned away nor refused him.
Together they went straight on, leading the great Lycian host.

## Decision record

| Greek range | Decision | Reason / remaining check |
|---|---|---|
| 12.195–209 | Keep the eagle's leftward flight, living serpent, wound, and dropped prey in one causal sequence. | The omen depends on the eagle's failure to carry the serpent home; do not flatten it into “a hawk drops a snake.” |
| 12.210–229 | Give Polydamas a public, reasoned interpretation rather than a vague warning. | His argument predicts victory without safe return; the omitted homecoming distinction is central. |
| 12.230–250 | Preserve Hector's “one omen” aphorism and his threat to Polydamas. | Hector rejects bird-signs while claiming Zeus' direct promise; the contradiction is dramatic, not a translation error. |
| 12.251–289 | Retain Zeus' dust-storm, the dismantling of wall elements, the Ajaxes' differentiated exhortations, and the snow simile. | The Greek moves from divine atmospheric pressure to hand-to-hand engineering and communal resistance. |
| 12.290–311 | Restore Sarpedon's shield construction, lion simile, and Lycia speech opening. | The working draft jumped directly to the assault; the social obligation of kingship motivates Sarpedon's action. |
