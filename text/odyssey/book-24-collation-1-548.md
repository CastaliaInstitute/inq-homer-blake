## Revised translation pass

### Lines 1–98 — The suitors’ shades

Hermes, the Guide and Slayer of Argus, summoned the souls of the suitors.
With his golden wand he charmed the eyes of men he wished and woke others,
leading the shades along the dank roads. They passed Ocean’s streams, the
white rock, the Gates of the Sun, and the people of dreams, and came swiftly
to the meadow of asphodel where the souls of the dead dwell. There they found
Achilles, Patroclus, Antilochus, and Ajax, the fairest and strongest among
the Danaans after Achilles.

Agamemnon’s grieving shade approached, with the souls of those who died
with him in Aegisthus’s house. Achilles asked why so many young men had died:
had Poseidon drowned them with winds and waves, or had hostile men killed
them on land while they cut cattle, sheep, a city, or women? Amphimedon,
Agamemnon’s guest-friend in Ithaca, answered that the suitors had met their
fate in Odysseus’s house.

### Lines 99–202 — Amphimedon’s account and Agamemnon’s praise

Agamemnon remembered having visited Amphimedon’s house to urge Odysseus
to sail with Menelaus toward Troy. Amphimedon told the whole truth of their
death. They wooed Odysseus’s wife; Penelope neither refused the marriage nor
completed it, but devised another design. She set up a great, fine web and
said: “Wait for my marriage until I finish this shroud for the hero Laertes,
lest the Achaean women blame me when death takes him before his possessions
have been buried.”

By day she wove the web and by night undid it beside the torches. For three
years she concealed the ruse and persuaded the Achaeans; in the fourth,
a woman who knew the truth betrayed her, and Penelope was forced to complete
the cloth. Then a god brought Odysseus to the farm at the city’s edge in the
likeness of a miserable, old beggar. No one recognized him; the suitors mocked
and struck him until Zeus awakened his purpose. With Telemachus he hid the
weapons, and when the bow came to his hands, he strung it, shot through the
axes, and killed the suitors. They all perished, their bodies still unwashed
in the house.

Agamemnon praised Odysseus: “Fortunate son of Laertes, you won a wife of
excellent mind. How well Penelope remembered her husband! Her fame will never
die; the immortals will give her a song among mortals. Not like the daughter
of Tyndareus, who killed her lawful husband and earned a hateful song and a
hard reputation for women, even for the good.”

### Lines 203–286 — Odysseus and Laertes

Odysseus reached Laertes’ well-made farm, bought long before by the old man.
There was a house, a cattle-yard, and servants who worked for him; a Sicilian
old woman cared for him in the country, away from the city. Odysseus told
Telemachus and the two herdsmen to prepare the best pig for dinner while he
tested whether his father would recognize him.

He found Laertes alone in the orchard, digging around a tree. The old man
wore a filthy patched tunic, sewn cowhide leggings, and gloves against the
thorns, with a goat-skin cap upon his head, increasing his grief. Odysseus
wept to see him and considered embracing him at once or first questioning him.
He chose to test his father and said that the orchard was well tended, but
that age and sorrow had withered the man who worked it. He asked whose servant
he was and whose orchard he tended, and whether the land truly was Ithaca.

Laertes answered that the country was Ithaca and that he served a man who,
he had been told, had come home after many years. He wished that Odysseus had
returned before the suitors consumed his wealth. Odysseus said he was that son,
returned in the twentieth year, and that the suitors had paid for their wrongs.
Laertes doubted him. Odysseus showed the scar made by the boar and named the
trees Laertes had given him as a child: thirteen pear trees, ten apple trees,
forty fig trees, and fifty vine-rows, promised when the seasons should ripen.

Laertes’s knees and heart gave way; he clasped the earth and prayed to Zeus.
Odysseus embraced and kissed his father, saying that he had killed the suitors.
Laertes asked for a sign of victory. Odysseus showed the scar, repeated the
orchard inventory, and displayed the clothes and bronze taken from the dead.
Laertes stood like an immortal, and the old Sicilian woman washed and clothed
him; Athena made him larger and fairer to behold.

### Lines 287–371 — Dolius and the household armed

The old man and his son went to the house. Dolius and his sons came in from
the orchard, weary from their work, summoned by the Sicilian woman. Laertes
told them that Odysseus had returned. Dolius embraced and kissed him, and
the sons gathered around the king. Odysseus asked whether they would stand
with him if the suitors’ families came to fight. Dolius answered that they
had nowhere else to go and would follow him. They armed themselves with bronze.

### Lines 372–464 — The families seek blood

The news reached the suitors’ families. They carried the dead away, lamenting,
and buried them. They gathered in the market, grieving for their sons and
brothers. Eupeithes, father of Antinous, spoke first, consumed by grief:
Odysseus had gone to Troy and lost all his companions; now he had destroyed
the best young men of Ithaca. Let them follow and take vengeance before he
escaped overseas.

Medon and the inspired singer Phemius came from the house and stood before
the assembly. Medon said that Odysseus had not acted without divine support:
he had seen a god beside him, sometimes like Mentor. Halitherses, aged and
wise, confirmed that the suitors had brought this upon themselves by refusing
to stop their violence. Half the people demanded war; the other half remained
silent, unwilling to follow Eupeithes.

Eupeithes and his party marched toward Laertes’s farm. The gods gathered in
Zeus’s house. Athena asked whether Odysseus’s quarrel should continue or peace
should be made. Zeus answered that Odysseus should rule, but that the people
must forget the slaughter, love one another as before, and let peace and wealth
return. Athena went down in the likeness of Mentor.

### Lines 465–548 — Eupeithes and the peace

Odysseus and his companions armed themselves. Laertes prayed to Athena, and
the goddess gave him strength and courage. The two sides met at the farm.
Eupeithes raised his spear, and Laertes struck him between the eyes; his helmet
and armor rang as he fell. Odysseus and Telemachus rushed forward and would
have pursued the rest, killing them with sword and spear, but Zeus hurled
a smoking thunderbolt before Athena’s feet.

Athena cried to Odysseus: “Hold, and end the quarrel of equal war, lest wide-
ruling Zeus grow angry.” Odysseus obeyed. The Ithacans dropped their weapons
and fled toward the city, terrified. Zeus thundered again. Athena, in Mentor’s
form, established oaths of friendship between both sides. Thus the gods made
the peace, and Odysseus ruled again in Ithaca.

## Decision log

- The book retains Hermes’s golden wand, the asphodel meadow, and the named
  heroic shades before Amphimedon’s account.
- Agamemnon’s praise distinguishes Penelope’s fidelity from Clytemnestra’s
  murder, and Amphimedon’s account retains the web, the disguised arrival,
  the hidden weapons, and the bow.
- Laertes’s recognition depends on the scar, the orchard inventory, and the
  returned objects; Dolius and his sons remain part of the armed household.
- The political aftermath preserves Medon, Phemius, Halitherses, Eupeithes,
  Athena’s question, Zeus’s settlement, Laertes’s spear, and the final oaths.
- This draft requires independent Greek-fidelity review, literary review,
  meter review, notes review, and production sign-off before promotion beyond
  `draft`.
