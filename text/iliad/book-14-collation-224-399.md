## Revised translation pass

Aphrodite went to the house of Zeus, while Hera, darting from Olympus,
crossed Pieria and lovely Emathia and sped over the snowy peaks
of Thracian horsemen; her feet did not touch the ground.
From Athos she crossed the surging sea and reached Lemnos,
the city of divine Thoas. There she met Sleep, brother of Death,
took his hand, and addressed him:

“Sleep, lord of all gods and all men, if ever you heard my word,
hear me now. I shall be grateful to you all my days.
Put the shining eyes of Zeus to sleep at once, after I lie beside him.
I will give you a beautiful golden throne, imperishable forever;
my son Hephaestus will make it, and set a footstool beneath it,
that you may rest your shining feet when you feast.”

Sweet Sleep replied: “August Hera, daughter of mighty Cronus,
I could easily lull another of the ever-living gods,
even Oceanus, the river from whom all things are born.
But I would not come near Zeus, nor put him to sleep,
unless he himself commanded it. Your other order already taught me fear:
on the day when that overbearing son of Zeus sailed from Ilium,
after destroying the city of the Trojans, I wrapped the aegis-bearing
Zeus in gentle sleep; you meanwhile devised harm in your heart:
you raised harsh winds over the sea and carried him away
to Cos, far from all his friends. When he woke, he raged,
flinging the gods throughout the house and seeking me above all;
he would have cast me unseen from heaven into the sea,
if Night, subduer of gods and men, had not saved me.
I fled to her, and he ceased, angry though he was,
for he feared to do anything displeasing to swift Night.
Now you ask me to do another impossible thing.”

Ox-eyed, august Hera answered: “Sleep, why do you turn these things over
in your mind? Do you think wide-seeing Zeus aids the Trojans
as he once aided them in anger for his son Heracles?
Come: I will give you one of the younger Graces to marry,
and she shall be called your wife.”

Sleep rejoiced and answered: “Swear now by the inviolable water of Styx.
With one hand take the nourishing earth, with the other the shining sea,
that all the gods beneath the earth who are called Titans
may be witnesses between us: swear to give me Pasithea,
one of the younger Graces, whom I have desired all my days.”

Hera did not refuse. She swore as he commanded and named
all the gods below, the ones called Titans. When she had sworn
and completed the oath, the two left Lemnos and Imbros,
clothed in mist, and swiftly made their way. They reached Ida,
rich in springs, mother of beasts, at Lecton, where first they left the sea;
then they walked upon the land, and the tops of the forest trembled
beneath their feet. There Sleep remained, before Zeus could see him;
he climbed a long fir, the tallest on Ida, reaching through the clear air,
and sat among its thick branches like a shrill bird the gods call Chalcis,
but men call Cypindis.

Hera swiftly climbed the peak of Ida, Gargaron, and the cloud-gatherer Zeus
saw her. When he saw her, desire covered his prudent mind,
as on that first day when they joined in love and went to bed,
concealing themselves from their dear parents. Zeus stood before her:
“Hera, why have you come here so eagerly from Olympus?
Your horses and chariot are not here for you to ride.”

Hera, thinking deceitfully, replied: “I go to visit the ends
of the nourishing earth, Oceanus, father of gods, and mother Tethys,
who raised me and cared for me in their house, after Rhea entrusted me
to them when Zeus set Cronus beneath the earth and barren sea.
I go to visit them and settle their endless quarrel;
they have long kept apart from bed and love since anger entered them.
Their horses stand at the far ridge of many-springed Ida,
and will carry me over the nourishing, wet earth.
For your sake I have come down from Olympus, lest you be angry with me
if I depart in silence for the house of deep-flowing Oceanus.”

Cloud-gathering Zeus answered: “Hera, there will be time to go there later;
but now let us turn to love and lie together. Never before has desire
for goddess or woman so poured over my breast and mastered me—
not when I loved Ixion's wife, who bore Pirithous,
wise as the gods; nor Danaë, daughter of Acrisius,
who bore Perseus, most renowned among men; nor the daughter
of far-famed Phoenix, who bore Minos and godlike Rhadamanthys;
not Semele in Thebes, nor Alcmene, who bore strong-hearted Heracles;
not Semele, who bore Dionysus, joy to mortals;
not Demeter, queen of lovely locks; nor glorious Leto;
not even you. As I desire you now, so sweet longing takes me.”

Hera, plotting, answered: “Most terrible son of Cronus,
what word have you spoken? If you desire to lie together here
on Ida's peaks, where everything is visible, how would it be
if some ever-living god saw us sleeping and told all the gods?
I could not return to your house after rising from the bed;
it would be a thing of shame. But if you wish it and it is dear to you,
there is the chamber your dear son Hephaestus made,
with close-set doors fastened to their posts. Let us go there,
since the bed is pleasing to you.”

Cloud-gathering Zeus answered: “Hera, do not fear any god or man
will see us. I will cover us with a golden cloud so dense
that the Sun himself, whose light is sharpest for seeing,
could not look through it.”

The son of Cronus caught his wife in his arms. Beneath them
the divine earth put forth fresh grass, lotus wet with dew,
crocus, and hyacinth, dense and soft, lifting them above the ground.
There they lay, and drew a beautiful golden cloud around themselves;
shining dewdrops fell from it. So the father slept quietly on Gargaron,
conquered by sleep and love, holding his wife in his arms.

Sweet Sleep ran to the Achaean ships to carry the message
to Poseidon, lord of earth, and standing near him spoke:
“Now, Poseidon, help the Danaans with a willing heart,
and grant them glory for a little while, while Zeus still sleeps;
I have covered him with soft sleep, and Hera has deceived him
into lying with her.”

Having spoken, Sleep went away among the famous races of men.
Poseidon urged the Danaans even more strongly. He sprang among
the foremost and cried: “Argives, shall we now yield victory to Hector,
Priam's son, so that he may take the ships and win glory?
He says and boasts that Achilles remains beside his hollow ships,
angry in his heart; but we shall not miss him greatly,
if we rouse one another to defend ourselves.

“Come, obey me: put on the finest and largest shields in the army,
cover your heads with the brightest helmets, take the longest spears,
and go. I will lead you, and I do not think Hector, however eager,
will stand before us. Let a man who has a small shield give it
to a weaker man and enter beneath the larger shield.”

They heard and obeyed. Their wounded kings—Diomedes, Odysseus,
and Agamemnon—ordered them, changing the armor: the brave man wore
the good armor, the weaker man received the lesser. When they had put
the shining bronze around their bodies, they went forward;
Poseidon led them, holding in his great hand a terrible sword,
long and sharp, like a flash of lightning. It is not lawful to mingle
with that sword in grievous battle; it holds men back through fear.
On the other side bright Hector ordered the Trojans.

Then blue-haired Poseidon and bright Hector stretched a dreadful
contest of war between the two hosts, one helping Trojans,
the other the Argives. The sea stirred toward the huts and ships
of the Achaeans, and the armies came together with a tremendous cry.
No wave raised from the sea by a bitter north wind roars so loudly
against the land; no fire's voice in a forest burns so fiercely;
no wind cries so terribly among the high-branched oaks,
when it roars in anger; as loud was the cry of Trojans and Achaeans.
