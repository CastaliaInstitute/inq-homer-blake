## Revised translation pass

The Trojans withdrew in turn from the fierce battle,
unyoked their swift horses beneath the chariots,
and gathered in council before they took their evening meal.
They stood upright; no man dared sit down,
for trembling held them all, since Achilles had appeared,
after a long absence from the grievous fight.

Prudent Polydamas, son of Panthous, began to speak;
he alone could see before him and behind.
He was Hector’s companion, born with him in one night,
but one prevailed greatly in speech, the other by the spear.
With good intent he addressed the assembly:

“Consider carefully, friends. I urge us now to go into the city,
and not to await bright dawn here upon the plain beside the ships,
far from the wall. While this man was angry with Agamemnon,
the Achaeans were easier to fight; I myself was glad to sleep
beside the swift ships, hoping to take the curved vessels.
But now I greatly fear swift-footed Peleus’s son.
His spirit is overmastering; he will not consent to remain
upon the plain, where Trojans and Achaeans divide between them
the strength of Ares. He will fight for the city and for the women.

“Come, let us go into the city, and believe me.
This is how it will be: tonight the immortal night
has stopped swift-footed Peleus’s son; but if he finds us here
tomorrow, roused with his armor, one among us will know him well.
Gladly will he reach holy Ilium who escapes;
many Trojans shall the dogs and vultures eat—would that such a word
might pass from my ear into the deed!

“But if we heed my counsel, though our hearts are grieving,
we shall hold our strength in the assembly through the night;
and the towers, the high gates, and the long, well-fitted doors
shall be drawn shut within the city.
At dawn we shall arm ourselves and take our stand upon the towers.
Then it will be harder for him, if he wishes to come from the ships
and fight around our wall. He will return again to the ships
after tiring his high-necked horses by driving them beneath the city;
his spirit will not allow him to rush within,
nor will he ever take it—before that the white dogs shall eat him.”

Crest-bright Hector looked darkly at him and answered:

“Polydamas, these words are no longer pleasing to me,
when you command us to wander back into the city.
Have you not yet had enough of being penned within the towers?
Before this, all mortal men used to call Priam’s city
rich in gold and bronze; but now the beautiful treasures of our houses
are utterly lost, and many possessions have gone for sale
to Phrygia and lovely Maeonia, since great Zeus has grown angry.

“Now, when the son of Cronus, of crooked counsel,
has granted me glory to win beside the ships
and drive the Achaeans into the sea,
do not, fool, bring such thoughts before the people.
No Trojan will obey you, nor will I permit it.
Come, let us all obey as I command.
Now take your evening meal throughout the camp,
remember the watch, and let each man keep awake.
Let any Trojan who is overly proud of his possessions,
and troubles the people, gather them and give them out
for the common feast; it is better that one of our own
should enjoy them than the Achaeans.

“At dawn let us arm ourselves and awaken sharp Ares
beside the hollow ships. If indeed the godlike Achilles
has risen beside the vessels, it will be worse for him,
if he wishes it so; but I shall not flee from the bitter battle.
I will stand face to face, whether he carries off great strength,
or whether I carry it away. Enyalios is common to all:
the killer may himself be killed.”

Thus Hector spoke, and the Trojans shouted in approval,
poor fools; for Pallas Athena had taken their wits away.
They praised Hector, who counseled evil,
and not one remembered the excellent counsel of Polydamas.

Then they took their evening meal throughout the camp.
But the Achaeans all night long mourned Patroclus with lamentation.
The son of Peleus led the loud mourning,
laying his man-killing hands upon his companion’s breast,
and groaning again and again like a bearded lion
whose cubs a man who hunts deer has snatched from the thick wood.
The lion comes too late, grieving; he searches many ravines
in the man’s tracks, hoping somewhere to find him,
for bitter anger seizes him.

So, groaning heavily, Achilles spoke among the Myrmidons:

“Ah me! I spoke a vain word on that day,
when I encouraged the hero Menoetius in his halls,
promising to bring his glorious son back to Opous
after I had sacked Ilium, and to give him his portion of the spoil.
But Zeus does not fulfill every thought for men.
For both of us it is fated to stain the same earth here in Troy;
nor shall old horse-driving Peleus receive me again
returning home, nor my mother Thetis;
the earth herself will hold me here.

“Now, Patroclus, since I shall go beneath the earth after you,
I will not give you burial until I have brought here
the armor and the head of Hector, your great-hearted slayer.
And before your pyre I will cut the throats of twelve
glorious children of Troy, enraged because you are dead.
Until then you shall lie here beside the curved ships,
while around you Trojan and deep-bosomed Dardanian women
shall mourn, shedding tears, through the night and the day—
women whose rich cities we ourselves took by force
with our strength and our long spear.”

So speaking, divine Achilles commanded his companions
to set a great tripod upon the fire,
that they might wash Patroclus quickly
of the blood-stained mortal dust.
They set the bath-tripod in the fire,
poured water into it, and kindled wood beneath.
The fire surrounded the belly of the tripod,
and the water grew warm in the shining bronze.
When the water boiled within the polished vessel,
they washed the body and anointed it richly with oil;
they filled the wounds with ointment aged nine years.
Then they laid him upon the bier and covered him
with a soft linen cloth from head to foot,
and above it with a white sheet.

All night the Myrmidons mourned Patroclus with lamentation
around swift-footed Achilles.

Then Zeus spoke to ox-eyed Hera, his sister and his wife:

“You have accomplished your purpose, ox-eyed, honored Hera:
you have roused swift-footed Achilles. Surely the long-haired Achaeans
were born from you yourself.”
