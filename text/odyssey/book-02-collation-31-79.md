## Revised translation pass

“Some message from the army returning, which he might tell us
When he had first heard it? Or does he declare some other public need?
He seems a good man, who will profit us. May Zeus accomplish
The purpose he has conceived within his heart.”

So he spoke; and the dear son of Odysseus rejoiced at the omen.
He did not sit long; he desired to speak. He stood in the middle
Of the assembly, and herald Peisenor, wise in counsel,
Placed the scepter in his hand. First he turned to the old man:

“Old man, that man is not far away; soon thou shalt know it thyself—
The man who gathered the people, and whose grief has reached me most.
I have heard no message of an army returning,
Which I might tell you after learning it first;
Nor do I announce another public matter.
It is my own need—two evils have fallen upon my house.
First, I have lost my excellent father, who once ruled among you,
And was gentle as a father. Now a much greater evil has come,
Which soon will utterly destroy my house and consume its livelihood.

“The suitors press upon my mother against her will—
The sons of the men who are here, and who are said to be the best.
They shrink from going to the great house of Icarius,
That he himself might dower his daughter and give her to whomever
He wished, the man who came most pleasing to him.
Instead they come here every day, and sacrifice oxen,
Sheep, and fattened goats, feasting and drinking the bright wine
Without restraint. Much of our livelihood is consumed;
For there is no man here such as Odysseus was, to ward the ruin
From our house. We are not such men as he was to defend it;
I would defend it, if only strength were available to me.

“But now deeds have been done that can no longer be endured;
My house is no longer kept in proper order. Be ashamed yourselves,
And stand in awe before the people dwelling round about;
Fear the anger of the gods, lest, indignant at these deeds,
They turn them back upon you. I beseech you by Olympian Zeus,
And by Themis, who dissolves and gathers the assemblies of men:
Hold back, my friends, and leave me alone to be worn by bitter grief—
Unless perhaps my noble father Odysseus did some evil to the bronze-greaved
Achaeans, and you, hostile to me, do these things as repayment,
Urging them on. Yet it would be more profitable for me
If you yourselves consumed my possessions and my goods.
If you ate them, perhaps one day there might be repayment;
Then we would go through the city, demanding our property in words,
Until everything had been returned. But now you cast
Unpaid griefs into my heart.”

## Decision log

- Preserve the scepter’s formal transfer from Peisenor to Telemachus and the
  young speaker’s movement into the middle of the assembly.
- Keep Telemachus’ “two evils” distinct: the loss of his father and the imminent
  destruction of his house and livelihood through the suitors’ daily feasting.
- Retain the suitors’ refusal to return to Icarius for a proper marriage
  arrangement, preserving the political and familial dimensions of *oikos*.
- Preserve the sacrificial inventory—oxen, sheep, fattened goats, and bright wine—
  as a material account of consumption rather than a generic accusation of waste.
- Keep Telemachus’ admission that he lacks Odysseus’ defensive strength, while
  allowing the conditional claim that he would act if he possessed that power.
- Retain the appeals to shame, neighbors, gods, Zeus, and Themis, and the
  counterfactual repayment clause that exposes the suitors’ expropriation.
