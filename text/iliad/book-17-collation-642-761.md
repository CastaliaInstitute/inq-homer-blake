## Revised translation pass

—the grievous message, that his dear companion has perished.
“But nowhere can I see such a man among the Achaeans;
for they themselves and their horses are held alike in mist.
Father Zeus, deliver the sons of the Achaeans from this darkness;
make the clear air return, and grant our eyes to see;
then slay us in the light, if such is your pleasure.”

So he spoke; and the father pitied him, shedding tears.
At once he scattered the mist and thrust the cloud away;
the sun shone forth, and all the battle stood revealed.

Then Ajax, good in the war-cry, spoke to Menelaus:

“Look now, Menelaus, child of Zeus, and see
whether Antilochus, the great-hearted son of Nestor,
still lives. Urge him to go quickly to Achilles,
the warrior, and tell him that his dearest companion has fallen.”

Menelaus, good in the war-cry, did not refuse.
He went like a lion from the fold, when, weary
from rousing dogs and men who will not let him take
the fat of the cattle, though they watch all night,
he presses forward hungry for flesh, yet achieves nothing;
for dense spears leap against him from bold hands,
and the burning brands which they hurl he fears,
though he rushes on. At dawn he goes away,
his heart wounded by the thwarting.
So Menelaus went unwillingly away from Patroclus;
for he feared that the Achaeans, overcome by the grievous flight,
would leave him as prey for the enemy.
He gave many commands to Meriones and the two Ajaxes:

“Ajax, leaders of the Argives, and Meriones,
let each of you now remember the kindness of poor Patroclus;
for he was gentle to all while he lived,
but now death and fate have found him.”

Thus he spoke and departed, golden-haired Menelaus,
looking everywhere like an eagle, said to have the keenest sight
among the birds beneath the sky; even when it is high above,
the swift-footed hare lying beneath a leafy bush
does not escape its notice; it swoops upon him,
quickly seizes him, and takes away his life.
So then your bright eyes, Menelaus, child of Zeus,
turned everywhere through the many ranks of companions,
to see if anywhere he might find Nestor’s son still living.

Very quickly he saw him on the left wing of all the battle,
encouraging his companions and urging them to fight.
Menelaus came close and spoke:

“Antilochus, child of Zeus, come here, that you may learn
the grievous news that should never have come to pass.
I think you yourself, looking on, already know
what evil the god rolls upon the Danaans,
and that the victory belongs to the Trojans.
Patroclus, best of the Achaeans, has been killed,
and a great longing has fallen upon the Danaans.
But run at once to Achilles beside the Achaean ships,
and tell him whether he may quickly save the naked body
to the ship; Hector of the shining helmet holds the armor.”

Antilochus was struck with horror as he heard the words.
For a long time speechlessness seized him;
his eyes filled with tears, and his clear voice was held back.
Yet he did not neglect Menelaus’s command.
He ran, and gave his armor to the blameless companion
Laodocus, who was turning the single-hoofed horses near him.
With tears falling, his feet carried him from the battle
to Peleus’s son Achilles, bearing the evil message.

Nor was Menelaus’s heart willing to defend his weary companions
from the place where Antilochus departed;
but he sent noble Thrasymedes to them,
while he himself returned again to the hero Patroclus.
He stood beside the Ajaxes, and spoke at once:

“I have sent that man to the swift ships,
to go to swift-footed Achilles; but I do not think
that he will go, though he is terribly angered at noble Hector;
for he could not fight the Trojans naked.
Let us ourselves devise the best counsel,
how we may drag away the corpse, and how we may escape
death and fate among the Trojans’ uproar.”

The great Telamonian Ajax answered him:

“You have spoken all things in due measure, glorious Menelaus.
But you and Meriones must stoop down quickly,
lift the corpse, and carry it out of the struggle;
and we two behind you will fight Trojans and godlike Hector,
holding one equal spirit, as we have always done,
standing together and enduring sharp Ares.”

So he spoke; and they bent down and lifted the corpse
high from the ground. Behind them the Trojan host cried out,
when they saw the Achaeans raising the dead.
They rushed forward like dogs that spring upon a wounded boar
before the young hunters; for a time they run,
eager to tear him apart, but when he turns among them,
trusting in his strength, they draw back and scatter,
each in a different direction, trembling.
So the Trojans followed them in a mass,
stabbing with swords and two-edged spears;
but when the two Ajaxes turned and stood against them,
their color changed, and no one dared to spring forward
and contend over the corpse.

Thus the two bore the dead man with all their strength
from the battle toward the hollow ships;
and war stretched behind them, fierce as fire
that, suddenly rushing upon a city of men,
blazes high while houses shrink in the great glare,
and the force of the wind roars over it.
So the unwearied uproar of horses and spear-bearing men
followed them as they moved.

As mules, gathering their strong strength,
drag from the mountain down a rugged path
either a great beam or a long ship’s mast,
while heart and body are worn together by haste,
by labor and by sweat—so these men, straining forward,
bore the corpse. Behind them the two Ajaxes held the Trojans back,
as a wooded ridge holds back water above a plain,
a ridge that checks even the strong streams of rivers,
turning all their flow across the fields;
the rushing waters cannot break it by their strength.
So the two Ajaxes always drove the battle backward.
The Trojans followed, and above all Aeneas, son of Anchises,
and radiant Hector.

As a cloud of starlings or jackdaws comes on,
clamoring dreadfully when they see a hawk approaching,
who brings slaughter to the little birds—so beneath Aeneas
and Hector the sons of the Achaeans went on with a dreadful cry,
forgetting their courage.
Many beautiful pieces of armor fell around the corpse
and around the ditch, as the Danaans fled;
and there was no slackening of the war.
