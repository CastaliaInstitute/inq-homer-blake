## Revised translation pass

“Remember the children and wives, the lands and fathers
of those who live, and those who have gone below the earth;
for their sake I kneel before you, though they are not here.
Stand fast with strength, and do not turn your faces into fear.”

So he spoke, and stirred the courage in every breast. Athena swept
the wondrous mist from their eyes; on either side there rose a light,
both toward the ships and toward the battle where men met.
They saw Hector, good at the war-cry, and his companions,
those who had stood behind and would not fight, and those who fought
beside the swift ships. Nor did great-hearted Ajax any longer find it
pleasing to remain where the other Achaeans had drawn back.

He paced the long decks of the vessels, stepping far,
and wielded in his hands a great sea-fighting spear,
joined with rings, two-and-twenty cubits long.

As a horseman, skilled in guiding horses, joins four together,
and drives them from the plain toward the city on the public road—
many men and women marvel as he leaps securely from one to another,
while they fly beneath him—so Ajax moved across the many ships,
striding their long decks; and his voice reached the heavens.
Without ceasing he shouted terribly, commanding the Danaans
to defend their ships and huts. Nor did Hector remain within
the close-packed tumult of the bronze-clad Trojans;
but as a bright eagle falls upon a flock of birds beside a river,
geese, cranes, or long-necked swans feeding in the meadow,
so Hector sprang straight toward a dark-prowed ship.
Zeus drove him on with a mighty hand, and roused the people with him.

Again a sharp battle rose beside the ships. You might have thought
that men who could not tire and could not fail were meeting one another,
so eagerly they fought. Their minds were set upon this end:
the Achaeans said they would not flee from evil, but perish;
and every Trojan heart hoped to burn the ships
and kill the Achaean heroes.

With that purpose they stood against one another. Hector seized
the stern of a seafaring ship, beautiful and swift,
the ship that had carried Protesilaus to Troy
and never brought him back to his native land.
Around that vessel Achaeans and Trojans slaughtered one another
at close quarters. They no longer kept apart for arrows or spears,
but stood near with one mind and fought with sharp axes,
hatchets, great swords, and spears with two edges.
Many handsome, black-hilted swords fell from the hands
of men who fought; others slipped from their shoulders;
and the black earth ran with blood.

When Hector had seized the stern he did not let it go.
Holding the curved stern-post in his hands, he called to the Trojans:

“Bring fire, and rise together with one cry!
Now Zeus has given us a day worth all our prayers:
to take the ships which came here against the will of the gods,
and laid so many sorrows upon us through the folly of our elders.
They restrained me when I desired to fight beside the sterns,
and held the people back. But if wide-seeing Zeus then darkened
our minds, he now himself urges us and commands us onward.”

So he spoke, and they rushed more fiercely upon the Argives.
Ajax could no longer hold his place; the missiles pressed him hard.
He withdrew a little, believing that he would die upon the deck,
seven feet in length, and left the level timbers of the ship.

There he stood, watching, and with his spear continually drove
from the ships any Trojan who carried the unwearying fire.
Always he shouted terribly to the Danaans:

“Dear friends, Danaan heroes, servants of Ares,
be men, my friends, and remember your furious strength!
Do we imagine there are helpers somewhere behind us,
or a stronger wall that might ward destruction from men?
There is no city here, built with towers,
where we might defend ourselves, holding another people at our back.
We lie in the plain of the Trojans, close-packed in bronze,
with our faces toward the sea and far from our fatherland.
Our light is in our own hands, not in a gentle kind of war.”

He spoke, and in his fury pursued them with his sharpened spear.
Any Trojan who carried blazing fire toward the hollow ships,
encouraged by Hector's command, Ajax waited for and struck
with his long spear; twelve men he wounded before the ships,
each in close encounter.
