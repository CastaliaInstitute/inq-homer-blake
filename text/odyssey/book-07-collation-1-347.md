## Revised translation pass

### Lines 1–13 — Nausicaa returns

So there the much-enduring, godlike man
Prayed in the grove; and Nausicaa drove
The mules toward the city. When she reached
Her father’s famous house, she stopped before
The doors; her brothers, like immortal gods,
Stood round the wagon, loosed the patient mules,
And carried in the garments. She herself
Went to her chamber; there the old nurse lit
The fire, Eurymedusa, who had once
Been brought from Apeira by the curved ships,
And given as a royal gift to serve
Alcinous, whose people heard his word
As they might hear a god. She had raised
Nausicaa in the palace, and for her
Lit fire and set the evening meal within.

### Lines 14–42 — The hidden road

Then Odysseus rose and went toward the town.
Athena, favoring him, poured a mist
Around him, lest some proud Phaeacian,
Meeting him first, should mock him with harsh words
Or ask his name. As he approached the lovely
City, the bright-eyed goddess met him there,
In the likeness of a young girl, bearing
A water-jar. She stood before him; he said:

“My child, will you conduct me to the house
Of King Alcinous, who rules these people?
I am a stranger, long afflicted, come
From far away across the sea. I know
No one who holds this city and this land.”

The goddess answered: “Stranger, father-like,
I will show you the house you ask to see.
It stands near my own father’s honored home.
Follow in silence; I will lead the way.
Look toward no man, and ask no question on
The road. This people does not gladly bear
The stranger, nor receive with open love
The man who comes from elsewhere. They trust the ships
That cross the mighty sea, swift as a wing
Or as a thought; Earth-Shaker gave them that.
Their vessels are the fastest in the world.”

She spoke, and went before him quickly; he
Kept close behind the goddess’ unseen steps.
The famous sailors did not see them pass
Among the streets; Athena, beautiful
And terrible, had cast a sacred mist
About him in the kindness of her heart.

### Lines 43–77 — The palace’s order

Odysseus marveled at their harbor, ships,
And meeting-place, and at the long, high wall
Built firmly with sharp stakes, a wonder to behold.
When they had reached the king’s renowned abode,
The goddess spoke:

“This is the house, stranger, that you asked me for.
You will find the god-fed kings within,
At supper. Enter; let no fear possess
Your heart. A man who carries courage through
His work is better in all things, even though
He comes from far away. First you will find
The queen within the hall. Her name is Arete;
She is of the same parents who begot
Alcinous, king. Poseidon first begot
Nausithous with Periboea, fairest
Of women, youngest daughter of the proud
Eurymedon, who ruled the great Giants.
He ruined his rebellious people and died;
Poseidon joined with her, and she bore him
Great-hearted Nausithous, king of the land.
Nausithous fathered Rhexenor and
Alcinous. Apollo of the silver bow
Struck Rhexenor down, newly wed, and left
One daughter, Arete. Alcinous
Made her his wife, and honors her beyond
All women dwelling under mortal men.

“She is most honored by her children, him,
The king, and by the people, who look up
To her as to a god, and greet her through
The city when she walks. Her good sense fails
Her nowhere; with wise counsel she dissolves
The quarrels even of the men. If she
Receives you kindly in her heart, you may
Hope to see your friends, and reach your high-roofed
House and the land that gave you birth.”

So spoke Athena, and departed over
The sea that never tires. She left fair Scheria,
And came to Marathon and broad Athens,
Then entered Erechtheus’ strong house. But he,
Odysseus, went toward the palace, deeply
Considering, before he crossed the bronze
Threshold. The house shone like the sun or moon.

### Lines 78–132 — The Phaeacian house

The bronze walls ran from threshold into shadow;
A dark-blue cornice crowned them. Golden doors
Held fast the inner house; the doorposts stood
In silver set upon a bronze threshold,
And silver was the lintel, gold the handle.
On either side stood golden dogs and silver,
Crafted by Hephaestus’ knowing mind to guard
Alcinous’ great house—deathless, ageless,
Forever young. Along the walls were thrones,
From threshold through the hall, and on them lay
Fine woven cloths, the work of women’s hands.
The leaders of the Phaeacians sat there,
Eating and drinking; theirs was an unfailing
Feast. Golden youths upon well-built pedestals
Held torches in their hands, to shine through night
Upon the banqueters. The king’s house had
Fifty serving women: some ground the grain,
Some sat at looms and twisted shining thread,
Their hands moving like leaves of the tall poplar.
From the freshly washed linen oil fell,
As skilled in woven work are these Phaeacians
Beyond all other men, and Athena gave
The women beautiful craft and noble minds.

Outside the courtyard, close beside the doors,
Was a great garden, four-square, enclosed on both
Its sides. Tall trees grew there, in flower and fruit:
Pear trees and pomegranates, apple trees,
Sweet figs, and olives in their full abundance.
Their fruit does not decay nor fail in winter,
Nor in the heat of summer, but endures
Through every season. The west wind brings one fruit
To birth and ripens another; pear on pear,
Apple on apple, grape upon the grape,
And fig upon the fig. One part lies open,
Dried by the sun; elsewhere they gather grapes,
And elsewhere tread them; before the bunches, some
Are green and shedding blossom, others darken.
At the far edge, arranged beds of every kind
Shine all the year. Two springs run there: one spreads
Throughout the garden; the other passes under
The courtyard threshold toward the lofty house,
And from it all the citizens drew water.
Such were the bright gifts of the gods within
The house of King Alcinous.

Odysseus stood and gazed, long-suffering,
At all these things; then crossed the threshold swiftly.
He found the leaders pouring wine in honor
Of Hermes, guide of men, whose final cup
They offered whenever they prepared for sleep.

### Lines 133–176 — The stranger’s supplication

He passed through the hall beneath the mist
Athena had poured round him, until he reached
Arete and the king. Around the queen
Odysseus cast his hands, and at that moment
The sacred mist fell back. The people there
Were silent, seeing a man within the house;
They marveled. Then he prayed:

“Arete, daughter of godlike Rhexenor,
To your knees and your husband’s I have come,
After great suffering, and to these guests.
May the gods grant them life and happiness,
And may each leave his treasures to his sons,
And all the honors given by the people.
But send me swiftly to my native land;
For long I have been tormented far away
From those I love.”

So speaking, he sat down beside the hearth,
In the ashes. All the people held their peace.
At last old Echeneus, a hero, spoke,
The oldest of the Phaeacians, skilled
In speech, and rich in knowledge of the past:

“Alcinous, this is neither fair nor right:
The stranger sits upon the ash beside
The hearth, while all these people wait in silence
For your command. Rise, raise the stranger up,
And seat him on a silver-studded chair;
Tell the heralds to mix the wine, that we
May pour for Zeus, delighting in the thunder,
Who follows reverent suppliants; and tell
The steward to set supper before the guest,
From what is in the house.”

When sacred Alcinous heard, he took
The much-devising hero by the hand,
Raised him from beside the hearth, and seated him
In the bright chair from which his dear son rose,
Laodamas, who sat beside his father
And was the king’s most cherished child. A maid
Brought water in a beautiful gold jug
Above a silver basin, so that he
Might wash his hands; she drew a polished table
Near him. The honored steward brought him bread
And many dishes, giving freely from
The stores at hand. Odysseus drank and ate.

### Lines 177–225 — A promise of passage

Then Alcinous spoke to the herald:
“Pontonoous, mix the bowl, and deal the wine
To all within the house, that we may pour
To Zeus the thunderer, who follows close
Upon the reverent suppliant.”

Pontonoous mixed the honey-sweetened wine,
And served it, beginning with the first cup. When
They had poured and drunk as much as each desired,
Alcinous addressed the leaders:

“Hear me, Phaeacian leaders and commanders.
Now go home and sleep after the meal. At dawn
We will call the elders in greater number,
Entertain our guest within the hall, and make
Beautiful offerings to the gods; then think
Of his conveyance, that our guest may reach
His native land without distress or pain,
Escorted by us, however far away.
Let no misfortune touch him on the road
Before he sets his foot upon his country;
Thereafter he will suffer what the thread
Of fate and the grave spinners fixed for him
When his mother bore him. If he is some god
Who has come down from heaven, the gods themselves
Are shaping something new; for they have always
Appeared among us openly, when we make
Splendid hecatombs, and feast beside us.
And if one of us meets them on the road,
They do not hide themselves; we are close to them,
As close as Cyclopes and the wild tribes
Of Giants.”

But wise Odysseus answered: “Let such thoughts
Be far from you, Alcinous. I am not
Like the immortals who possess the wide sky,
In form or body; I am mortal, like
The men you know who bear the heaviest grief.
I could tell greater sorrows still, all those
I suffered by the will of heaven. But let
Me eat, though grief is in me. Nothing is
More shameless than the hateful belly, which
Commands a man to remember food, however
Worn down and sorrowful he may be. So I
Have sorrow in my heart, yet it commands
That I should eat and drink, and makes me forget
The things I suffered. At sunrise urge your men,
That I, unhappy, may set foot again
Upon my native land, though much has come to me;
And when I see my possessions, servants,
And high-roofed house, let death come then.”

All praised his words, and ordered that the guest
Be sent away, since he had spoken rightly.
After they poured and drank their fill, the people
Went to their homes; godlike Odysseus stayed
Within the hall, with Arete and the king,
While servants cleared away the feast.

### Lines 226–297 — Arete’s question and the sea’s account

Arete, white-armed, began the questioning,
For she knew the shirt and cloak upon him—beautiful
Garments she herself had made with her attendants:

“Stranger, I will ask you first: who are you,
And from what land? Who gave you these garments?
Did you not say that you had wandered here
Across the sea?”

Odysseus answered: “Queen, it is difficult
To tell the whole of sorrow; the gods of heaven
Have given me too much. Yet I will tell
The thing you ask. Far out upon the sea
Lies Ogygia, where Calypso lives,
The cunning daughter of great Atlas, dread
And beautiful; no god or mortal man
Keeps company with her. A spirit brought
Me, lonely, to her hearth, after Zeus struck
My ship apart with a white thunderbolt
In the middle of the wine-dark sea.
All my good comrades died; I clung to the keel
Of the curved ship, and drifted nine full days.
On the tenth dark night the gods brought me
To Ogygia, where Calypso took me in,
Loved me, fed me, and promised I should live
Immortal and unaging forever. Yet
She never persuaded the heart within my breast.

“For seven years I stayed there without change,
And soaked with tears the deathless garments she
Had given me. When the eighth year came around,
She urged me to depart, whether a message
From Zeus compelled her or her mind had turned.
She sent me from the island on a raft
Bound fast with many ropes; she gave me bread,
Sweet wine, and shining immortal clothes, and sent
A gentle wind behind me. For seventeen days
I crossed the sea; on the eighteenth appeared
The shadowed mountains of your land, and my
Heart rejoiced, though I was still to meet
The sorrow Poseidon raised, Earth-Shaker, who
Stirred winds against me, closed the sea-road, and
Allowed no wave to bear me gently onward.

“The storm broke the raft apart. I swam across
The gulf until wind and water carried me
To your land. A wave drove me against the rocks,
But I drew back and swam again, until I came
Upon a river, where the place seemed best:
Smooth from the stones, and sheltered from the wind.
I fell there, exhausted; immortal night
Came on, and I climbed beyond the heaven-fed river,
And slept in the thicket, drawing
The leaves around me. A god poured sleep on me.
There I slept all night, and through the dawn, and
Into the middle day; then the sun declined,
And sweet sleep left me. I saw your daughter’s maids
Playing upon the shore, and she among them,
Like one of the goddesses. I begged her help.
She did not lack good sense, as you might hope
The young would when they meet a stranger. She gave
Me food and bright wine, washed me in the river,
And gave me these clothes. Though I grieve, I have
Told you the truth.”

### Lines 298–347 — The guest’s bed

Alcinous answered him: “Stranger, my daughter
Did not show all the judgment she should have shown,
For she did not lead you to our house with all
Her maids, though you first came to her in prayer.”

But wise Odysseus answered: “Do not blame
Your blameless child. She did tell me to follow
With her attendants; I would not, for I feared
And felt ashamed that you, seeing us together,
Might grow angry. The peoples of this earth
Are jealous in their hearts.”

Then Alcinous answered: “Stranger, my heart
Is not one that grows angry without cause.
All better things are fitting. May Zeus, father,
Athena, and Apollo grant that you,
Being as you are, and thinking as I do,
Might take my daughter’s hand, and be my son
By marriage, staying here. I would provide
A house and wealth, if you would stay by choice;
No Phaeacian would compel you. May this
Not please our father Zeus. Tomorrow I will
Make certain of your passage, so that while
You sleep the men may row you in a calm sea
Until you reach your land and house, wherever
It lies—even beyond Euboea, which our men
Call farthest of all lands. They once conveyed
Fair Rhadamanthys there to see Tityus,
Earth-born; they went and came home the selfsame day,
Without fatigue. You will learn how swift my ships
And young men are upon the sea.”

Odysseus rejoiced, and prayed: “Father Zeus,
May every word Alcinous has spoken
Be brought to pass. Then my fame on the fruitful
Earth would never die, and I would reach my home.”

Arete ordered the women to set
A bed beneath the portico, and lay
Fine purple blankets over it, with rugs
Above, and woolen cloaks to cover him.
The women went out bearing torches. When they
Had made the close-woven bed, they called him:

“Rise now, stranger; your bed is ready.”

It seemed a welcome thing to sleep. The much-
Enduring Odysseus lay beneath the roof,
Upon the pierced bed beneath the echoing hall.
Alcinous slept within the house’s heart,
And the queen prepared the bed and resting-place.

## Decision log

- Correct the working-book’s inherited conflation with Book 6: Book 7 opens with Nausicaa’s return and moves directly to Athena’s mist-guided escort.
- Preserve the Phaeacian genealogy and the extraordinary status of Arete; her judgment is not decorative but a condition of Odysseus’ return.
- Treat the palace catalogue as social infrastructure: crafted guardians, servants, textile labor, orchards, and water all make hospitality materially possible.
- Keep Odysseus’ supplication at the hearth and in the ashes, followed by Echeneus’ civic correction and Alcinous’ public welcome.
- Preserve the tension between Alcinous’ expansive offer of kinship and Odysseus’ insistence that eating and departure come before narration.
- Correct the chronology and measurements of Odysseus’ account: nine days clinging to the keel, seven years with Calypso, seventeen days at sea, the eighteenth-day mountains, and the river shelter.
- Keep Arete’s clothing recognition as the question that opens the retrospective narrative, and end with the promised bed beneath the portico rather than importing the games and Demodocus from Book 8.
