## Revised translation pass

Prudent Telemachus answered him:
“Eurymachus, and all you splendid suitors, I shall no longer
Entreat you or speak of these things; the gods know them now,
And all the Achaeans know. But give me a swift ship and twenty
Companions, who can make the road for me in either direction.
I will go to Sparta and sandy Pylos, to inquire about my father’s
Return, long delayed—whether some mortal may tell me of it,
Or whether I may hear from Zeus a voice that carries fame
Most greatly among men.

“If I hear that my father lives and is coming home,
Then, though worn down, I shall endure for one more year.
But if I hear that he is dead and is no longer among men,
I shall return to my beloved country, raise his mound,
Give him abundant funeral gifts, all that is fitting,
And give my mother to another husband.”

So he spoke, and sat down. Then Mentor rose among them,
The companion of blameless Odysseus, to whom Odysseus,
Going away in the ships, entrusted his whole house:
To obey the old man and guard everything securely.
With wise intent Mentor addressed the people:

“Hear me now, Ithacans, while I say what I must say.
May no scepter-bearing king hereafter be gentle,
Kind, and mindful of what is right; may every king be harsh
And do iniquitous things, since no one remembers godlike Odysseus,
Whom he ruled as a father, gentle among his people.

“I do not so much begrudge the proud suitors their violent deeds,
Their evil counsels; for they set their own heads at risk
And devour the house of Odysseus by force,
Saying that he shall never return.
But I blame the rest of the people, who sit here all in silence,
And do not rebuke with words or stop a few suitors,
Though you are many.”

## Decision log

- Preserve Telemachus’ withdrawal from persuasion—“the gods and all Achaeans
  know”—before he states the practical request for a ship and twenty companions.
- Retain the Pylos/Sparta itinerary, the one-year endurance condition, and the
  burial and remarriage alternatives as a coherent plan rather than a prophecy.
- Keep Mentor’s relationship to Odysseus exact: Odysseus entrusted him with the
  whole house, obedience to the old man, and secure guardianship.
- Preserve Mentor’s bitter inversion of kingship: the absent father was gentle,
  while a future king might be harsh and unjust because the people have forgotten.
- Keep the contrast between the suitors’ self-endangering violence and the
  larger civic blame placed on the silent many who fail to rebuke the few.
- End at Mentor’s rebuke on line 241; Leocritus’ refusal begins at line 242.
