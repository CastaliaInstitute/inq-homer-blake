## Revised translation pass

But when Patroclus, equal to a god, rushed upon the wall a fourth time,
Apollo cried out to him in terrible, winged words:

“Draw back, Patroclus, born of Zeus. It is not your portion
to sack the city of the proud Trojans with your spear,
nor even Achilles', who is far better than you.”

He spoke; and Patroclus drew far back,
shunning the anger of Apollo the Far-Shooter.

Hector held his one-hoofed horses at the Scaean Gate;
he wondered whether to drive again into the tumult of battle,
or call the people back within the wall.
While he pondered, Phoebus Apollo came beside him,
made in the likeness of a strong and youthful man,
Asius, the maternal uncle of horse-taming Hector,
full brother of Hecabe, son of Dymas,
who dwelt in Phrygia beside the streams of Sangarius.
In his likeness the son of Zeus spoke to Hector:

“Hector, why have you ceased from battle? It is not fitting.
Would that I were as much weaker than you as I might be stronger:
then I should soon make you turn away from hateful war.
But come, drive your strong-hoofed horses against Patroclus;
perhaps you may seize him, and Apollo grant you the glory.”

So he spoke, and the god returned into the labor of men.
Bright Hector ordered wise Cebriones to lash the horses into battle.
Apollo entered the multitude and cast confusion among the Argives,
but gave glory to the Trojans and to Hector.
Hector let the other Danaans be, nor stripped their armor;
he drove his strong-hoofed horses straight against Patroclus.

From the other side Patroclus leaped down from his horses,
holding his spear in his left hand. In the other he took
a sharp, glittering stone that covered his hand,
and hurled it without delay; his cast was not in vain.
He struck Cebriones, the bastard son of renowned Priam,
who held the reins before the horses, in the brow with the stone.
The stone crushed both his eyebrows together;
the bone did not hold, and his eyes fell into the dust
before his feet. Like a diver he fell from the well-made chariot,
and his spirit left his bones.

Patroclus mocked him as he rode:

“How lightly the man has leaped! He turns a neat somersault.
If only he were somewhere in the fish-filled sea,
this man could satisfy many bellies, diving from a ship
to search for oysters, though the sea were rough;
so lightly he has tumbled now from his horses in the plain.
There are divers among the Trojans too.”

So he spoke, and sprang upon Cebriones like a lion
that ravages the folds, is wounded in the chest,
and its own courage destroys it; so upon Cebriones,
Patroclus, you sprang with eager heart.
Hector leaped from the horses on the other side.

The two fought around Cebriones like lions
that on a mountain's summit, hungry and high in spirit,
fight over a slain deer; so around Cebriones
Patroclus, son of Menoetius, and radiant Hector,
masters of the battle-cry, rushed to cut one another's flesh
with pitiless bronze.
Hector seized Cebriones by the head and did not release him;
Patroclus held him by the foot. The other Trojans and Danaans
joined battle around them, and the war grew strong.

As East Wind and South Wind contend together in the mountain valleys,
shaking the deep forest of oak, ash, and smooth-barked cornel,
when the long branches strike one another with a terrible noise
and the broken boughs crash—so Trojans and Achaeans,
leaping upon each other, slaughtered one another,
and neither remembered the ruinous fear.

Many sharp spears and feathered arrows, leaping from the bowstrings,
were fixed around Cebriones; many great stones battered shields
as the warriors fought over him. He lay large in the whirl of dust,
forgetful of his horsemanship.

While the sun stood high in the middle of heaven,
the missiles of both sides reached their mark,
and the people fell. But when the sun turned toward the hour of unyoking,
then, beyond what was fated, the Achaeans were stronger.
They dragged the hero Cebriones away from the weapons and the Trojans,
and stripped the armor from his shoulders.

Patroclus rushed upon the Trojans with evil in his heart.
Three times he sprang upon them, equal to swift Ares,
shouting terribly; three times nine men he killed.
But when he rushed a fourth time, equal to a god,
then, Patroclus, the end of your life appeared.

Phoebus came against you in the fierce battle,
terrible; but Patroclus did not see him as he came,
for the god met him wrapped in thick mist.
He stood behind him and struck his broad back
with the flat of his hand; his eyes spun around.
From his head Apollo knocked the helmet;
it rolled beneath the feet of the horses with a ringing sound,
and its horsehair was stained with blood and dust.
Before that it was not permitted that the horse-haired helmet
should be fouled with dust, for it guarded the head and fair brow
of godlike Achilles; but now Zeus gave it to Hector to wear,
though the day of his own destruction was near.

All along Patroclus' hands the long-shadowed spear was shattered,
heavy, great, and crested; from his shoulders the shield fell,
with its strap, down upon the ground.
The lord, Apollo, son of Zeus, loosened the corselet from him.
Delusion seized his mind, and his shining limbs were loosened;
he stood stunned. Behind him, between his shoulders,
a Dardanian man struck him with his sharp spear:
Euphorbus, son of Panthous, who surpassed his generation
with spear, horsemanship, and swift feet.
He had first come with his chariot and brought down twenty men
when he was learning the ways of war.
He was the first to cast at you, Patroclus, horseman,
but he did not overcome you. He ran back into the crowd,
having pulled the ash spear from your flesh;
he did not stand to face you, naked though you were in the battle.

Patroclus, struck by a god's blow and by the spear,
withdrew among his companions, shunning his fate.
When Hector saw great-hearted Patroclus draw back,
wounded by the sharp bronze, he came close through the ranks
and struck him with his spear in the lower belly;
the bronze passed through. Patroclus fell with a crash,
and deep sorrow seized the Achaeans.

As when a lion overcomes a tireless boar,
and both, high in the mountain, fight around a little spring,
each desiring to drink; at length the lion by force
subdues the boar, panting hard—so Hector, son of Priam,
near his spear, took the life from the brave son of Menoetius,
who had killed many men, and spoke over him in triumph:

“Patroclus, surely you said that you would ravage our city,
take the free day from the Trojan women,
and carry them in your ships to your dear fatherland—fool!
Before them Hector's swift horses have stretched their feet
to battle. I myself, with the spear, excel among the Trojans
who love the war, and defend their necessary day.
But vultures shall eat you here.

“Poor man, Achilles was no help to you,
though he gave you many commands as you went:
‘Do not come back to the hollow ships, driver of horses,
until you have stripped the bloody tunic from the breast
of man-killing Hector.’ So he spoke, I think,
and your foolish mind obeyed him.”

With failing strength Patroclus, horseman, answered:

“Now boast greatly, Hector; Zeus, son of Cronus,
and Apollo have given you the victory,
who subdued me easily; they themselves stripped the armor
from my shoulders. If twenty men like you had met me,
all would have perished here beneath my spear.
But deadly fate and Leto's son have killed me,
and among men Euphorbus; you are the third who strips me.

“And I tell you this—lay it within your heart:
you yourself shall not live long, but death and mighty fate
already stand near you, and you shall fall beneath the hands
of blameless Achilles, son of Aeacus.”

So he spoke; and the end of death covered him.
His spirit flew from his limbs and went to Hades,
lamenting its fate, leaving its manhood and its youth.

Even when he was dead, radiant Hector addressed him:

“Why do you prophesy my steep destruction, Patroclus?
Who knows whether Achilles, son of fair-haired Thetis,
will not first be struck beneath my spear,
and lose his spirit?”

So he spoke, and planted his foot upon him,
and drew the bronze spear from the wound,
then thrust him away from the spear upon his back.
At once he went after Automedon with the spear,
the godlike companion of swift-footed Aeacides,
for he desired to strike him; but the immortal horses,
beautiful gifts that the gods had given Peleus,
carried Automedon away.
