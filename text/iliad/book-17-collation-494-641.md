## Revised translation pass

With Hector came Chromius and godlike Aretus also;
both pressed upon the team, and their hearts greatly hoped
to kill the two men and drive away the high-necked horses.
Fools! They were not destined to return from Automedon unblooded.

Automedon prayed to father Zeus, and his dark heart filled
with courage and strength. At once he spoke to faithful Alcimedon:

“Alcimedon, do not hold the horses far from me;
let them breathe upon your back. I do not think
that Hector, son of Priam, will check his fury
until he has mounted the fair-maned horses of Achilles,
and killed us two, throwing the Argive ranks into flight—
unless he himself should be taken among the first.”

So speaking, he called to the two Ajaxes and Menelaus:

“Ajax, leaders of the Argives, and Menelaus,
leave the dead to those who stand about him,
the best men, to defend him from the ranks;
but guard us two, while we ward off the pitiless day.
For Hector and Aeneas, the best of the Trojans,
have made the tearful battle fierce against us.
Yet all these things lie upon the knees of the gods;
I too shall cast my spear, and Zeus will care for the rest.”

He spoke, and hurled his long-shadowed spear.
It struck Aretus’s even shield; the shield did not hold it,
and the bronze drove through beneath the belt,
into the lower belly. As when a young man with a sharp axe,
striking behind the horns of a field-fed ox,
cuts through the sinew, and the beast springs forward
and falls upon its back—so Aretus sprang and fell backward;
the spear, quivering deep in his belly, loosed his limbs.

Hector cast his shining spear at Automedon;
but Automedon saw it coming and avoided the bronze.
He bent forward, and the long spear passed behind him,
then sank into the ground, while its butt-end quivered;
there the mighty violence of war was spent.
And now the two would have rushed upon each other
with swords at close quarters, had not the two Ajaxes,
answering the call of their companion, come through the crowd.
Hector, Aeneas, and godlike Chromius recoiled before them,
leaving Aretus there, his heart torn out.

Then Automedon, equal to swift Ares, stripped the fallen man
of his armor, and spoke in triumph:

“Now, surely, I have lightened a little the grief
for Menoetius’s dead son, though I have slain one man beneath him.”

He lifted the blood-stained spoil into the chariot,
then mounted, his feet and hands above it,
bloodied as a lion that has fed upon a bull.

Again the strong and grievous, tearful battle broke
around Patroclus; and Athena descended from heaven
to awaken the strife, for Zeus, whose voice is wide,
had sent her to rouse the Danaans; his mind had changed.
As when Zeus stretches a purple rainbow across the sky,
a sign to mortal men of war or of the bitter storm
that makes men cease from their labor upon the earth
and afflicts the sheep—so she wrapped herself in a purple cloud,
entered the Achaean host, and stirred each man.

First she urged Menelaus, son of Atreus, mighty in battle;
she took the form and tireless voice of Phoenix:

“Menelaus, shame and reproach shall be yours
if the swift dogs of Troy drag beneath their wall
the faithful companion of noble Achilles.
Stand fast with all your strength, and stir the whole army.”

The strong-voiced Menelaus answered:

“Old father Phoenix, ancient in your years,
if only Athena would grant me strength,
and turn aside the rush of the weapons!
Then I would gladly stand beside Patroclus and defend him;
for the dead man has struck my spirit deeply.
But Hector holds a terrible fire of courage,
and never ceases to destroy with bronze;
for Zeus grants him the glory.”

So he spoke; and the bright-eyed goddess rejoiced,
because he had asked her first of all the gods.
She put strength into his shoulders and his knees,
and into his breast the courage of a fly—
that fly which, though driven from a man’s skin,
will not cease to bite, for human blood is sweet to it.
With such courage she filled his dark heart;
he went up to Patroclus and cast his shining spear.

Among the Trojans was Podes, son of Eetion,
rich and brave; Hector honored him above the people,
for he was his dear companion at the feast.
Golden-haired Menelaus struck him beneath the belt
as he rushed away, and the bronze passed through.
He fell with a crash; but Menelaus dragged the body
from the Trojans into the ranks of his companions.

Apollo stood near Hector and urged him on,
made like Phaenops, son of Asius,
who of all Hector’s guest-friends was dearest,
living in Abydos within his house.
In his likeness the Far-Shooter spoke:

“Hector, who among the Achaeans would fear you now,
when you have yielded to Menelaus—he who before
was but a soft-handed spearman? Now he goes alone,
carrying the dead away from the Trojans,
and has killed your faithful companion, Podes,
son of Eetion, a brave man among the foremost.”

So he spoke; and a black cloud of grief covered Hector.
He went through the first ranks, armed in flashing bronze.
Then the son of Cronus took up his tasselled, shining aegis;
he hid Ida beneath the clouds, and thundered greatly,
flashing and shaking it; he granted victory to the Trojans
and put the Achaeans to flight.

First the Boeotian Peneleus began the flight.
He had been struck in the shoulder, always facing forward,
by the spear of Polydamas, who came close and cast it;
the spear grazed the bone beneath his flesh.
Hector wounded Leitus, son of great-hearted Alectryon,
near the wrist, and stopped his fighting.
He looked about and fled, for he no longer hoped
to fight the Trojans with a spear in his hand.

As Hector charged after Leitus, Idomeneus struck him
upon the corselet, in the breast beside the nipple;
the long spear broke at the socket, and the Trojans cried aloud.
Hector cast his own spear at Idomeneus, son of Deucalion,
who stood upon his chariot, but missed him by a little.
Then he struck Meriones’ companion and charioteer, Coeranus,
who had followed him from well-built Lyctus.
Meriones had first left the curved ships and come on foot;
he would have given great strength to the Trojans,
had not Coeranus swiftly driven the fleet horses to him.
He brought light to Meriones and warded off the pitiless day,
but lost his own life beneath man-slaying Hector.
Hector struck him beneath the jaw and ear;
the spear drove out his teeth and cut his tongue in two.
He fell from the chariot, and the reins spilled upon the ground.
Meriones bent and gathered them in his hands,
then called to Idomeneus:

“Now lash the horses until you reach the swift ships;
you know yourself that the Achaeans have no more strength.”

He spoke; and Idomeneus lashed the fair-maned horses
toward the hollow ships, for fear had entered his heart.

Nor did great-hearted Ajax and Menelaus escape Zeus’s notice,
when he gave the Trojans the victory that turned the scale.
The great Telamonian Ajax began to speak:

“Ah me! Even the most childish man could now know
that father Zeus himself helps the Trojans.
Every weapon strikes its mark, whether a bad man casts it
or a good; but Zeus directs them all.
For us, our weapons fall uselessly to the earth.
Come, let us ourselves devise the best counsel,
how we may drag away the dead, and how we may return alive,
bringing joy to our dear companions, who look this way
in anguish and no longer believe that Hector’s murderous strength
and hands invincible can be held back;
they think he will fall upon the black ships.
Would that some companion might quickly carry word
to Peleus’s son; for I do not think he has heard—”
