## Revised translation pass

Lycomedes pitied the fallen son of Arisbas, his noble companion,
and came close to him, then cast his shining spear.
He struck Apisaon, son of Hippasus, shepherd of the people,
beneath the liver, under the midriff, and loosed his knees at once;
he had come from deep-soiled Paeonia, and with Asteropeus
had often held the foremost place in battle.

Asteropeus pitied him as he fell, and rushed forward,
eager to fight the Danaans; but he could no longer reach them:
everywhere shields stood around Patroclus, and spears projected.
Great Ajax moved among them all, giving his orders:
“Let no man draw back from the dead, nor stand apart
before the other Argives in the first rank;
but let us plant ourselves close around him,
and fight at hand.” So mighty Ajax commanded.

The dark earth ran red with blood, and the near-set fighters fell,
Trojan and their strong allies together with the Danaans;
nor did the Danaans fight without bloodshed.
Yet fewer of them were dying, for each remembered always
to ward death from the other in the press.

Thus they fought like fire; you could not have said
that sun or moon still shone in the heavens,
so thickly were the foremost warriors held in mist,
those who stood around the dead son of Menoetius.
But the other Trojans and well-greaved Achaeans fought at ease
beneath the clear sky; the sun’s sharp light was spread abroad,
and no cloud appeared over the earth or over the mountains.
They fought by pauses, standing far apart and avoiding
one another’s groaning arrows; but those in the midst
endured their pain beneath the mist and war,
and the pitiless bronze wore down the bravest men.

Nor yet had the two glorious men, Thrasymedes and Antilochus,
heard that blameless Patroclus had died;
they still believed him living, fighting in the foremost tumult
among the Trojans. They themselves had gone apart,
looking toward death and the flight of their companions,
for so Nestor had ordered them, urging battle from the black ships.

All day long the great strife of grievous contention rose;
and through weariness and sweat the knees, the shins,
the feet beneath each man, his hands and eyes,
were soiled as they fought around the good companion
of swift-footed Aeacides.

As when a man gives the hide of a great ox,
wet with oil, to his people to stretch;
and they, receiving it, stand apart and draw it round in a circle—
at once the moisture goes, the oil sinks inward,
and the hide is stretched by the many pulling it from every side—
so here they dragged the corpse a little way in either direction.
Their hearts told each side that it would prevail:
the Trojans thought to haul him toward Ilium,
the Achaeans toward the hollow ships.
Around him rose a savage struggle, such as not even Ares,
the driver of armies, or Athena, seeing it, could have scorned,
though anger had reached its height.
Such evil Zeus stretched over Patroclus that day,
upon men and horses alike.

And still divine Achilles had not learned that Patroclus was dead.
Far from the swift ships he fought beneath the Trojan wall;
his heart never expected that Patroclus had fallen,
but thought him living, pressed against the gates,
and sure to return, since Achilles did not at all imagine
that he could take the city without him, or he without Achilles.
Often he had heard this apart from his mother,
who told him the great designs of Zeus;
but she did not tell him the full evil that had come upon him:
that his dearest companion had perished.

The fighters kept their sharpened spears around the corpse,
pressing without cease and slaughtering one another.
Then one bronze-clad Achaean cried:

“Friends, it is no honor for us to return to the hollow ships.
Let the black earth open here for every man;
that would be far better for us than to yield this body
to the horse-taming Trojans, that they may drag him into their city
and win themselves glory.”

Then a great-hearted Trojan answered in turn:

“Friends, even if fate has decreed that all of us
shall be subdued beside this man, let no one flee the battle.”

So one called out, and roused the strength of each.
They fought, and the iron uproar, brazen and terrible,
rose through the tireless air to heaven.

The horses of Aeacus’s son stood apart from the battle
and wept, when first they learned that their charioteer
had fallen in the dust beneath man-slaying Hector.
Automedon, brave son of Diores, struck them often
with the swift lash; often he spoke to them gently,
and often with threats; but they would neither turn back
to the ships beside the broad Hellespont,
nor enter the battle with the Achaeans.
They stood fast like a pillar set upon the tomb
of a dead man or woman, holding the splendid chariot,
their heads fixed firmly against the ground.
Hot tears ran down their eyelids to the earth
as they mourned their charioteer, and their thick manes,
fallen from the yoke, were soiled on either side.

The son of Cronus saw them mourning and pitied them.
He shook his head and spoke within his heart:

“Ah, poor creatures! Why did we give you to mortal Peleus,
you who are ageless and immortal?
Was it that you might suffer sorrow among wretched men?
For there is nothing more miserable than a man,
of all that breathe and creep upon the earth.
Yet Hector, son of Priam, shall not drive you and your wrought car
away; I will not permit it. Is it not enough for him
to possess the armor and boast over it in vain?
But I will put courage into your knees and your spirit,
so that you may carry Automedon safely from the battle
to the hollow ships. I will still grant you glory:
you shall kill until you reach the well-benched ships,
and the sun shall sink and holy darkness come.”

So speaking, he breathed good courage into the horses.
They shook the dust from their manes and swept the swift chariot
through Trojans and Achaeans.
Automedon fought among them, grieving for his companion,
leaping with the horses like a hawk among geese.
He easily fled beneath the Trojans’ uproar,
and easily sprang upon the crowd to follow them;
but he could not take a man when he drove in pursuit,
for, alone within the holy chariot, he could not at once
thrust with the spear and restrain the eager horses.

At last a companion saw him with his eyes:
Alcimedon, son of Laerces, son of Haemon.
He stood behind the chariot and spoke to Automedon:

“Automedon, what god has put this fruitless counsel
within your breast and robbed you of your good sense?
How do you fight the Trojans alone in the foremost ranks,
while your companion has been killed and Hector himself
wears the armor of Aeacus’s son upon his shoulders?”

The son of Diores answered him in turn:

“Alcimedon, what other Achaean is your equal
to hold and tame the strength of these immortal horses,
unless Patroclus, equal in counsel to the gods,
were living? But now death and fate have found him.
Take the whip and the silent reins;
I will leap down from the chariot and fight.”

He spoke; Alcimedon sprang forward upon the team
and quickly took the whip and reins in his hands.
Automedon leaped down. Glorious Hector saw him,
and at once called to Aeneas, who stood close by:

“Aeneas, counselor of the bronze-clad Trojans,
I see these two horses of swift-footed Aeacus
appear in battle with their wretched charioteers.
I hope we might seize them, if you are willing in your spirit;
for the two of us could not stand face to face against Ares
if they came upon us with their charge.”

So he spoke, and the good son of Anchises did not refuse.
They went straight at the team, their shoulders wrapped
in dry, hard oxhide; much bronze flashed upon them.
