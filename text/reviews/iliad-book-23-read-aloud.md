# Iliad Book 23 — read-aloud and fidelity revision log

**Scope:** Iliad 23.1–897, current working translation  
**Date:** 2026-09-03  
**Status:** editorial working evidence; not a gate pass  
**Reviewer:** AI-assisted draft review; human sign-off required

This pass tests Patroclus’s funeral, the dream, the pyre, the chariot race,
the athletic contests, and the closing prizes. It checks the Longfellow-inspired
cadence against the seven Greek-collation units; it does not certify meter or
replace an independent omissions/additions review.

## Findings and actions

| Passage | Finding | Action or decision |
|---|---|---|
| 23.1–108 | The funeral begins in motion—horses circle the body—before Achilles’s private lament and Patroclus’s return as a soul. | Preserve the triple circuit, the twelve Trojan youths, the rejected bath, the shared-urn request, and the distinction between soul and embodied embrace. |
| 23.109–272 | The pyre’s ritual sequence depends on exact offerings, Apollo’s cloud, and the winds’ intervention; the dead are gathered only after the fire has done its work. | Retain the hair vow to Spercheius, four-necked horses, two dogs, twelve youths, Boreas and Zephyrus, Iris’s Oceanus-bound errand, and the bone-gathering. |
| 23.273–612 | The chariot race balances divine interference with human skill: Athena harms Eumelus and later helps Diomedes; Antilochus’s risky pass is resolved by apology and Menelaus’s generosity. | Preserve the five starters, Phoenix at the turning-post, the distinct prize order, and the full Menelaus–Antilochus arbitration. |
| 23.613–718 | Nestor’s cup is a memorial prize rather than a competitive award, and the boxing and wrestling contests restore communal speech beside grief. | Keep Nestor’s Amarynceus recollection, Epeius’s clean victory, and the equal wrestling decision between Ajax and Odysseus. |
| 23.719–897 | The foot race, stone throw, archery, and final spear distribution must remain in sequence; the last event is not a spear contest. | Corrected the working synopsis: Odysseus wins the foot race, Polypoetes the stone throw, Meriones the archery, and the final distribution gives Agamemnon the cauldron and Meriones the spear. |

## Critical craft notes

- The verse is strongest when public ceremony and private grief share the same
  breath: the games must feel like a continuation of mourning, not a reset.
- Longfellow-like elevation belongs in Patroclus’s dream, Nestor’s memory, and
  the similes of the races; the comic dung-slip should remain quick and plain.
- Prize names and contest order are narrative facts, not expendable catalogue
  detail; the synopsis now agrees with the source-collated verse.
- The meter report remains a syllable screen. A named human reader must still
  assess stress, proper names, and the long contest speeches before the Verse
  gate can pass.
