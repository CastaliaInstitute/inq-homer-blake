## Revised translation pass

“I think it shall be done, as the immortals have laid it
Within my heart; I am no prophet, nor do I know
With certainty the signs of birds. He shall not remain long
Apart from his native land, not if iron chains should hold him;
He will devise a way to come, for he is full of devices.

“But tell me this with truth: art thou the very son of Odysseus?
In head and beautiful eyes thou art astonishingly like him;
For often we met together before he went to Troy,
When all the best of the Argives sailed there in hollow ships.
Since then I have not seen Odysseus, nor he me.”

Prudent Telemachus answered her:
“Therefore I will tell thee all the truth. My mother says
That I am his son; but I myself do not know. No one has yet
Recognized his own birth with certainty. Would that I had been
The son of some fortunate man, growing old among his possessions!
But now, they say, I am the son of the most unfortunate
Of all mortal men—for thou hast asked me this.”

Then the goddess, gray-eyed Athena, answered him:
“Surely the gods have not made thy lineage nameless hereafter,
Since Penelope bore thee such as thou art. But come, tell me truly:
What feast is this, and what company? Why hast thou need of it?
Is it a banquet, or a wedding? This is no common contribution;
They seem to feast within the house in arrogance,
And any thoughtful man who saw so many shameful things
Would feel indignation.”

Prudent Telemachus answered her:
“Stranger, since thou askest and questionest me of these things,
Once this house was destined to be rich and blameless,
While that man was still present in his country.
But now the gods, devising evil, have willed otherwise;
They have made him invisible beyond all men. I should not grieve
So deeply even for his death, if he had fallen among the Trojans,
Or in the arms of his companions after all the war he endured.
Then the whole Achaean host would have made him a mound,
And afterward he would have won great glory for his son.

“But now the Harpies have snatched him away without fame;
He has gone unseen and unheard, and left me grief and lament.
Nor do I mourn for him alone, since the gods have made other
Sorrowful troubles for me. All the greatest men who rule
The islands—Dulichium, Samos, wooded Zacynthus,
And those who hold rocky Ithaca—court my mother
And consume my house. She neither refuses the hateful marriage,
Nor has the strength to bring it to completion;
And they waste away, eating my possessions. Soon they will destroy
Me also.”

Pallas Athena answered, stirred to anger:
“Ah me! How greatly thou hast need of absent Odysseus,
That he might lay his hands upon the shameless suitors.
If only he would now come and stand before the first doors
Of the house, wearing helmet, shield, and two spears—
Such as I first saw him when he drank and rejoiced
In our house, returning from Ephyra, from the halls
Of Ilus, son of Mermerus. For Odysseus had gone there
In his swift ship, seeking a man-slaying poison,
That he might smear it upon his bronze arrows.
But Ilus would not give it, fearing the everlasting gods;
My father gave it, for he loved Odysseus greatly.
If such a man should meet the suitors, all would be short-lived,
And their marriages would be bitter.

“Yet these matters lie upon the knees of the gods:
Whether he will return and exact payment in his halls,
Or whether he will not. But I urge thee to consider
How thou mayest drive the suitors from the house.”

## Decision log

- Preserve Athena’s guarded prophecy: she is not claiming seer’s authority,
  but speaks from what the immortals have placed within her heart.
- Keep the physical likeness between Telemachus and Odysseus while retaining
  Telemachus’ uncertainty about his own paternity and his wish for a fortunate,
  settled father.
- Preserve the feast as a question of kind, not merely size: Telemachus and
  Athena distinguish a wedding, a common contribution, and predatory consumption.
- Retain Telemachus’ full geography—Dulichium, Samos, Zacynthus, and rocky Ithaca—
  and the suitors’ twofold violence against his household and his person.
- Keep Athena’s imagined Odysseus with helmet, shield, and two spears, together
  with the Ephyra/Ilus poison episode and the gods’ prohibition against the poison.
- Preserve the unresolved divine condition at the end: return and vengeance
  remain with the gods, while Telemachus is charged to devise an earthly response.
