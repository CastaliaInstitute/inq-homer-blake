## Translation

Odysseus and Telemachus moved the weapons from the hall, leaving them
two swords, two spears, and two shields. If the suitors should ask,
they would say that smoke had spoiled the arms, or that drunken men
might turn iron against each other. Athena went before them with a golden
lamp, and the walls shone. “Be silent,” Odysseus said; “the gods have done this.”

Penelope descended like Artemis or golden Aphrodite. Melantho mocked
the ragged stranger; he answered that necessity had made him a beggar,
and warned that either Odysseus or the grown Telemachus might yet judge
the women’s conduct. Penelope rebuked the handmaid and called for a chair,
a fleece, and the stranger’s story.

He answered with his Cretan fiction: Crete, Cnossus, Minos, Deucalion,
and Idomeneus; he called himself Aethon and said that he had welcomed
Odysseus at Amnisus after a storm. He and his companions remained twelve
days before sailing. He described the purple cloak,
the golden brooch with its hound and fawn, the snow-white tunic,
the dark curled hair, and Eurybates, broad-chested and black-haired.
Penelope knew the garments she had given her husband, and wept.
He told her that Odysseus lived near Thesprotis and would return.

Then Penelope spoke of the suitors and the web for Laertes’ shroud:
by day she wove it, by night undid it, and for three years concealed
her design. In the fourth, the treacherous handmaids betrayed her.
Her parents pressed her to marry; her grown son grieved that the Achaeans
consumed his wealth.

She ordered Eurycleia to wash the stranger’s feet. The old nurse,
who had reared Odysseus from birth, found the scar above his knee,
the wound a boar’s white tusk gave him on Parnassus with Autolycus
and his sons. The basin fell; bronze rang, and water spilled. “You are
Odysseus, dear child,” she cried. Athena turned Penelope’s mind aside,
and Odysseus seized the nurse and commanded silence. He would know
the dishonoring women himself after the suitors’ fall.

Penelope told her dream: twenty geese ate wheat, and an eagle from the
mountain broke their necks. In a human voice it said the geese were
the suitors and that it, once an eagle, was now her husband returned.
Odysseus said the meaning was plain. Penelope answered that dreams pass
through gates of horn or ivory, and that ivory dreams deceive.

At dawn she would set twelve axes in a row. Whoever could string
Odysseus’ bow and send an arrow through them all would become her husband;
she would leave the house, though she would remember it even in dreams.
“Do not delay,” said Odysseus. “Before they touch the bow, Odysseus returns.”

Penelope went upstairs to her tearful bed, and Athena poured sweet sleep
upon her eyelids.
