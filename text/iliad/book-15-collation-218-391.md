## Revised translation pass

Poseidon left the Achaean people and sank into the sea; the heroes missed him.
Then Zeus spoke to Apollo: “Go now to Hector, bronze-armed. The Earth-Shaker
has gone to the bright sea, avoiding my sharp anger—for others beneath Cronus
would have heard our battle. It is better for both of us that he yielded,
for he would not have completed his work without sweat. Take the tasseled aegis
in your hands and shake it, terrifying the Achaean heroes. Let bright Hector
be your care: rouse his great courage until the Achaeans flee to their ships
and the Hellespont. From there I will direct the work and word,
so that the Achaeans may breathe again after their labor.

“Before the desire of Peleus' son is fulfilled I will not cease my anger,
nor let any immortal defend the Danaans here. Thus I promised Thetis
when she clasped my knees and begged me to honor Achilles, sacker of cities.”

Apollo obeyed his father and went down from Ida's mountains like a hawk,
swiftest among birds. He found Priam's son Hector sitting, no longer fallen;
his spirit was gathering, and he recognized the companions around him.
His breathing and sweat had ceased, for the mind of aegis-bearing Zeus
had roused him. Apollo stood near and asked: “Hector, son of Priam,
why do you sit apart, faint? Has some grief reached you?”

Hector, still weak, answered: “Who are you, best among the gods,
that you question me face to face? Do you not know that Ajax struck me
beside the sterns, killing my companions with a stone and stopping my courage?
I thought today I should reach the dead and the house of Hades.”

Apollo replied: “Take heart. Cronus' son has sent me from Ida
to stand beside you and defend you—I am Phoebus Apollo,
who have saved both you and the steep city before.
Now rouse the many horsemen to drive their swift horses against the ships;
I will go before you and make the road level, turning the Achaean heroes.”

He breathed great courage into the shepherd of the people. As a stalled horse,
well fed at the manger, breaks its tether and runs clattering over the plain,
accustomed to bathe in a beautiful river, proud, holding its head high,
its mane flying over its shoulders, and its knees carrying it quickly
through the accustomed fields and pastures—so Hector moved his swift feet
and knees, rousing the horsemen when he had heard the god's voice.

The Danaans followed in a mass, like hounds and countrymen chasing a horned deer
or wild goat, until a steep rock or shadowed forest preserves the prey;
then a bearded lion appears before them, and turns them all, however eager.
So the Danaans, while they could follow the lines, stabbed with sword and spear;
but when they saw Hector moving through the ranks, fear took them,
and their courage fell beside their feet.

Then Thoas, son of Andraemon, best of the Aetolians, skilled with the spear
and good in close battle, spoke; few Achaeans could surpass him in assembly:
“What a great wonder I see! Hector has risen again, escaping his fate.
Everyone expected Telamonian Ajax's hands to kill him.
Some god has rescued and saved him—he who has loosened the knees of many Danaans.
I think he will do so again; Hector does not stand here without Zeus' thunder.
Come, let us send the multitude back toward the ships;
we who claim to be best in the army will stand and try first to stop him,
holding up our spears. I think even eager Hector will fear to enter our crowd.”

They heard and obeyed. Around Ajax and King Idomeneus they arranged
Teucer, Meriones, and Meges, calling the best to stand against Hector
and the Trojans, while the multitude withdrew toward the Achaean ships.

The Trojans struck together, led by Hector with long strides;
before him Phoebus Apollo walked, clothed in a cloud around his shoulders,
holding the dread, tasseled, shaggy aegis, which Hephaestus had made
and given Zeus to carry for the terror of men. Apollo held it in his hands
and led the people. The Argives stood massed, and a sharp cry rose from both;
arrows leapt from strings, and many bold spears flew from hands.
Some fixed in the flesh of eager young men; many stood midway in the earth,
before reaching the white skin, desiring to glut themselves with flesh.

While Phoebus held the aegis quiet in his hands, the missiles reached both sides
and men fell. But when he shook it directly before the swift Danaans
and himself cried terribly, he bewitched their hearts;
they forgot their warlike strength. As two wild beasts suddenly scatter
a herd of cattle or a great flock of sheep in the black depth of night,
when the herdsman is absent, so the courage of the Achaeans was driven away;
Apollo gave glory to the Trojans and to Hector.

Then man killed man as the lines broke. Hector killed Stichius,
leader of the bronze-clad Boeotians, and Arcesilaus, faithful companion
of great-hearted Mnestheus. Aeneas stripped Medon and Iasus.
Medon was the bastard son of divine Oileus and brother of Ajax;
he lived in Phylace, far from his native land, after killing his stepmother's
kinsman. Iasus had been leader of the Athenians, son of Sphelus' son Bucolus.
Polydamas killed Mecisteus; Polites struck Ech ius in the first clash;
godlike Agenor killed Clonius; Paris struck Deiochus in the back,
the lowest part of the shoulder, as he fled among the foremost.

While the Trojans stripped the fallen, the Achaeans fled through the trench
and stakes, driven here and there, and entered the wall by necessity.
Hector shouted through the ranks: “Rush toward the ships and leave the human
spoils. Whoever I see apart from the ships, I will contrive his death there;
his kindred will not claim him when he is dead, but dogs will drag him
before our city.” He lashed his horses and drove along the ranks;
the Trojans shouted with him and held the chariots together.

Apollo went before them and easily trampled down the banks of the deep trench,
throwing them into the middle and making a long, broad road,
as far as a spear's cast when a man tests his strength.
The Trojans poured forward in phalanx; Apollo carried the honored aegis,
and threw down the Achaean wall as easily as a child at the sea
builds toys of sand, then kicks and scatters them again in childish play.
So you, Phoebus, confused the long labor and misery of the Argives
and roused flight among them.

They stood beside the ships, calling to one another and raising their hands
to the gods, each praying greatly. Nestor, the Gerenian shepherd of the Achaeans,
prayed with outstretched hand to the starry heaven:
“Father Zeus, if ever in Argos rich in flocks someone burned the fat thighs
of ox or sheep and prayed to return, and you promised and nodded,
remember this and defend us from the pitiless day. Do not let the Trojans
master the Achaeans so.”

He prayed, and Zeus the counselor thundered greatly, hearing the old son
of Neleus. When the Trojans heard the thunder of aegis-bearing Zeus,
they sprang more fiercely upon the Argives and remembered their courage.
As a great wave from the broad sea falls over a ship's walls
when the wind's strength drives it, for the wind most raises the waves,
so the Trojans crossed the wall with a great cry. They drove their horses
among the sterns and fought at close quarters with their spears;
some fought from their horses, some mounting the dark ships,
and used long boarding poles that lay upon the vessels,
shod with bronze for sea-fighting.
