## Revised translation pass

The suitors raised a tumult through the shadowed halls,
And all prayed to be laid beside their beds. Then prudent
Telemachus began to speak among them:

“Suitors of my mother, swollen with insolent excess,
For now let us feast and take our pleasure, and let no cry
Be raised; it is a beautiful thing to hear a singer such as this,
Whose voice resembles the voices of the gods.
Tomorrow let us go together to the assembly,
That I may speak plainly and command you all
To leave these halls. Seek other feasts for yourselves,
Eating your own possessions and taking turns among your houses.
But if this seems to you more profitable and more just—
That one man’s livelihood should perish without payment—
Consume it. I shall call upon the gods who live forever,
If Zeus may ever grant that such deeds be paid back;
Then, without payment, you shall perish within these halls.”

So he spoke; and all, biting their lips, wondered at Telemachus,
Because he had spoken boldly. Antinous, son of Eupeithes,
Answered him: “Telemachus, surely the gods themselves are teaching thee
To speak high words and boldly. May the Son of Cronus never make thee
King in sea-girt Ithaca, though that inheritance is thine by birth.”

Prudent Telemachus answered him:
“Antinous, wilt thou be angry at the word I speak?
I should desire that very thing, if Zeus would give it.
Dost thou call kingship the worst thing that can befall a man?
It is no evil to be a king; his house soon becomes rich,
And he himself more greatly honored. Yet there are many other kings
Among the Achaeans in sea-girt Ithaca, young and old;
One of them may hold this power, since noble Odysseus has died.
But I shall be lord of our house and of the servants
Whom noble Odysseus won for me.”

Then Eurymachus, son of Polybus, answered him:
“Telemachus, these matters lie upon the knees of the gods:
Who shall be king among the Achaeans in sea-girt Ithaca.
Mayest thou keep thy possessions and rule thy own halls;
May no man come while Ithaca is still inhabited
To take thy property from thee against thy will.
But I wish to ask thee about this stranger:
From where is the man, and what land does he claim as his?
Where are his family and his father’s fields?
Does he bring some message from thy father’s return,
Or has he come desiring some private need of his own?
How suddenly he sprang up and went away, nor waited
For us to learn his name! He did not look like a base man.”

Prudent Telemachus answered him:
“Eurymachus, my father’s return has perished from my mind.
I no longer trust a message, should one come from anywhere,
Nor do I heed a prophecy, whatever oracle my mother
May summon into the house to question.
This stranger is an inherited guest-friend from Taphos;
He claims to be Mentes, son of wise Anchialus,
And lord of the oar-loving Taphians.”

So Telemachus spoke, though within his mind he knew
That the immortal goddess had been there. The suitors turned
To dancing and the lovely song, and waited for evening to come.
When dark evening came upon them in their pleasure,
They went each man to his own house to sleep.

Telemachus went to the beautiful chamber built for him,
High in a place apart, within the court, and there lay down,
His mind turning over many thoughts.
With him came faithful Eurycleia, carrying the burning torches,
Daughter of Ops, son of Peisenor—the woman whom Laertes
Had bought with his own possessions when she was still young,
Giving the price of twenty oxen; and he honored her in his halls
As he honored his prudent wife, but never shared her bed,
For he feared his wife’s anger. She loved him more than any servant,
And had raised him when he was little.

She opened the doors of the closely fitted chamber,
And sat upon the bed. Telemachus took off his soft tunic
And placed it in the hands of the thoughtful old woman.
She folded it, smoothed it, and hung it upon a peg
Beside the pierced bedstead; then she went out from the chamber,
Drew the door shut with its silver handle,
And pulled the thong across the fastening.
There, covered in a fleece, he lay awake through all the night,
Turning in his mind the road Athena had revealed to him.
