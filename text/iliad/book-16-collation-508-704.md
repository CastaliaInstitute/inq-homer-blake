## Revised translation pass

When Glaucus heard the cry, a dreadful grief came upon him;
his heart was shaken, though he could not yet give help.
With his hand he pressed his arm, for a wound tormented him,
the wound Teucer had dealt him when he came against the high wall,
defending his companions from destruction.
He prayed to Apollo the Far-Shooter:

“Hear me, lord, whether you are in the rich land of Lycia
or in Troy; you can hear everywhere the man in trouble,
as now trouble has reached me.
I have this grievous wound, and sharp pains drive through my arm;
the blood cannot dry, and the shoulder grows heavy beneath it.
I cannot hold my spear steady, nor go and fight the enemy.
The best of men has fallen, Sarpedon, son of Zeus;
yet Zeus himself does not defend his son.
But you, lord, heal this stubborn wound,
quiet the pain, and give me strength,
that I may call the Lycians and urge them to fight,
and myself contend around the dead.”

So he prayed, and Phoebus Apollo heard him.
At once he quieted the pain, dried the black blood
from the grievous wound, and put strength into Glaucus' heart.
Glaucus knew it and rejoiced that the great god
had so swiftly heard his prayer.

First he went everywhere among the leaders of the Lycians,
urging them to fight around Sarpedon; then he moved
with long strides among the Trojans, seeking Polydamas,
Panthous' son, and noble Agenor. He went beside Aeneas
and Hector in bronze, and standing near them spoke winged words:

“Hector, you have utterly forgotten the allies
who waste their lives far from friends and fatherland for your sake,
while you will not come to their aid.
Sarpedon lies here, the leader of the shield-bearing Lycians,
who guarded Lycia by his judgments and his strength;
bronze Ares beneath Patroclus has overcome him.
Come, dear friends, stand beside us and let your hearts be shamed;
do not let the Myrmidons strip his armor
and dishonor the dead, in anger for all the Danaans
whom we slew with our spears beside the swift ships.”

So he spoke; and an unbearable grief seized the Trojans,
a grief not to be soothed, for Sarpedon had been the city's support,
though he was a stranger there, and many people followed him;
he himself excelled in battle.
They went straight against the Danaans, eager for the fight;
Hector led them, enraged for Sarpedon.

But the shaggy heart of Patroclus, son of Menoetius,
roused the Achaeans. He first spoke to the two Ajaxes,
already burning for battle:

“Ajaxes, now let it be dear to you to defend us,
as you were before among men—or better still.
Here lies the man who first leaped upon the Achaean wall,
Sarpedon. If we could seize him and dishonor him,
strip the armor from his shoulders, and kill one of his companions
who defend him here with pitiless bronze!”

So he spoke, and they themselves desired to ward the danger off.
When Trojans and Lycians, Myrmidons and Achaeans,
had strengthened their lines on either side,
they joined with a dreadful cry to fight around the dead;
the armor of the men rang greatly.

Zeus stretched a hateful night across the fierce battle,
that the grievous labor of war might surround his beloved son.
The Trojans drove the bright-eyed Achaeans back;
for a man not at all the worst among the Myrmidons had been struck,
the godlike Epigeus, son of Agacles.
Once he had ruled well-settled Budeion;
but after he killed his noble cousin,
he had fled to Peleus and silver-footed Thetis,
and they sent him with Achilles, breaker of ranks,
to well-horsed Ilium, to fight the Trojans.

As Epigeus touched the dead man, bright Hector struck him
upon the head with a stone; his skull split completely
inside the heavy helmet, and he fell face downward upon the corpse;
death that destroys the spirit poured around him.

Grief came upon Patroclus for his fallen companion.
He sprang through the foremost fighters like a swift hawk
that scatters jackdaws and starlings.
So straight upon the Lycians and Trojans, driver of horses,
you rushed, Patroclus, your heart angered for your companion.
With a stone he struck Sthenelaus, beloved son of Ithaemenes,
upon the neck, and shattered the tendons.
The foremost fighters and bright Hector gave ground.

As far as the cast of a long javelin reaches,
when a man tests it in a contest or in war
against enemies who destroy the spirit,
so far the Trojans retreated, and the Achaeans drove them.

Glaucus, first among the shield-bearing Lycians,
turned and killed great-hearted Bathycles,
beloved son of Chalcon, who lived in Hellas,
and excelled among the Myrmidons in wealth and prosperity.
Glaucus turned suddenly and struck him in the middle of the chest,
when Bathycles seized him in pursuit; he fell with a crash.
The Achaeans were taken by deep grief when the good man fell,
and the Trojans rejoiced greatly; they stood in a mass around him.
Yet the Achaeans did not forget their strength,
but carried their courage straight against the enemy.

Then Meriones took a Trojan warrior, Laogonus,
brave son of Onetor, who was priest of Idaean Zeus
and was honored among the people like a god.
He struck him beneath the jaw and the ear;
the life swiftly left his limbs, and hateful darkness seized him.

Aeneas cast his bronze spear at Meriones,
hoping to strike him as he advanced beneath his shield.
But Meriones saw it coming and avoided the bronze;
he bent forward, and the long spear planted itself behind him
in the earth, while its butt-end quivered.
There mighty Ares drove the strength from it;
the point of Aeneas' spear, shaking, sank into the ground,
for it had leaped vainly from his strong hand.

Aeneas was angered and spoke:

“Meriones, though you are a dancer, my spear would soon
have stilled you forever, had it struck you.”

Famous for his spear, Meriones answered him:

“Aeneas, though you are strong, it is hard for you
to quench the spirit of every man who comes against you.
You too are mortal. If I should strike you in the middle
with sharp bronze, you would soon, though strong
and trusting in your hands, give me glory,
and give your life to Hades of the famous horses.”

The brave son of Menoetius rebuked him:

“Meriones, why do you speak such things, though you are good?
Dear friend, the Trojans will not leave the dead because of taunts;
the earth will hold one of them first.
The end of war is in the hands, and words belong to counsel;
there is no need to multiply speech. We must fight.”

So he spoke and led the way, and Meriones, a man like the gods,
followed after him.
The sound of those who cut trees rose in the mountain valleys,
and far away the noise was heard;
so from the broad earth rose the crash of bronze,
hide, and finely made shields,
as swords and double-edged spears struck together.

No man, however wise, could any longer have recognized
divine Sarpedon, for arrows, blood, and dust
covered him completely from head to the tips of his feet.
The fighters crowded continually around the dead,
as flies buzz in a cattle-yard about the full milk-pails
in spring, when the vessels are wet with milk.

So they crowded around the body; nor did Zeus turn
his shining eyes away from the fierce battle.
He watched continually and pondered in his spirit,
thinking many thoughts about the death of Patroclus:
whether Hector should soon kill him in the hard battle
beside godlike Sarpedon, and strip the armor from his shoulders,
or whether he should heap still more grievous labor upon many men.

As he considered, this seemed better to him:
that the brave companion of Peleus' son Achilles
should drive the Trojans and bronze-clad Hector back to the city,
and take the spirit from many men.
First he put cowardice into Hector's heart;
Hector climbed into his chariot and turned to flee,
calling upon the other Trojans to flee as well,
for he understood the sacred balance of Zeus.

Nor did the mighty Lycians remain;
all were seized by fear when they saw their king
lying with his heart struck, among the heap of dead men.
Many had fallen upon him when Cronus' son
had drawn out the fierce quarrel.

They took the bronze armor from Sarpedon's shoulders,
and the brave son of Menoetius sent it to his companions
to carry away to the hollow ships.

Then Zeus, who gathers the clouds, spoke to Apollo:

“Come now, dear Phoebus, go and cleanse the dark blood
from Sarpedon, lifting him from the arrows;
carry him far away, and wash him in the river's streams,
anoint him with ambrosia, and clothe him in immortal garments.
Then send him with swift guides, Sleep and Death,
the twin brothers, who shall quickly set him down
in the rich land and broad people of Lycia.
There his brothers and companions shall prepare his body
with mound and pillar; that is the honor of the dead.”

So he spoke, and Apollo did not disobey his father's word.
He went down from Ida's mountains into the dreadful battle.
At once he lifted divine Sarpedon from the arrows,
carried him far away, washed him in the river's streams,
anointed him with ambrosia, and clothed him in immortal garments.
Then he sent him with swift guides, Sleep and Death, the twins,
who quickly set him down in the rich and broad land of Lycia.

But Patroclus ordered his horses and Automedon,
and pursued the Trojans and Lycians; he was greatly deluded.
Had he guarded the word of Peleus' son,
he would have escaped the evil fate of black death.
But the will of Zeus is always stronger than the will of men:
it frightens even the brave and takes victory away with ease,
and again it gives the courage to fight.

Whom did you first kill, Patroclus, and whom last,
when the gods called you to death?
First Adrestus, and Autonous, and Echeclus,
Perimus son of Megas, Epistor, and Melanippus;
then Elasus, Mulius, and Pylartes—
all these you took, while the others turned to flee.

Then the sons of the Achaeans would have taken steep-gated Troy
under Patroclus' hands, for he raged before them with his spear,
if Phoebus Apollo had not stood upon the well-built tower,
thinking death for him and giving aid to the Trojans.
Three times Patroclus climbed the high wall at the corner;
three times Apollo thrust him back,
touching his shining shield with immortal hands.
