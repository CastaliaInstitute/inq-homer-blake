## Revised translation pass

The young men crowned the mixing-bowls with wine,
And reached their hands toward the food set ready before them.
When the suitors had put away their desire for drink and food,
Other things began to occupy their minds:
Song and the dance, the ornaments of a feast.
The herald placed a beautiful lyre in the hands
Of Phemius, who sang among the suitors under compulsion.
He touched the strings and began a lovely song.

Then Telemachus bent his head toward gray-eyed Athena,
So that the others might not hear, and spoke softly:
“Dear stranger, will you be angry at the word I speak?
These men care only for such things as lyre and song;
They care easily, since they consume another man’s livelihood
Without payment—his bones, perhaps, already rotting white
In the rain upon the mainland, or rolling in the sea.
If only they should see him returning here to Ithaca,
They would all pray to be lighter of foot than they are now,
Or richer in gold and raiment. But now he has perished
By an evil fate, and no comfort remains for us,
Even if some mortal on earth should say he will come;
The day of his return has been destroyed.

“But come, tell me this truth and conceal nothing:
Who art thou, and from what people? Where is thy city,
And who are thy parents? Upon what ship didst thou come?
How did the sailors bring thee to Ithaca, and who did they claim
That they were? For I do not think thou camest here on foot.
Tell me this truly, that I may know it well:
Art thou a new stranger to us, or a guest inherited
From my father’s house? For many others too have entered
Our halls from foreign lands, since he himself was friendly to men.”

Then the goddess, gray-eyed Athena, answered him:
“Therefore I will tell thee all these things most truly.
I claim to be Mentes, son of wise Anchialus,
And I rule the oar-loving Taphians. Now I have come
With ship and companions, sailing the wine-dark sea
Toward men of another tongue, to Temese for bronze;
And I carry shining iron. My ship lies yonder in the fields,
Apart from the city, in the harbor of Rheithron,
Beneath wooded Neriton. We claim that our fathers were
Guests and friends from the beginning, if thou wilt go
And ask the old hero Laertes, who, they say, no longer
Comes into the city, but suffers far away upon his farm,
With an old serving-woman who sets food and drink before him
Whenever weariness takes his limbs, as he creeps upward
Through the vineyard land upon the slopes.

“Now I have come; for they told me thy father was in the city.
But the gods, it seems, hinder his road. Noble Odysseus
Has not yet died upon the earth; he is still alive somewhere,
Held back upon the wide sea, upon an island ringed with water;
And cruel men keep him there, against his will.
But now I will prophesy to thee, as the thought lies within my heart.”

## Decision log

- Preserve the suitors’ consumption of another man’s livelihood and Telemachus’
  private shame; the feast’s music is inseparable from household dispossession.
- Keep Phemius as a singer performing under compulsion, not a neutral entertainer;
  the distinction prepares Penelope’s later objection to his song.
- Retain Telemachus’ sequence of questions—identity, people, city, parents, ship,
  sailors, and inherited guest-friendship—before Athena’s fabricated biography.
- Preserve Mentes’ Taphian identity, Anchialus, the voyage to Temese for bronze,
  shining iron, and the ship’s position at Rheithron beneath wooded Neriton.
- Keep Laertes’ rural withdrawal and the old woman’s food and drink as physical
  signs of the absent king’s diminished household life.
- End with Athena’s turn into prophecy and the precise claim that Odysseus is
  alive but held against his will; the prophecy itself begins at line 201.
