## Revised translation pass

“Glaucus, why are you and I honored above all others in Lycia,
by seat, by portions of meat, and by the fuller cups of wine?
All men look upon us there as if we were gods, and we possess
a great estate beside the banks of Xanthus—beautiful orchards
and wheat-bearing fields. Therefore we must stand among the first
and meet the burning battle, so that a Lycian in his close armor
may say: ‘Our kings do not rule Lycia without glory;
they eat the fat sheep and drink the honeyed, excellent wine.
But their strength is also noble, since they fight among the first.’

“Dear friend, if, by fleeing this war, we could forever be
without age and death, I would neither fight in the foremost
nor send you into the battle where men win glory. But now
the countless fates of death stand near; no mortal can escape
or evade them. Come: either we shall give another his glory,
or another shall give it to us.”

So he spoke, and Glaucus neither turned away nor refused him.
Together they went straight on, leading the great Lycian host.

Mnestheus, son of Peteus, saw them and shuddered;
they were advancing upon his tower, bringing ruin with them.
He searched the Achaean wall for some leader who might defend
his companions from the danger, and there he saw the two Ajaxes,
never weary of war, and near them Teucer, newly come from his hut.
Yet he could not make his voice heard by shouting:
so great was the din, the cries reaching heaven,
the blows upon shields, the horse-haired helmets,
and the gates, for every entrance was beset,
and men stood beneath them trying by force to break inside.

At once he sent the herald Thoötes to Ajax:
“Run, noble Thoötes; hasten and call both Ajaxes.
Let both come here, for the steep edge of death
will soon be met at this place. The Lycian leaders
have risen with a strength they showed in former battles.
If toil and conflict have also begun elsewhere,
let Telamonian Ajax come alone, the strong one,
and let Teucer follow, skilled in the bow.”

The herald heard and did not disobey. He ran beside
the wall of the bronze-clad Achaeans, stood beside the Ajaxes,
and quickly spoke: “Leaders of the Argives in their bronze,
the dear son of Peteus commands you to go there,
that you may meet the labor, if only for a little while.
Let both come, for the steep edge of death will soon be met;
the Lycian leaders have risen with the strength
they showed in former battles. But if war and conflict
have begun here too, let Telamonian Ajax go alone,
and let Teucer follow, skilled in the bow.”

The great Telamonian Ajax did not refuse him.
He spoke at once to Ajax, son of Oileus:
“You and strong Lycomedes must remain here,
standing to rouse the Danaans to fight with all their strength.
I will go there and meet the battle, then quickly return
when I have given them aid.”

Having spoken, Telamonian Ajax went away;
his brother and companion Teucer followed him,
and Pandion carried Teucer's curved bows beside them.
When they reached the tower of great-hearted Mnestheus,
entering within the wall where the need was greatest,
the Lycian leaders were already mounting the battlements,
like a black storm descending. They joined the fight at once,
and the war-cry rose.

First Telamonian Ajax killed Epicles, Sarpedon's companion,
striking him with a jagged stone. Epicles had stood inside
the wall, high beside the parapet; no ordinary man,
not even one in his first youth, could easily have lifted
that stone in both hands, such men as mortals are now.
But Ajax raised it from above and cast it down;
he crushed the four-bossed helmet and broke together
the bones of the head. Epicles fell from the lofty tower
like a diver, and the life left his bones.

Teucer shot Glaucus, son of Hippolochus, as he rushed forward;
he saw the arm bare where the armor had slipped,
and stopped his battle-joy. Glaucus leapt back from the wall
unseen, that no Achaean might behold his wound
and boast over him in words.

Sarpedon grieved when he perceived Glaucus withdrawing,
yet did not forget the battle. He struck Alcmaon,
son of Thestor, with his spear; then drew the weapon out,
and Alcmaon fell face-first, his ornamented armor clattering
around him. Sarpedon seized the parapet with his powerful hands
and dragged it away; the whole work followed him,
and the wall above was laid bare, opening a road for many.

Ajax and Teucer met him. One struck the bright strap
that ran around the great shield upon his chest with an arrow;
but Zeus guarded his son from the fates, lest he should fall
beside the sterns of the ships. Ajax sprang upon the shield,
but his spear did not pass through it; he only drove Sarpedon
back from the parapet a little. Yet Sarpedon did not yield;
his heart still hoped to win glory. Turning among the godlike
Lycians, he called aloud:

“Lycians, why do you relax your warlike strength?
It is hard for me, though I am strong, to break the wall alone
and open a road beside the ships. Come after me:
the work is better when many hands are joined.”

So he spoke, and fearing their lord's rebuke,
they pressed more fiercely around their counselor and king.
On the other side the Argives strengthened their ranks
within the wall, and a great work appeared to them:
neither the strong Lycians could break the wall
and make a road to the ships, nor could the spear-bearing Danaans
drive the Lycians back after they had first reached it.

They fought like two men disputing over a boundary marker,
holding measures in their hands upon a field held in common,
each contending for an equal strip within a narrow space.
So the parapets divided them; yet above those stones
they struck one another around the breast, beneath the ox-hide
shields, the round bucklers, and the winged shields.
Many were wounded in the flesh by pitiless bronze—
some when, turning, they left the back exposed;
many were pierced clean through the shield itself.
Everywhere the towers and parapets streamed with human blood,
from Trojans and Achaeans on both sides.

Yet even so they could not make the Achaeans afraid;
they held fast like the scales of a hardworking woman,
who, holding the balance and the wool, raises each side evenly,
that she may win a wretched wage for her children.
Thus equal were the battle and the war, until at last
Zeus gave a greater glory to Hector, Priam's son,
who first sprang inside the Achaean wall.

Hector cried through the ranks so that all the Trojans heard:
“Rise, horse-taming Trojans! Break the wall of the Argives,
and cast the wondrous fire upon the ships!”

He urged them on; they all heard his voice and rushed together
against the wall. Some mounted the parapets with sharpened spears,
while Hector seized a stone that stood before the gates,
thick at its base and sharp above. Two of the best men
among those living now could hardly have levered it
from the ground onto a wagon. But Hector swung it easily alone;
the son of crooked-counseling Cronus made the stone light for him.

As a shepherd easily carries the fleece of a ram,
lifting it in one hand, and the small weight scarcely burdens him,
so Hector carried the stone straight toward the planks
that held the lofty double gates closely fitted.
Two cross-bars within secured them, and a single bolt
was fastened across them. Coming close, he stood firm,
and, planting his feet for strength, cast it into their middle,
so that the blow should not be weak. He broke both hinges;
the stone fell inward with its weight, and the gates thundered.
The cross-bars could not hold; the planks burst apart,
one here, one there, beneath the stone's stroke.

Then bright Hector sprang in, his face like swift night;
dreadful bronze flashed around his body, and in his hands
he carried two spears. No one could have stopped him
when he leapt through the gates, except the gods.
Fire burned in his eyes. Turning among the crowd,
he called the Trojans to climb across the wall;
they obeyed his urging. Some crossed the wall,
and others poured through the gates themselves.
The Danaans fled toward the hollow ships,
and an unquenchable tumult rose among them.
