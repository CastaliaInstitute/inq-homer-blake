## Revised translation pass

So Cronus' son nodded beneath his dark brows.
He fitted the armor upon Hector's body,
and terrible Enyalius, the god of war, entered him;
his limbs were filled with strength and courage.
He went among his famous allies with a great shout,
and to all he appeared in the shining armor
of great-hearted Peleus' son. Moving among them,
he urged Mesthles, Glaucus, Medon, Thersilochus,
Asteropaeon, Deisenor, Hippothous, Phorcys,
Chromius, and Ennomus the seer:

“Hear me, countless peoples of the allies round about.
I did not gather each of you here because I lacked a multitude,
or because I was needy, drawing you from your cities;
I brought you to guard the Trojan wives and innocent children
from the war-loving Achaeans.
For this I wear out your people with gifts and food,
and enlarge the courage of every man.
Therefore now turn straight upon the enemy:
let him perish, or let him be saved—for that is the talk of war.

“Whoever drags Patroclus, even dead,
into the city of the horse-taming Trojans,
and Ajax yields him, to that man I will give half the spoils;
I myself will keep the other half.
His glory shall be as great as mine.”

So he spoke, and they went straight against the Danaans,
lifting their spears; their hearts hoped to drag the dead man
from beneath Telamonian Ajax. Fools! Many would lose their spirits
upon him before they gained the body.

Then Ajax spoke to warlike Menelaus:

“Dear Menelaus, nourished by Zeus, I no longer expect
that we two shall return from the battle alive.
I fear less for Patroclus, who soon may satisfy
the dogs and birds of the Trojans, than for my own head,
and for yours, since the cloud of war surrounds all things,
and Hector appears to us as sudden destruction.
Call now the best of the Danaans, if any will hear.”

So he spoke; and Menelaus did not disobey.
He shouted through the Danaans until his voice carried:

“Dear friends, leaders and guardians of the Argives,
you who drink the people's due beside Agamemnon
and Menelaus, and each gives signs to his people—
from Zeus honor and glory follow.
It is hard for me to look for every leader separately,
for the battle's tumult is so great.
Let whoever can come himself, and let his heart be ashamed
that Patroclus should become food for the Trojan women and dogs.”

Swift Ajax, son of Oileus, heard him sharply,
and first came running against the battle.
After him came Idomeneus and his companion Meriones,
equal to murderous Enyalius.
Who could name all the others who stirred the Achaeans'
fight again from behind?

The Trojans came on together, and Hector led them.
As a great wave roars at the mouth of a heaven-fed river,
while the sea-shore thunders around it
as the sea bellows outward—so the Trojans came with a cry.
The Achaeans stood around Menoetius' son with one heart,
fenced by their bronze shields; and Cronus' son poured
a great mist around their shining helmets.
He had not hated Menoetius' son before, while he lived
as the companion of Aeacides; but he hated that he should become
prey for Trojan dogs. Therefore he roused his companions to defend him.

The Trojans drove the bright-eyed Achaeans backward.
They left the dead man behind and shrank away;
yet the high-hearted Trojans, eager as they were,
could not take any of them with their spears—
they dragged only the corpse. For a little while the Achaeans
would be forced to withdraw from it; but Ajax quickly turned them,
Ajax who surpassed the other Danaans in form and deed,
after blameless Peleus' son.

He sprang through the foremost fighters,
like a boar in courage, which easily scatters dogs
and young men in the mountains when it wheels through the valleys.
So glorious Ajax, son of Telamon, easily scattered
the ranks of Trojans who stood around Patroclus,
thinking most of dragging him to their city
and winning glory.

Hippothous, glorious son of Pelasgian Lethus,
had bound a strap around the ankle and tendons of Patroclus,
and dragged his foot through the bitter battle,
desiring to please Hector and the Trojans.
But evil came upon him, and no one, however eager, could hold it back.
The son of Telamon sprang through the crowd
and struck him at close quarters through the bronze cheek-piece
of his helmet. The horsehair crest, struck by the great spear
and the thick hand, rang around the shaft;
the brain ran bloody through the opening beside the ear.
His strength was loosed, and he let Patroclus' great foot fall
upon the ground; he fell face forward beside the dead man,
far from rich Larissa, and did not repay his dear parents
for their rearing; brief was his life,
cut short beneath the spear of great-hearted Ajax.

Hector cast his shining spear at Ajax;
Ajax saw it coming and avoided the bronze by a little.
But the spear struck Schedius, son of great-hearted Iphitus,
the best of the Phocians, who lived in renowned Panopeus
and ruled many men from his home.
The bronze point passed beneath the middle of his collarbone
and came out above the far shoulder.
He fell with a crash, and his armor rang upon him.

Ajax struck Phorcys, son of Phaenops,
as he stood over Hippothous, in the middle of his belly.
He split the hollow of the corselet,
and the bronze poured through his inward parts.
Phorcys fell in the dust and grasped the earth in his hand.
The foremost fighters and radiant Hector gave ground;
the Argives raised a great cry, dragged away the corpses
of Phorcys and Hippothous, and stripped the armor from their shoulders.

Then the Trojans would have entered Ilium,
overcome by the Argives and their lack of courage,
and the Argives would have won glory by their own strength
even beyond the will of Zeus—if Apollo himself had not roused Aeneas,
appearing in the form of Periphas, son of Epytus,
the herald who had grown old beside Aeneas' aged father,
wise in his heart and faithful in proclaiming words.
In his likeness the son of Zeus spoke to Aeneas:

“Aeneas, how could you defend Ilium even against a god,
when you have seen other men trusting in strength,
in courage, in their own numbers,
and in a people greater than our own?
Zeus greatly desires victory for us rather than the Danaans;
yet you yourselves tremble and will not fight.”

So he spoke; and Aeneas recognized Apollo the Far-Shooter
when he looked him in the face. He called loudly to Hector:

“Hector, and all you leaders of the Trojans and allies,
now it would be shameful for us to enter Ilium,
overcome by the Achaeans through cowardice.
Yet some god, I think, has stood near me and said
that Zeus, the highest counselor, is our helper in battle.
Therefore let us go straight against the Danaans,
and not let them carry Patroclus, though dead,
near to the ships.”

So he spoke, and leaped far beyond the foremost fighters,
and stood before them; the others turned and faced the Achaeans.
Then Aeneas struck Leiocritus with his spear—
