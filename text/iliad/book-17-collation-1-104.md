## Revised translation pass

Nor did Patroclus' fall escape Menelaus, son of Atreus,
dear to the war-god. He went through the foremost fighters,
clad in flashing bronze, and moved around Patroclus
as a cow circles her first-born calf, lowing and unlearned in birth;
so golden-haired Menelaus went around him.
Before the body he held his spear and shield in every direction,
desiring to kill whoever came against him.

Nor did the son of Panthous, skilled with the ash spear,
neglect Patroclus when he fell. He stood near him
and spoke to war-loving Menelaus:

“Son of Atreus, Menelaus, nourished by Zeus, leader of the people,
draw back; leave the corpse and let the human spoils be.
No Trojan or renowned ally struck Patroclus first
with his spear in the bitter battle; therefore let me gain
good glory among the Trojans. I might strike you,
and take the sweet breath of life from you.”

Golden-haired Menelaus answered him in anger:

“Father Zeus, it is not good for a man to boast so violently.
Neither leopard, lion, nor the destructive boar,
whose heart in its breast swells greatly with strength,
has such a spirit as the sons of Panthous, skilled with the spear.
Nor did the strength of horse-taming Hyperenor profit him,
when, delighting in his youth, he stood against me
and called me the weakest warrior among the Danaans.
He did not think that his own feet would carry him home
to gladden his beloved wife and dear parents.
So I shall also break your courage if you stand before me.
But I command you: go back into the crowd,
do not stand against me before you suffer evil;
the fool learns only after the deed is done.”

So he spoke, but the other was not persuaded; he answered:

“Now, Menelaus, son of Zeus, you shall pay in full
for the kinsman you have killed, and you boast as you speak.
You have made his wife a widow in the inner chamber,
and brought grievous lament and sorrow to his parents.
Would that I might become a comfort to those poor mourners,
if I could carry your head and armor and cast them
into the hands of Panthous and godlike Phrontis.
But the labor will not remain untested for long,
nor will courage or fear be without its trial.”

With that he struck Menelaus' all-sided shield;
the bronze did not pierce it, but the spear-point bent
within the strong shield. Then Menelaus, son of Atreus,
rose behind his bronze, praying to Father Zeus.
When the other drew back, he thrust beneath his belly,
and leaning upon the spear with all his heavy hand,
sent its point straight through the tender neck.
Euphorbus fell with a crash, and his armor rang upon him.
His hair, like the Graces', and the braided locks
bound with gold and silver, were drenched in blood.

As a man raises a flourishing young olive tree
in a solitary place where water is abundant,
beautiful and newly green, while every wind shakes it
with winds of every kind and it blooms in white flower—
then a sudden wind comes with a violent storm,
tears it from the trench, and stretches it upon the earth—
so Menelaus, son of Atreus, killed Euphorbus,
skilled with the spear, and stripped his armor.

As when a mountain-bred lion, trusting in his strength,
seizes from a grazing herd the finest bull;
first with strong teeth he breaks its neck,
then tears and swallows all the blood and inward organs,
while dogs and herdsmen cry out from far away
and will not come against him, for green fear seizes them—
so none of the Trojans had courage in his breast
to stand against glorious Menelaus.

Then Menelaus would easily have carried away
the famous armor of the son of Panthous,
if Phoebus Apollo had not been angry with him.
Apollo roused Hector, equal to swift Ares,
in the shape and voice of Menthes,
leader of the Cicones, and spoke to Hector:

“Hector, you pursue the horses of brave Aeacides,
but they are difficult for mortal men to master or drive;
no one but Achilles can control them,
whose immortal mother bore him.
Meanwhile Menelaus, warlike son of Atreus,
has straddled Patroclus and killed the best of the Trojans,
Euphorbus, son of Panthous, and ended his battle-strength.
Come now, drive against him; perhaps Apollo will grant you glory.”

So he spoke, and the god returned into the labor of men.
But dreadful grief enclosed Hector's dark heart.
He looked along the ranks, and at once he saw one man
carrying away the famous armor, and another lying on the earth;
blood flowed down the wound in his neck.

Hector went through the foremost fighters, clad in flashing bronze,
shouting sharply, like a fire of Hephaestus that cannot be quenched.
Nor did he escape the keen cry of the son of Atreus.
Menelaus, troubled, spoke to his great-hearted spirit:

“Ah me! If I abandon the beautiful armor and Patroclus,
who lies here because of my honor, will any Danaan
who sees me be angry? But if, alone, out of shame,
I fight Hector and the Trojans, lest the many surround me—
for Hector leads them all—what profit is there in these words?
Whenever a man desires to fight a godlike warrior
whom a god honors, great sorrow soon rolls upon him.
Therefore no Danaan who sees me go against Hector
will be angry with me, since Hector fights with divine aid.
If only I might hear the cry of noble Ajax;
then we two would return together and remember our courage,
even against a god, if somehow we might draw away the dead.”
