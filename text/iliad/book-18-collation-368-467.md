## Revised translation pass

So they spoke thus to one another. But silver-footed Thetis
came to the house of Hephaestus, imperishable and starry,
conspicuous among the immortals, a house of bronze
which the crooked-footed smith had made for himself.
She found him sweating as he turned among his bellows,
laboring urgently; for he was making twenty tripods
to stand around the wall of his well-founded hall.
Under the base of each he had set golden wheels,
that they might move of themselves into the divine assembly,
and again return to the house—a wonder to behold.
They lacked only their ornamented handles;
he was fitting those on and fastening the bonds,
working with all the skill of his knowing heart.

While he labored at these things, the goddess Thetis came near.
The lovely Charis, bright-veiled, saw her as she approached;
the famous smith had made her his wife.
She took Thetis’s hand, spoke to her, and called her by name:

“Why have you come to our house, Thetis, long-robed goddess,
honored and dear? You do not often visit us.
Come farther in, that I may set before you the gifts of friendship.”

So speaking, the shining goddess led her inward.
She seated Thetis upon a silver-studded, beautiful throne,
finely wrought, with a footstool beneath her feet;
then she called Hephaestus, famed for his craft, and spoke:

“Hephaestus, come here; Thetis has need of you.”

The famous smith with crippled feet answered her:

“Then surely a goddess both terrible and honored is within,
one who saved me when pain came upon me,
after I had fallen far away through the will of my shameless mother,
who wished to hide me because I was lame.
I should have suffered grievously then,
if Eurynome and Thetis had not received me into their arms—
Eurynome, daughter of Ocean who turns back upon himself.
With them I dwelt for nine years,
forging many ingenious works in their hollow cave,
curved clasps, spiral brooches, cups, and necklaces;
around us the immeasurable stream of Ocean
roared and foamed as it ran.
No other god or mortal man knew of me there,
but only Thetis and Eurynome, who saved me.
Since Thetis has now come to our house,
I owe the fair-haired goddess a full return for saving my life.
Set beautiful gifts of hospitality before her,
while I put away my bellows and all my tools.”

So he spoke. The huge, wondrous smith rose from the anvil,
limping; beneath him his thin legs moved with difficulty.
He set the bellows away from the fire,
and gathered every tool with which he worked into a silver chest.
With a sponge he wiped his face, both hands,
his strong neck, and his hairy breast;
he put on a tunic, took a thick staff,
and went limping out through the door.
Two golden maidens hurried beneath their lord,
like young women alive.
In their breasts they had understanding,
and voice, and strength; from the immortal gods
they had learned the works of the gods.
They moved beneath their master’s steps, and he came near
to where bright-throned Thetis sat.
He took her hand, spoke to her, and called her by name:

“Why have you come to our house, Thetis, long-robed goddess,
honored and dear? You do not often visit us.
Tell me what you have in mind; my heart commands me to fulfill it,
if I am able to fulfill it and if the thing is within completion.”

Thetis answered him, shedding tears:

“Hephaestus, is there among all the goddesses upon Olympus
one who has endured in her heart such grievous sorrows,
as many as Zeus, son of Cronus, has given me beyond the others?
Of all the sea-born sisters he gave me to a mortal man,
Aeacides Peleus, and I endured the marriage-bed
against my will, through great compulsion.
Now he lies in the house, broken by grievous old age.
And I have other sorrow besides:
when he gave me a son to bear and raise,
a hero beyond all others, who sprang up like a young tree,
I raised him like a plant in a garden’s young ground
and sent him upon the curved ships to Ilium
to fight the Trojans; but I shall never receive him again,
returning home into the house of Peleus.
While he lives and sees the light of the sun,
he suffers, and I cannot help him, though I go to him.

“The maiden whom the sons of the Achaeans gave him as his prize,
Agamemnon, lord of men, took back from his hands.
For her sake my son consumed his heart in grief;
and while the Trojans drove the Achaeans back to their sterns,
they did not let them come out beyond the ships.
The elders of Argos entreated him,
naming many splendid gifts; but he himself refused
to ward off the ruin.
Yet he clothed Patroclus in his own armor,
and sent him into the battle, giving him a great host as well.
All day they fought around the Scaean Gates,
and that day they would have taken the city,
had not Apollo, after doing much evil,
killed the brave son of Menoetius among the foremost
and given glory to Hector.

“Therefore I now come to your knees:
if you are willing, give my short-lived son
a shield, a helmet, beautiful greaves fitted at the ankles,
and a corselet. His own were lost with his faithful companion,
who was subdued by the Trojans; and my son now lies upon the earth,
his heart in grief.”

The famous smith with crippled feet answered her:

“Take courage; let not these things trouble your mind.
Would that I could hide him from death’s grievous noise,
when his terrible fate comes upon him,
as surely as beautiful armor shall be his,
such as another among the many generations of men
shall wonder at when he looks upon it.”
