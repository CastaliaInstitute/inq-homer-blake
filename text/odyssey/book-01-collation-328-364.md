## Revised translation pass

From her upper chamber the thoughtful daughter of Icarius,
Penelope, heard the inspired song. She descended
The high stairway of her house, not alone;
Two serving-women went beside her.
When the divine woman reached the suitors, she stood
Beside the roof’s strong central pillar, holding before
Her shining cheeks the veil drawn close about her face;
Her faithful attendant stood on either side.

She wept, and spoke to the godlike singer:
“Phemius, thou knowest many songs to charm the hearts of men,
Deeds of gods and men, such as singers make famous.
Sing one of these while they sit silent drinking their wine;
But cease from this grievous song, which always wears my heart
Within my breast, since an unforgotten sorrow has fallen upon me.
For I long with ceaseless remembrance for such a head—
The head of a man whose fame is broad through Hellas
And the midst of Argos.”

Prudent Telemachus answered her:
“Mother, why begrudge the faithful singer the pleasure
His mind directs? Singers are not to blame;
Zeus himself is to blame, who gives to men who labor
As he wills, to each his portion. There is no reproach
In Phemius’ singing the evil fate of the Danaans;
Men praise most highly that song which comes newest
To those who hear it. Let thy heart and spirit endure
To listen. Odysseus was not the only man to lose
The day of his return in Troy; many other men perished.

“Go into the house and attend to thine own work,
The loom and the distaff, and command thy serving-women
To perform their tasks. Speech shall concern all men,
But me above all; for in this house the power is mine.”

She, astonished, went back into the house;
For she laid the prudent words of her son within her heart.
She climbed again to the upper chamber with her women,
And there wept for her dear husband Odysseus,
Until gray-eyed Athena cast sweet sleep upon her eyelids.

## Decision log

- Preserve Penelope’s descent from the upper chamber with two attendants and
  her veiled stance at the pillar; her grief enters the public feast embodied.
- Keep Phemius’ repertoire as songs of gods and men, while marking this return
  song as grievous because it is the newest and most painful to Penelope.
- Retain Telemachus’ claim that singers are not at fault and that Zeus distributes
  each man’s portion, without making the speech an excuse for the suitors.
- Preserve the distinction between Odysseus’ lost return and the many Danaans
  who also perished at Troy; grief is personal, but the song is collective.
- Keep Telemachus’ command in the vocabulary of household work—loom, distaff,
  and serving-women—before his assertion that speech belongs to men and power
  in the house is his.
- End with Penelope’s astonishment, her inward keeping of the words, and
  Athena’s sleep; Telemachus’ public speech begins in the next unit.
