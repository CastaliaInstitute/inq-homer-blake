## Translation

The hall was silent when Odysseus ended his story.
Alcinous rose and bade the Phaeacians complete
Their gifts: a tripod and a cauldron, bronze
To answer bronze, and treasure fit for home.
At dawn they bore the offerings to the ship;
The heralds stowed them beneath the rowing-benches.

Then came the feast, and Demodocus sang.
But Odysseus, turning toward the sinking sun,
Desired the darkness, as the farmer longs
For supper when the oxen leave the field.
He asked to go. Alcinous poured the wine,
Gave him farewell, and pledged that he would find
His wife and friends unharmed. Bread, meat, and wine
Were carried down; a bed was spread astern.
The oars fell, and the ship went out like a chariot
Upon a plain. Through all the night he slept.

At dawn they reached the harbor of Phorcys,
Where two headlands shelter the quiet water.
An olive tree stood near the harbor's head,
And close beside it opened the holy cave
Of Naiad nymphs, with jars and mixing-bowls,
And purple looms, and two unequal doors:
The northern open to mankind; the southern,
Sacred, forbidden. They lifted sleeping him
From the ship, and laid him down upon the sand,
With all his gifts. Beneath the olive's roots
They hid the treasure, lest a passerby should steal it.

Poseidon then complained to Zeus: “Shall men
No longer fear the sea, if Phaeacian ships
Carry them all unharmed? Odysseus now
Has reached his home.” Zeus permitted punishment.
The Earth-shaker struck the returning ship;
It rooted in the sea and hardened into stone.
Alcinous, remembering an ancient word,
Sacrificed twelve bulls, and ordered that no more
Phaeacian ships should guide men over the deep.

Odysseus woke, but Athena's mist lay round him;
His own land seemed strange. He counted all his gifts,
Then mourned beside the water, fearing betrayal.
A young shepherd came, with folded cloak and spear.
Odysseus asked what country held him. The stranger said:
“You stand in Ithaca, rough, but rich in wine,
In rain and grain, in goats and cattle, springs
And woods; its fame has traveled even to Troy.”

Odysseus answered with a Cretan tale:
He had slain Orsilochus, escaped the island,
And Phoenicians had put him ashore while sleeping,
Leaving his foreign spoils beside him. Athena smiled.
She changed into her own divine form and said:
“No man is wiser in danger, nor more subtle.
I am Athena, who have stood beside you always.
You must endure, and tell your name to none.”

She lifted the mist. He knew Phorcys, the olive,
The cave, and Neriton. Together they bore
The gold, bronze, and garments into the hollow;
Athena rolled a stone before the door.
Then she told him of the suitors who consumed
His house, and of Penelope's long delay:
“Think how they shall meet their death.” He answered:
“Stand with me, as at Troy.” She promised aid,
But first touched him with the wand of transformation.

His skin grew old, his hair fell out, his eyes
Were ringed with age; his tunic became foul rags,
And round him hung a deer's hide. He took a staff,
A patched and filthy bag. “Go first,” she said,
“To Eumaeus, your faithful swineherd, who keeps
The pigs by Raven Rock and Arethusa's spring.
Ask him the state of your house. I will go on
To Lacedaemon, and bring home your son.
Telemachus sits safe with Menelaus, though young men
Wait by the sea-road, planning his death.”
She touched him once again; the change was complete.
Then Athena crossed the island, toward Lacedaemon,
To summon the son of long-enduring Odysseus.
